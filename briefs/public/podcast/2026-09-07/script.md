Good evening. This is India Markets Brief from toro I-Q. Your read on Monday's session.

The biggest thing that changed since I last spoke to you is the price of oil. Brent crude is reported to have settled near ninety seven dollars a barrel on Monday, about nine percent above where it sat on the twenty eighth of August. That followed the heaviest strikes on oil tankers yet in the American war with Iran, over a weekend when India was shut. India buys nearly all the oil it burns and has no say in that price. That ninety seven dollars comes from press reports, not data I could confirm, so treat it as reported.

But oil is not what moved share prices here. America's jobs number landed on Friday evening, after we had closed. American employers added about a hundred and sixty two thousand jobs against expectations near fifty three thousand, which pushed up the odds of an American rate rise this month. Indian software shares fell on Monday. Every outlet linked those two, and I want to be straight with you: that link is an interpretation, not a confirmed fact.

The Nifty fifty closed at twenty three thousand seven hundred and seventy nine, down half a percent. The index of Indian software shares fell two point three percent, a fourth losing session in a row, and every company in it was lower. Outside software there was no single story, but the weakness was wide, and I will come back to that.

America was shut on Monday for Labor Day, so New York never opened. If you see Monday's Nifty compared with American markets this week, that is Monday's India against Friday's America. Asia did trade alongside us, and went the other way, hard. Korea's main index rose four point six percent, led by Samsung and S-K hynix, the two Korean chipmakers that supply artificial intelligence servers. Japan's Nikkei rose about two percent. Same jobs number, opposite directions. A hotter American economy does not hurt demand for those chips. But Indian software sells project work to American companies, whose budgets get cut first when borrowing looks dearer.

Now the movers. Screening the day's biggest gives eleven names: ten that rose three percent or more, and one that fell sharply. Just one of the eleven has a company announcement behind it. That one was Tribhovandas Bhimji Zaveri, a hundred and sixty year old Mumbai jeweller, up ten percent, and still climbing on a deal filed back on the thirty first of August. G-R-T Jewellers has agreed to buy just over seventy four percent from the founding family, and must now offer other shareholders about two hundred and fifty rupees a share. The share closed Monday near five hundred and twenty nine, so the market is not pricing the offer, it is pricing the business under a new owner.

The one that fell was Ram Ratna Wires, which makes copper winding wire, down about six percent. Its founding family owns sixty nine percent and has pledged none of it against a loan, so no lender was selling stock to cover a debt. Nothing on the record explains the fall.

Here is what struck me most, and I have no answer for it. Four hundred and fifty three of seven hundred and ten shares fell, and the big money was not selling. Foreign investors are reported to have bought a net two hundred and eighty crore rupees, and Indian institutions about five hundred and sixty seven crore. No source explains how a broad decline happened with nobody large selling into it. The tempting answer is smaller traders cutting risk while institutions sat still, but that is a guess, and I would rather say I do not know.

Three things also did not move when they should have. IndiGo, the airline, rose slightly on a day when war fear pushed oil prices up. Fuel is one of an airline's biggest costs, so that is the wrong direction. The gold lenders, the companies that lend against your jewellery, fell while gold itself rose one percent. Their loans are backed by that gold, so a higher gold price should have helped them. And the rupee took an oil shock and higher American rate odds on the same day, and finished unchanged. A weak dollar everywhere explains some of that, but only part.

What to watch. The American rate decision lands on the fifteenth and sixteenth of September. The odds of a rise sit between about fifty two and sixty five percent depending on which market you look at, so I will give you the range, not one number. Before that, India's August inflation figure arrives mid month. It measures August prices, so none of this oil move is in it. The September figure, out in mid October, is the first that can carry it.

That's your brief. Before I sign off: this has been general market commentary, not investment advice. For investment advice tailored to your situation, consult a SEBI-registered Investment Adviser. Markets are risky; you may lose money; act with care. See you tomorrow.
