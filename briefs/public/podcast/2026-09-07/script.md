Good evening. This is India Markets Brief from toro I-Q. Your read on Monday's session.

It has been ten days since I last spoke to you, so let me start with the thing that changed most in that gap. The price of oil. Brent crude is reported to have settled near ninety seven dollars a barrel on Monday, about nine percent above where it sat on the twenty eighth of August. Behind that was the heaviest exchange of strikes on oil tankers yet in the war between the United States and Iran, and it ran across a weekend when India was shut. India imports almost all the oil it burns and has no say in that price. Our own data file could not give me a clean oil number on Monday, so that ninety seven dollar figure is press reported rather than confirmed, and I am telling you that rather than dressing it up.

But oil is not what moved Indian share prices on Monday. That was a different foreign event. America's jobs number landed on Friday evening India time, after we had already closed. Payrolls rose about a hundred and sixty two thousand against expectations near fifty three thousand, and that pushed up the odds that the American central bank raises interest rates this month. Indian software shares then fell on Monday. The link between those two is the read every news carrier ran on the day. It is a read, not a confirmed fact. The fall itself is a fact, and here it is.


The Nifty fifty closed at twenty three thousand seven hundred and seventy nine, down half a percent, and the Sensex fell by the same. The index of Indian software shares fell two point three percent, a fourth losing session in a row, with every company in it lower. That is more than four times the fall in the main index. Outside software the day was almost featureless. Small caps finished flat, and the typical share fell about as much as the index did. So this was shallow and wide, not sharp. One honest gap. Fourteen of our sixteen sector readings failed to fetch on Monday, so software and pharmaceuticals, which rose three quarters of a percent, are the only two sectors I will put a number on tonight. Where you hear a company name later, it is a company, not a sector.


Now two things about the world India traded into, and the first one almost nobody will tell you. America was shut on Monday. It was Labor Day, so New York never opened. Every comparison you read this week between Monday's Nifty and American markets is really comparing Monday's India with Friday's America. Asia did trade alongside us, and Asia went the other way, hard. Korea's main index rose four point six percent, with Samsung up nearly six percent and S-K hynix up more than eight. Japan's Nikkei rose about two percent. Same jobs number, opposite directions, and the reason is what each side sells. North Asia sells the physical equipment that artificial intelligence buildouts run on, and a hotter American economy does not hurt that order book. Indian software sells project work to American corporate clients, and those budgets are the first thing squeezed when borrowing costs look like rising.


Two pieces of India news landed while this channel was quiet, and both are good with a catch. Growth in the June quarter came in at seven point eight percent, at the top of the forecast range, with manufacturing up nine point two percent. And foreign exchange reserves hit a record, above seven hundred and forty billion dollars. But roughly a hundred and thirty six billion of that came in through a special swap window, which means those dollars have to be handed back. The record and the borrowing are the same fact.


Now the movers, and I want to be honest about the shape of this list. Ten shares rose three percent or more on Monday, there was exactly one genuine faller, and only one name in the whole list has documents behind it.

That one is Tribhovandas Bhimji Zaveri, a hundred and sixty year old Mumbai jeweller, up ten percent. G-R-T Jewellers has agreed to buy just over seventy four percent of the company from the founding family, and has made the offer to remaining shareholders that the rules require, at about two hundred and fifty rupees a share. The share closed Monday at about five hundred and twenty nine rupees. So the market is not pricing that offer. It is pricing what the business is worth under a new owner, and the gap between those two numbers is the lesson.

The biggest move of the day was Syrma S-G-S Technology, an electronics manufacturer, up twelve percent, and nobody can tell you why. Two of our desks ran the checklist separately and both came back empty. No filing, no large block trade, no dated broker note, no index event. The sector explanation does not survive either, because that group split down the middle on Monday, with some names up seven percent while Dixon and Kaynes finished flat to slightly lower.

The single genuine faller was Ram Ratna Wires, which makes copper winding wire, down about six percent. We checked the obvious worry first, which is whether the founding family had been forced to sell shares they had borrowed against. They had not. They own sixty nine percent of the company with none of it pledged, and they have disclosed as much. Nothing on the public record explains Monday.

One more, in case your screen looked alarming. H-E-G showed a drop of more than sixty percent. Nothing was lost there. Monday was the record date for the company's demerger, so the price base was reset, and shareholders now hold a share in a second company as well.


Here is what struck me most about Monday, and I do not have an answer for it. Four hundred and fifty three of seven hundred and ten shares fell. And neither of the two big institutional blocs was selling. Foreign investors bought a net two hundred and eighty crore rupees, and Indian institutions bought five hundred and sixty seven crore. A broad decline happened with nobody large handing stock out, and no source I could find explains it. The tempting answer is that smaller traders and dealing desks reduced risk on foreign news while the institutions sat still. That is a guess, and I would rather say I do not know.

Three other things did not move when they should have. IndiGo closed slightly higher on a day oil carried a war premium, which is the wrong direction for an airline's fuel bill. The gold lenders fell while gold itself rose one percent, with Muthoot Finance down about one percent and Manappuram down four. And the rupee took a crude shock and a hawkish repricing of American rates on the same day and finished unchanged at ninety four rupees and forty nine paise. Part of that is a soft dollar, which eased slightly. That is a partial account, not a complete one.


Now, what to watch. The American interest rate decision lands on the fifteenth and sixteenth of September, announced around half past eleven at night India time. The odds of a rise sit somewhere between fifty two and sixty five percent depending on which venue you read, so I will keep giving you the range instead of pretending it is one number. Before that, India's August inflation figure arrives in the middle of the month, and it is the first one that can carry oil moving from eighty nine dollars to ninety seven.
