Good evening. This is India Markets Brief from toro I-Q. Your read on today's session.

Today the Nifty closed at its lowest level in five months, and three things did it, all landing over the holiday weekend. Oil went up. American interest rates went up. And the Reserve Bank of India is about to pull one trillion rupees of cash out of the banking system.

Here is where the market closed. The Nifty fifty finished at twenty-three thousand one hundred and eighteen point six, down one point one nine percent. It opened higher and fell all day, pricing two days of bad news in one session. The Bank Nifty fell one point four three percent. Smaller companies fell twice as hard, the midcap index down two point zero eight percent and the smallcap index down two point four four percent. One sector rose. Indian software shares closed up two point one nine percent, after being up more than five percent in the morning. The reported reason was a weekend call by the head of the AI company Anthropic for a slower pace of AI development, read here as giving Indian services firms more time to adapt.

The rest of Asia was mixed rather than crushed, and India fell more than all of it, because the reasons were India's own: a weaker rupee, a higher bond yield and a foreign seller.

Now the two things hanging over the market. On the worrying side, oil and American rates arrived together. Saudi Arabia's main export pipeline, the route around the closed Strait of Hormuz, has been shut since a drone strike last Thursday, with no restart date. Brent crude traded above one hundred and seven dollars a barrel while India was open, a four-month high, and the rupee fell to a seven-week low, ninety-five point nine four five to the dollar. Then rates. The ten-year American bond yield touched five percent on Monday, and a Fed rate rise tomorrow is priced at about ninety-two percent. A five percent yield on a safe dollar asset pulls foreign money out of markets like India, and today foreign investors sold a net two thousand nine hundred and seventy-seven point nine crore rupees, a fifth straight day of selling. On the supportive side, August inflation came in at four point eight two percent, exactly what forecasters expected, so it did not move the market. And domestic institutions bought two thousand six hundred and eighty-six crore rupees of shares, absorbing about ninety percent of the foreign selling. That is why a five-month low still felt orderly rather than panicked.

Now the movers. The biggest fall among large companies was Solar Industries, the explosives maker, down thirteen point six four percent. Over the holiday it agreed to buy the whole of Omnia, a South African mining-explosives and farm-chemicals business, for about twelve thousand nine hundred and fifty-one crore rupees in cash. The filing does not say how that cash will be raised or what the combined company's debt will look like, and that open question is what got sold. Solar Industries is twelve percent of the defence index, which is a good part of why defence shares fell almost six percent today.

The biggest gain was Tata Chemicals, up nineteen point nine nine percent and locked at its upper price limit. The Reserve Bank of India has told Tata Sons, the group's holding company, that it cannot give up its registration as an investment company and should move towards a stock-market listing. Tata Chemicals owns two point five three percent of Tata Sons, a stake that at the private valuations quoted in the press would be worth more than Tata Chemicals itself. No listing has been announced and there is no date.

The third is Welspun Corp, the pipe maker, down nine point eight four percent, with nothing in its filings to explain it. What it has is a run-up, nearly seventy-four percent in three months and two hundred and four percent in a year. My reading, and it is only a reading, is that a stock like that is full of holders who leave first on a bad day, and several of today's other big falls fit the same pattern.

Here is what I think is going on underneath all of this. Over the summer Indian banks raised about a hundred and thirty-six billion dollars, mostly deposits from Indians abroad, and swapped those dollars with the Reserve Bank of India for rupees. The dollars became a record jump in the country's reserves. The rupees became a cash surplus of about ten and a half trillion rupees in the banking system. The Reserve Bank's answer is to sell one trillion rupees of government bonds, starting Thursday, and take those rupees back. More bonds for sale means a higher bond yield, reported today at about seven point one percent, a four-month high. That, in my reading, is what sold property shares, down just over four percent, and lenders like Shriram Finance, down four point seven four percent. It is not a rate rise, the policy rate is unchanged. But for any business that is really a bet on the cost of money, it felt like one today.

What to watch. Tomorrow night at about half past eleven, Indian Standard Time, the Fed announces its rate decision. That lands after our close, so Thursday is the first session here that can trade it. Thursday morning also brings the first fifty thousand crore rupee bond sale, and the number to watch is the yield the Reserve Bank has to offer to get it sold.
