Good evening. This is India Markets Brief from toro I-Q. Your read on today's session.

Today the Nifty closed at its lowest level in five months, after a long weekend in which three things turned against India at once. Oil went up. American interest rates went up. And the Reserve Bank of India is about to drain one trillion rupees of cash from the banking system.

The Nifty fifty finished at twenty-three thousand one hundred and eighteen point six, down one point one nine percent. The Bank Nifty fell one point four three percent, and smaller companies fell twice as hard as the index, the smallcap index down two point four four percent. The stocks that had run hardest through the summer fell hardest. One sector rose. Indian software shares closed up two point one nine percent, after being up more than five percent in the morning. The reported reason was a weekend call by the head of the AI company Anthropic for a slower pace of AI development, read as giving Indian services firms more time to adapt.

The rest of Asia was mixed rather than crushed, and India fell more than all of it, because the reasons were India's own: a weaker rupee, a higher bond yield and a foreign seller.

On the worrying side, oil and American rates arrived together. Saudi Arabia's main export pipeline, the route around the closed Strait of Hormuz, has been shut since a drone strike last Thursday. Brent crude traded above one hundred and seven dollars a barrel while India was open, and the rupee fell to a seven-week low, ninety-five point nine four five to the dollar. The ten-year American bond yield touched five percent on Monday, and a Fed rate rise tomorrow is priced at about ninety-two percent. Five percent on a safe dollar asset pulls foreign money out of markets like India. Today foreign investors sold a net two thousand nine hundred and seventy-seven point nine crore rupees, a fifth straight day of selling. On the supportive side, domestic institutions bought two thousand six hundred and eighty-six crore rupees of shares, absorbing about ninety percent of that, which is why the fall was orderly rather than a panic.

Now the movers. The biggest fall among large companies was Solar Industries, the explosives maker, down thirteen point six four percent. Over the holiday it agreed to buy Omnia, a South African explosives and farm-chemicals business, for about twelve thousand nine hundred and fifty-one crore rupees in cash. The filing does not say how that cash will be raised, and that open question is what got sold. Solar Industries is twelve percent of the defence index, which is part of why defence shares fell almost six percent today.

The biggest gain was Tata Chemicals, up nineteen point nine nine percent and locked at its upper price limit. The Reserve Bank of India is reported to have told Tata Sons, the group's holding company, that it cannot give up its registration and should move towards a stock-market listing. Tata Chemicals owns two point five three percent of Tata Sons, and at the valuations quoted in the press that stake alone would be worth more than Tata Chemicals itself. No listing has been announced and there is no date.

Here is what I think is going on underneath. Over the summer Indian banks took in a flood of dollar deposits from Indians abroad and swapped them with the Reserve Bank of India for rupees. Those rupees are now a cash surplus of about ten and a half trillion rupees in the banking system. The Reserve Bank's answer is to sell one trillion rupees of government bonds, starting Thursday, and take that cash back. More bonds for sale means a higher bond yield, reported today at about seven point one percent, a four-month high. That, in my reading, is what sold property shares, down just over four percent, and lenders like Shriram Finance, down four point seven four percent. It is not a rate rise, the policy rate is unchanged, but for a business that is really a bet on the cost of money, it felt like one.

What to watch. Tomorrow night at about half past eleven, Indian Standard Time, the Fed announces its rate decision. It lands after our close, so Thursday is the first session here that can trade it. Thursday morning also brings the first fifty thousand crore rupee bond sale, and the number to watch is the yield the Reserve Bank has to offer to get it sold.
