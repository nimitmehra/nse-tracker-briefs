## The three orchestrator questions

### 1. The forward-look points at Monday 31 August and skips Friday 28 August. Does the listener get a usable forward-look?

**No. The script silently deletes the day the listener is standing in, and it does so on a rationale that does not survive its own evidence.**

The writer's stated reason (metadata line 12) is that "the Friday items are same-day or already past by any plausible publication time, and a stale forward-look is worse than a later one." Two problems with that.

First, it is factually wrong for at least half the dropped list. The script's own deliberate-cuts note names four Friday items: Jackson Hole, the Reserve Bank's weekly statistics, the bond sale, and the non-banking-finance comment deadline. An episode finalised on Friday morning India time reaches a listener *ahead of* the Jackson Hole address, which lands Friday evening or night India time, and ahead of the Reserve Bank's Friday-evening release. Those are not stale. They are the listener's next several hours. Calling them "already past by any plausible publication time" assumes a publication time later than the one this episode actually has.

Second, and worse than the omission: the script does not merely leave Friday out, it affirmatively closes the door on it. "The date to keep is Monday the thirty first" tells a listener, on Friday morning, that the next thing worth their attention is three days away. That is not a gap the listener can fill; it is a claim they will act on. And the episode then ends on "See you tomorrow" — an internal contradiction the listener can hear: if tomorrow is worth an episode, why is there nothing in today?

This is not a strict L3 hit under the skill's letter — nothing is mislabelled, no past event is called future or vice versa. It is an omission plus a misdirection, and the skill's L3 was written before day-name deviations existed. But it is the kind of defect L3 exists to catch, and the fix is one sentence. **Add it. My independent judgment is that the writer's override was the right instinct applied to the wrong day: pointing at Monday is correct and valuable, but it should be the second half of the forward-look, not the whole of it.**

Suggested insert before the Monday line: *"Before that, two things land on Friday. The head of the American central bank speaks at Jackson Hole in the evening, India time, and the Reserve Bank publishes its weekly figures. Neither usually moves the market on its own, but Jackson Hole sets the tone for the following week."*

### 2. Is "Four stale stories were caught and thrown out along the way, each of which would have read as Thursday's news unchecked" earning its place?

**No. It is the desk talking about itself, and it should be cut.**

I want to separate it from the sentence immediately before it, because the two are not the same thing and only one of them fails.

"Nine of the other ten risers have no cause we could establish" **earns its place.** It is a fact about the market that changes what a listener does: nine shares went up and nobody can tell you why, so do not construct a reason. The uncertainty is the content.

"Four stale stories were caught and thrown out along the way" **does not.** The stories were discarded. They do not exist for the listener. They cannot act on them, cannot look them up, cannot be more careful because of them. What the sentence actually conveys is *we were diligent* — and the clause "each of which would have read as Thursday's news unchecked" makes that explicit, since its only function is to say how bad the error would have been if the desk had not caught it. That is a claim about the desk's quality wearing the clothes of a market fact.

It is also the third of three such lines in the episode, after "our screen's own rule and not the market" and "this next part is our reasoning and not a sourced finding". Individually each is a small breach; three in one seven-hundred-word script is a voice problem, not a slip. The exemplar's rule six is blunt: the listener gets the story, not the analyst's view of the story. **Cut it, keep sentence 35.**

### 3. Does "inverted" land for a naive listener with no visual?

**No — and the reason is not the word itself, it is where the word appears.**

"Inverted" is a spatial metaphor with nothing to be spatial about in audio. There is no chart, no line, no axis. The listener has to build the picture and then flip it, in the half-second before the next sentence starts.

But the sharper problem is that the script *already said it in plain English one sentence earlier*: "The same pattern ran backwards on Monday." That is the definition, it is correct, it needs no visual, and it works. The payoff sentence then abandons it for a harder synonym the listener has never heard in this episode. The script climbs down from clear language to jargon at precisely the moment the idea has to stick.

The fix is not a beat of support, it is a word swap: *"It ran backwards twice in three sessions. That is worth noticing, not worth calling a pattern."* That keeps the vocabulary the listener was just taught, and it drops "something to describe, not a trend", which is itself a small piece of the desk narrating its own confidence level.
