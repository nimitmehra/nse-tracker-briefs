Good evening. This is India Markets Brief from toro I-Q. Your read on today's session.

Two government notifications organised today's session, and neither of them was a market event. The electronics ministry cleared its first batch of component projects, nearly seven thousand nine hundred crore rupees across thirty one projects, on letters dated Monday. The defence ministry published a sixth list of four hundred and five items that must now be bought from Indian suppliers. Both sets of shares rose while the market fell.

Here is where the market closed. The Nifty fifty finished at twenty four thousand one hundred and fifty four, down zero point five five percent, a sixth straight down session. The Sensex fell zero point six three percent. But the index fell and the market did not. Inside the Nifty fifty itself, eight shares rose and thirty three fell. The smallcap index was the only green headline index, up zero point one nine percent. And six of the eleven biggest movers we track finished the day at their highest price in a year. That is a market pulling apart, not a market selling off.

One sector did almost all of the damage. The technology index fell one point nine three percent, three and a half times the benchmark, and every one of its eleven members fell. Wipro and Infosys were two of the five biggest losers on the Nifty.

Asia split in two today. Japan and Korea were sold, China was bought, and emerging market funds barely moved. Nothing outside India explains an Indian fall of half a percent. Today was made at home.

The worrying part is oil, and it is not about today. The Strait of Hormuz has gone the wrong way since our last brief. The Abu Dhabi state oil company reported a third attack on one of its ships inside forty eight hours, and weekend traffic through the strait nearly stopped. Brent is now about nine percent higher than it was on the seventh of August. That matters, because the Reserve Bank of India cut its inflation forecast this month and said plainly that it did so because crude was cheap. The forecast is not wrong yet. The reason behind it has reversed inside a fortnight.

Here is a second one that gets missed. Prices at the factory gate are rising at nearly twice the pace of prices in the shops. What factories charged rose eight point two nine percent in July. What households paid in the shops rose four point four five percent. That gap, close to four percentage points, gets absorbed by whoever sits in between and cannot raise prices. Packaged goods, chemicals, cement, building products. That is a margin story, not an inflation story.

Now the movers. The biggest gain was Paras Defence and Space Technologies, up ten percent, locked at its price limit, at a one year high. The indigenisation list was announced at twelve nineteen and a report naming Paras followed at half past two, both during trading hours. That is the cleanest explanation on the board today. But hold the size in your head. The whole list carries an estimated three thousand and seventy crore rupees of business potential, spread over years and across the entire defence supply chain. Paras on its own is worth roughly four times that, and nothing has been ordered yet.

Jyoti C N C Automation rose eight point three percent on the other notification, also to a one year high. It is putting more than a thousand crore rupees into its Rajkot plant over five years, and it told the exchange so at ten twenty two on Monday night. An overnight disclosure is exactly what the next session is entitled to price.

The one real fall was Ahluwalia Contracts, a construction company, down ten point eight five percent. June quarter sales rose twelve percent and net profit fell almost eighty percent. Revenue up with profit down like that is not a demand problem. It is a cost problem, in a business where fixed price contracts and input costs decide the margin. The results were filed after the close on Friday, the recording of the management call went up after the close on Monday, and the market gave its verdict today. The call made it worse, not better.

The same sequence ran the other way at Tube Investments of India, up seven point nine four percent. June quarter revenue rose seventeen percent to six thousand two hundred and fifteen crore rupees, and the growth was double digit in every segment rather than one lucky division. Results, then the call on Monday morning, then the verdict today. Opposite ending.

One more, because the timing is the story. eMudhra rose eight point three five percent, the strongest share in a falling technology sector. It told the exchange that a criminal complaint against it had been closed. That filing was published at seventeen minutes past four in the afternoon. The good news arrived an hour after the people who bought it had gone home.

Here is what struck me most about today, and it is the most useful thing I can tell you. The papers explained the fall with high crude and rising American bond yields. Brent moved by one hundredth of one percent today. It did not move. Crude sits at a high level, and long American yields really are near their highest since two thousand and seven, but a level is not an event. Neither of them did anything on Tuesday. And now my own limit. I could not find a dated reason for the technology fall either, and I am not going to invent one. I can show you what the press got wrong. I cannot tell you the cause.

Tomorrow night, around half past eleven our time, the United States Federal Reserve publishes the minutes of its July meeting. Three of its regional presidents voted for a rate increase, the first time three have dissented the same way since two thousand and sixteen. Those minutes land after our close, so India trades them on Thursday. And one date to put further out. The Reserve Bank's concessional window for non-resident deposits, which is reportedly what has been holding the rupee up this year, closes on the thirtieth of September.
