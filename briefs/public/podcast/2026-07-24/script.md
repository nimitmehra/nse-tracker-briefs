Good evening. This is India Markets Brief from toroIQ. Your read on today's session.

Indian shares fell for a fifth straight day today, the worst losing streak since January, and the pressure was largely homegrown. A ten percent American tariff on Indian exports reached its legal expiry this morning and lapsed. But it brought no relief at all, because Washington is reported to be moving the same duty onto a new, open-ended legal footing, and the bigger tariff deadline still lands on the first of August with the India-United States trade deal unsigned. The fall stayed shallow only because domestic funds kept buying.

Here is where the market closed today. The Nifty fifty finished at twenty-three thousand seven hundred and sixty-seven, down zero point four three percent, with the Sensex down the same. But the Bank Nifty actually closed up a fraction, and the technology index was the one green sector, up about eight-tenths of a percent. Auto, metal and energy shares led the losses. The fear gauge, India VIX, rose about four percent to fourteen, a jump into the weekend, even though the level is still low.

India fell, but it fell far less than its neighbours. The emerging-market basket dropped almost two percent, and Taiwan fell over two and a half percent on a global chip sell-off, while India lost less than half a percent. That gap is a flows story, not a good-news story. Domestic buying cushioned the foreign selling; nothing actually turned positive.

Now the two forces that framed the day. On the worrying side was oil. Brent crude breached one hundred dollars a barrel on Thursday, on fresh supply scares in the Red Sea and near the Strait of Hormuz, and stayed near ninety-eight through today's session. For a country that imports about eighty-five percent of its oil, that is the pressure the market kept selling. Crude did ease almost three percent, but only after the Indian close, so it is a possible relief for Monday, not a help to today. On the supportive side was domestic money. Local funds bought about five thousand four hundred crore rupees of shares against under four thousand crore of foreign selling, and the Reserve Bank's reserves actually rose, so it was not spending dollars to defend the rupee.

Now the movers. The biggest clean gainer was PVR INOX [SAY: P-V-R eye-nocks], the cinema operator, up about five and a half percent. It swung to a fifty-six crore rupee profit from a loss a year ago, with revenue up almost twelve percent, and turned cash-positive on strong footfalls. That is a genuine turnaround, not a momentum pop.

The biggest fall was Spandana Sphoorty [SAY: span-DAA-na s-FOOR-tee], a microfinance lender, down about ten and a half percent. It scraped back to a wafer-thin twelve crore rupee profit, and the market read that as an unconvincing recovery rather than a real one.

And a momentum name gave back. Syrma SGS [SAY: SUR-ma S-G-S], an electronics manufacturer up a third over three months, fell almost seven percent. Its board meets next week to consider raising fresh capital through a share sale, and on a stock trading near ninety times earnings, that dilution worry was enough to sell.

Here is what struck me most about today. It was a heavy results day, and the lesson was that a good past quarter is not enough if the future looks softer. Acutaas Chemicals [SAY: ah-KOO-taas] reported revenue up fifty-nine percent and still fell almost six percent, because management held its growth guidance flat and the stock was already expensive. Motilal Oswal's [SAY: MOH-tee-laal os-WAAL] profit rose ten percent, but it came from treasury gains, not its core business, and it fell seven percent. In a nervous market, investors are pricing the next two quarters, not the last one.

Now, what to watch. The single biggest event is the first of August, the United States reciprocal-tariff deadline. The trade deal is stuck, unsigned, over farm and dairy tariffs, so treat it as reported, not confirmed. And on Monday, watch whether that late easing in oil gives this five-day slide a breather.
