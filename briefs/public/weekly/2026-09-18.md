## 1. The week in plain English

The Nifty 50 finished the week at 23,346.40, about 0.22% below the previous Friday's close of roughly 23,397. The Sensex finished at 74,294.96, down 0.65%, which the press counted as its sixth losing week in a row, the longest run since 2020. That is the fifth consecutive losing week for the Nifty on our own record. The whole of the week's damage was done on one day. Tuesday fell 1.19% to a five-month closing low, and the three sessions after it clawed almost all of that back without quite finishing the job.

This was a four-session week, and we saw three of the four sessions with our own eyes. Monday 14 September was Ganesh Chaturthi and the exchange was closed. Tuesday and Wednesday we observed. Thursday 17 September was a real trading session, and the busiest day of the week, but our daily process did not run that day; everything we know about Thursday comes from Friday's 48-hour account, and Thursday's index closes below are worked backwards from Friday's numbers rather than observed. Friday we observed. Please keep that in mind throughout: where this brief says "Thursday", it is reporting the day second-hand.

Four things mattered, and each can be said in one sentence.

**Three costs turned against India at once over the long weekend, and Tuesday paid for all of them in a single session.** Oil rose after a drone strike shut Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, the route that carries its crude to the Red Sea and around the closed Strait of Hormuz, and Brent, the international oil price, settled at $108.75 on Tuesday, roughly a fifth higher than at the end of August. The interest rate on ten-year American government bonds touched 5% for the first time since 2007, and a 5% return with no risk attached is the number that makes a foreign investor ask why they hold Indian shares. And the Reserve Bank of India had announced on Friday 11 September that it would sell ₹1 trillion of government bonds to drain surplus cash out of the banking system, which pushed India's own ten-year bond yield to a four-month high of about 7.1%. Tuesday fell 1.19%, and 629 of the 712 shares we track fell against 83 that rose.

**The three events the market feared all happened on Wednesday night and Thursday, and none of them produced an accident.** The US Federal Reserve raised its policy rate by a quarter of a percentage point to 3.75 to 4.00%, its first rise since 2023, by a unanimous vote. The Bank of Japan raised its rate to 1.25%, the highest since 1995. And the Reserve Bank sold the first ₹50,000 crore of its bonds on Thursday to ₹66,590 crore of bids, without the jump in yields that Tuesday's selling had braced for. Thursday's close was described everywhere as little changed, and by Friday India VIX, the market's own measure of how nervous it is, had fallen to 11.39 from Tuesday's 13.43.

**On Thursday evening the board of Tata Sons and the trusts that own two thirds of it fell out in public, and on Friday the Tata group's shares paid for it.** The board re-appointed N. Chandrasekaran as executive chairman for five years by a four-to-one vote, and Tata Trusts called the resolution a legal nullity the same evening. Tata Consultancy Services fell 3.88% on Friday. TCS is heavy enough in the Sensex that its fall alone is the reason the Sensex closed 0.03% lower on a day the Nifty rose 0.33%.

**Underneath the index, small companies had their best day of the week on Friday, and the mechanism was an index provider rather than any news.** A global index publisher, FTSE, put its September membership changes into effect at Friday's close, and the funds that copy that index had to buy the companies being added or upweighted, at the closing price, that afternoon. The Nifty Smallcap 250 rose 1.54% and 364 of the 500 shares in the Nifty 500 rose. That buying is a one-time event, and Monday without it is the honest test of whether the rise was real.

Two more facts belong up here. Foreign institutions sold Indian shares on Tuesday and Thursday, a combined ₹6,187 crore, and then bought a net ₹599.54 crore on Friday, the first day of net foreign buying since 1 September; domestic institutions bought on all three days we have figures for, ₹7,323.49 crore in total. And over the week as a whole more shares fell than rose, 393 against 319 in our universe, the fourth week in a row in which that has been true.

**Tuesday 15 September was the day of the week, and the fall came late.** The Nifty opened 0.76% higher on a bid for IT shares, fell through the day and closed at its low, 23,118.60, down 1.19%, its lowest close in five months. The Sensex was up about 587 points during the morning and finished down 778, a swing of roughly 1,365 points. The smaller the company, the harder the fall: the Nifty Midcap 50 lost 2.08% and the Nifty Smallcap 250 lost 2.44%. Across the 712 shares we track, 83 rose and 629 fell, and the middle share lost 2.34%, twice what the index lost. India fell more than every Asian market open alongside it; Japan's Nikkei actually rose.

The cause was the long weekend. Three things turned against India while the market was shut, and Tuesday was the first session in which anyone could trade any of them. Oil: the Saudi East-West pipeline had been shut since a drone strike on Thursday 10 September, Brent settled at $105.68 on Monday with an intraday high near $109, and traded above $107 while India was open on Tuesday, a four-month high. American rates: the ten-year US Treasury yield, the interest rate the American government pays to borrow for ten years and the benchmark against which every other investment in the world is measured, touched 5% in Monday's American session for the first time since 2007. The cost of money at home: the Reserve Bank's ₹1 trillion bond-sale plan, announced on the previous Friday, had pushed India's ten-year yield to about 7.1%. Two inflation readings for August were also on the record before Tuesday's open (our public daily says both were released on Monday 14 September and checked that at the ministry's own release; our private daily left the release day open, and we do not resolve that here): consumer prices rose 4.82%, the tenth monthly rise in a row and the highest since December 2024, but the figure matched forecasts and sits inside the Reserve Bank's 2 to 6% target band, so it did not move Tuesday's market on its own; wholesale prices, the prices producers pay and charge each other, rose 9.92%, with fuel and power up 22.93% on the year.

The order in which sectors fell is the evidence for that cause. The sectors hurt most by dearer money and dearer oil fell hardest. Realty, the purest interest-rate trade because property is bought with borrowed money, fell 4.04%. Banks and lenders sold together on the 7.1% yield and the bond supply to come: State Bank of India −2.78%, ICICI Bank −2.10%, Shriram Finance −4.74%. Autos fell 2.01% and consumer durables 2.41%, both of them the oil-and-rates pair; Voltas closed at a 52-week low. Fourteen of fifteen sectors fell. The one that rose, IT, closed up 2.19% after being up 5.2% in the morning, on a reading that a weekend essay by the chief executive of Anthropic urging AI laboratories to slow down gives Indian services firms more time to adapt; that more than half the gain was gone by the close tells you how heavy the index selling was.

Two things fell on Tuesday for their own reasons, and Section 7 has both in full. Solar Industries fell 13.64% after filing, on the holiday Monday, an all-cash purchase of South Africa's Omnia Holdings for about ₹12,951 crore with nothing said about how it will be paid for, and it dragged the defence index down 5.96%. And the shares that had run hardest into September fell hardest: the exchange's Momentum-Quality 50 index, a basket of shares picked for recent price strength, fell 3.53%, twice the broad market, with no company-specific news at most of the names in it.

One thing about Tuesday we cannot explain: why the fall came in the last hour rather than at the open. Both the 5% American yield and the $107 oil price were known at 09:15. No Indian outlet located a catalyst for the afternoon reversal and neither did we. Our private record reads it as positioning ahead of Wednesday's Fed decision. Our public daily that day noted that Tuesday was, by its account, the weekly expiry of Nifty options, which adds noise to a day like this; our private record places the weekly expiry on Thursday instead, and we have not resolved which is right (Section 9). Cause of the intraday reversal: not established in our briefs.

**Wednesday 16 September rose 0.43%, and most shares did not rise with it.** The Nifty closed at 23,217.60 and Bank Nifty rose 0.89%, but on the BSE 119 shares fell for every 100 that rose, and in our universe 332 rose against 373 that fell with the middle share flat. The Midcap 50 was unchanged and the Smallcap 250 fell 0.16% after being down 2.06% in the morning. This was a large-company, financials-led session.

The cause is the calendar. Wednesday was the last session before the Fed's decision, which landed at about 23:30 India time, well after the close, so nothing about Wednesday can be attributed to the decision itself. The market was buying ahead of it, on two specific supports. First, rate relief before the bond supply arrived: the Indian ten-year yield eased about 4 basis points to roughly 7.05% (a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point), and the sectors that had fallen on Tuesday's rates leg rose on Wednesday's: PSU banks +1.44%, realty +0.99%, and life insurers +2.6 to 2.7%, because insurers hold long-dated bonds against long-dated promises and are the most rate-sensitive shares on the exchange. Twelve of fifteen sectors rose. Second, the exchange's own share sale: the NSE's anchor book, the portion of a share offer sold to large institutions the day before public bidding opens, was cut to ₹6,250 crore from ₹9,000 crore on roughly ₹22,569 crore of demand, and the listed companies that are selling NSE shares in the offer, among them State Bank of India (+2.42%) and the life insurers, led the index.

The best sector on Wednesday was FMCG, up 1.63%, and about a point of that was one company: ITC rose 2.38% on another round of cigarette price rises reported by the trade press. The worst was IT, down 1.58%, which gave back all of Tuesday's gain; TCS fell 2.76% with the rupee flat, so the fall was the AI story cooling rather than the currency. Oil eased a little during Indian hours, Brent at $107.56 against Tuesday's $108.75 settle, but the larger fact that day was negative and went uncarried by the Indian business press: Oman's foreign minister postponed indefinitely the Iran and Gulf meeting on reopening the Strait of Hormuz that had been the reason for an oil-price relief the previous week. Also on Wednesday, the Reserve Bank measured the cash surplus it is trying to drain: it took in ₹2.90 trillion of surplus bank cash at a single auction, and a second auction for ₹1 trillion drew only ₹40,302 crore, meaning banks would rather hold the cash than lend it to the central bank at 5.24%. We did not produce a public daily on Wednesday, only a private one; that gap is dealt with in Section 9.

**Thursday 17 September was the busiest day of the week, and we did not observe it.** The exchange was open. It was the first Indian session after the Fed's decision, the day of the Reserve Bank's first ₹50,000 crore bond sale, the opening day of bidding in the NSE's own share offer, and, on our private record, the weekly expiry. Our daily process did not run. There is no breadth count, no sector table and no list of movers for Thursday, and the index closes in the table above are derived from Friday's figures. What we know comes from Friday's 48-hour brief. On that account the Nifty rose about 0.2%, the Sensex was flat, mid- and small-cap indices rose about 1%, and every close-of-day wire report used the same words: little changed.

The cause of the flatness is that three feared events all passed without incident. The Fed raised its rate by 25 basis points to 3.75 to 4.00%, twelve votes to none, the first rise since 2023, with 16 of the 18 committee members expecting one more this year; the ten-year US yield stayed near 5%. A rise the market had priced at 92 to 94% likely for a week is not a surprise when it arrives. The Bank of Japan raised its rate by the same 25 basis points to 1.25%, and the yen fell anyway, which matters because a sharply stronger yen would have forced investors who borrow cheaply in yen to invest elsewhere to unwind those positions, and that unwinding has hit Indian shares before. It did not this time. And the Reserve Bank's ₹50,000 crore bond sale drew ₹66,590 crore of bids, sold in full, and left the ten-year yield at about 7.06%, essentially where it had been on Wednesday. Tuesday's selling had been braced for the opposite. Oil helped too: Brent settled at $104.82, down 0.95%, and Saudi Arabia said half the pipeline's capacity would return within days and the full line in about six weeks, a report we hold as reported rather than confirmed. Foreign institutions sold a net ₹3,208.76 crore on Thursday, the week's largest sale, and domestic institutions bought ₹3,617.75 crore. India VIX closed at 12.29, an observed figure.

Two more things happened on Thursday that set up Friday. The NSE's share offer opened for bidding at ₹1,700 to ₹1,785 a share, and the listed companies selling shares in it (New India Assurance, IFCI, GIC Re) rose as much as 9%. And after the close, the Tata Sons board and Tata Trusts fell out in public, which is Friday's story.

**Friday 18 September: the Nifty rose 0.33%, the Sensex fell 0.03%, and the gap is one stock.** The Nifty closed at 23,346.40, its third gain in a row, with 26 of its 50 shares up and 24 down. The Sensex had been about 400 points higher in the morning and gave it all back in the closing auction, the window from 15:15 to 15:35 in which the day's final prices are set by matching all closing orders at once. TCS fell 3.88% to ₹2,105. TCS carries a larger weight in the Sensex, which has 30 members, than in the Nifty, which has 50, and that weight difference is the entire gap between the two indices on the day.

The cause is the Tata Sons dispute. On Thursday evening the Tata Sons board re-appointed N. Chandrasekaran as executive chairman for a further five years by a four-to-one vote, with Noel Tata voting against. The same evening Tata Trusts, which owns about 66% of Tata Sons, said the resolution was a legal nullity under the company's articles. That is an assertion; no court, regulator or filing has ruled on it. A wire report that the board had also approved a stock-market listing of Tata Sons came from a single anonymous source and was contradicted by the Trusts the same day; it is a claim, not a fact. Friday priced the split across the group: Tata Chemicals fell 11.04%, after rising 19.99% on Tuesday when the Reserve Bank's letter telling Tata Sons to proceed to a listing was reported, so Tuesday's rise and Friday's fall are the same story read in opposite directions; Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles fell 3.40%, Voltas 2.12%, Tata Steel 0.93%, Titan 0.88%. Infosys, with no Tata problem, fell 0.68%. Nifty IT's 1.03% fall was one stock and one governance story, not the rupee.

Everything else on Friday went up, and small companies went up most. The Nifty Smallcap 250 rose 1.54% with 184 of its shares up and 66 down; the Nifty 500 rose 0.82% with 364 up and 135 down. The cause is mechanical: the FTSE index provider implemented its September membership review at Friday's close, so every fund that copies a FTSE index had to buy the added or upweighted Indian companies, at the closing price, on Friday afternoon. The exchange's bulk-deal record shows Vanguard index funds doing exactly that: Avalon Technologies (+13.80% on twelve times normal volume, with a single Vanguard purchase 16% of the day's turnover), SJS Enterprises (+6.92%, 40% of the day's turnover), Pine Labs, SKF India. Route Mobile, deleted from the index, fell 5.72%. Twelve of sixteen sector indices rose. Metal rose 1.51%, almost all of it Hindustan Zinc's 4.14% rise on silver and copper prices. Realty (+1.19%) and PSU banks (+0.66%) rose on rate relief after the bond sale had cleared. HDFC Bank rose 2.52% on ₹2,880 crore of turnover, most of Bank Nifty's rise, and we have no established cause for it. Asia was strong alongside India: the Nikkei rose 1.71%, the KOSPI 2.30%, the Hang Seng 2.31%. The rupee firmed 0.28% to 95.863 against a dollar that rose on its own index, which tells you the rupee's recovery was about cheaper oil rather than a weaker dollar. India VIX fell 7.36% to 11.39. And foreign institutions bought a net ₹599.54 crore, their first net purchase since 1 September.

## 3. The market underneath the index: breadth, flows, fear and the prices that drove it

**Breadth is a count, not an average.** It asks how many separate companies rose and how many fell, rather than how far the index travelled. An index can rise because one enormous company rose, or because most companies rose, and only the count tells you which. Friday is this week's proof: the Sensex fell while 364 of the Nifty 500's shares rose, because one heavy stock had a bad day.

Tuesday was the week's one true breadth event. Fewer than 12 shares in every 100 rose, and the middle share lost twice what the index lost. That is what a crowd leaving looks like, not 629 separate company stories. Wednesday's rise was not shared: the index gained 0.43% while more shares fell than rose and the middle share was flat, which is the signature of a session in which only large financial companies were bought. Friday's rise was shared, and then some, on the strongest breadth of the week, on a day the headline Sensex fell. Thursday's count does not exist. The derived index path, with mid- and small-cap indices up about 1% while the Nifty was flat, points to a positive day for most shares, but we do not assert a number we did not see. One thing we can measure across the unseen Thursday: from Wednesday's close to Friday's close, 557 of the 713 shares we track rose and 155 fell, with the middle share up 1.96% over the two sessions, so whatever Thursday's count was, the two sessions together were strongly positive for the typical share.

Over the week, 319 shares rose and 393 fell out of 712, and the middle share lost 0.31%. Tuesday was so one-sided that the three sessions after it could not fully reverse it for the typical share, even though the index itself recovered almost all of its Tuesday fall (Nifty −1.19% on Tuesday, −0.22% on the week). This is the fourth week in a row in which fallers outnumbered risers on our record: 313 against 385 in the week to 21 August, 316 against 388 to 28 August, 258 against 446 to 4 September, and now 319 against 393. The week to 11 September was never measured, because no weekly brief was written for it; Section 8 says more about that lapse.

**Who bought and who sold.** Two pools of professional money dominate Indian trading. Foreign institutional investors are funds and institutions based outside India. Domestic institutional investors are Indian mutual funds, insurers, banks and pension funds, which is to a large degree household savings arriving through monthly investment plans. Foreign investors sold on both of the sessions before Friday for which a figure exists, and Thursday's sale, on the day the market absorbed the Fed's rise, was the larger of the two. Tuesday's was the fifth straight session of foreign selling and the largest of the five by about three times. Domestic institutions bought on every session we can see, and over the three sessions their buying ran at about 1.3 times foreign selling; on Tuesday they absorbed roughly 90% of what foreigners sold, which is a large part of why a fall to a five-month low happened in an orderly way. Friday's foreign purchase of ₹599.54 crore was the first net foreign buying since 1 September. It is one session, and one purchase is not a turn.

What we cannot say is how long the foreign selling streak ran, because Wednesday's figure was never published by anyone, on any day, and still had not been by Friday evening. If Wednesday was a sale, Thursday was the seventh straight day of foreign selling; if not, the streak broke on Wednesday. We do not know, and we do not print a streak length we cannot count. One observation from our own records, offered only as that: foreign investors' gross turnover on Friday, purchases of ₹38,461.63 crore against sales of ₹37,862.09 crore, was about two and a half times Tuesday's, which is consistent with index funds transacting in the closing auction on the FTSE review day; the small net figure sits inside a very large gross one.

**The price of fear spiked and came back out.** India VIX is the market's own estimate, worked out from option prices, of how much the Nifty 50 is likely to move over the next thirty days; a high reading means protection against a surprise is expensive because people are buying it. It closed at 10.68 at the end of the 4 September week, was about 12.29 by the previous Friday on our arithmetic, jumped 9.30% on Tuesday to 13.43 (the exchange's own figure was 13.27), its first close above 13 since early August, then fell on each of the next three sessions to 13.17, 12.29 and finally 11.39 on Friday, a 7.36% fall on the last day alone. That Friday fall is the cost of insuring against the Fed and the bond sale coming out in one session, once both had happened without an accident. A VIX of 13 on a five-month-low close is a market that fell in an orderly way, not one that panicked; for scale, the index's range over the past year runs from 10.57 to 27.89. The previous weekly wrote that low volatility "is what protection costs when nobody is buying any. It is not a forecast of calm." Ten sessions later the VIX had risen from 10.68 to 13.43 and the Nifty had fallen from 23,897.70 to 23,118.60. The week ended with the VIX back near where that warning was written.

**The rupee.** The rupee closed at 95.945 to the dollar on Tuesday, down 0.26% and its weakest in seven weeks (the record low is 96.96), with the central bank reported to be defending the mid-95s. About half of Tuesday's fall was the broad dollar, which rose 0.51% on its index that day, and the rest was India's own oil bill. It was unchanged in level on Wednesday. On Thursday a 24-hour composite price series showed 96.132, which would be the first reading above 96; but that series is not the onshore 15:30 close, no confirmed onshore close above 96 exists for Thursday, and the Reserve Bank's reference rate, which would settle it, was not fetched, so the honest state is that the rupee's weakest point of the week was Thursday and that it may or may not have crossed 96. On Friday it firmed 0.28% to 95.863, against a dollar that rose 0.30% on its own index, which means the rupee's recovery was about oil coming off rather than the dollar. Friday to Friday the rupee was about 0.17% weaker. Separately, the Reserve Bank's weekly figures published on Friday showed foreign-exchange reserves of $780.78 billion for the week to 11 September, down $4.93 billion from the record set the week before. Our public daily on Tuesday had set a test in advance: a fall of $5 billion or more would be the first sign of genuine rupee defence that the recent foreign-currency deposit inflows can no longer hide. The fall came in $70 million short of that line, the split between currency sales and gold revaluation was not published, and we do not call it intervention. Cause of the reserves fall: not established in our briefs beyond "consistent with, not proof of" defence.

**Oil.** Brent settled at $104.61 on Friday 11 September, $105.68 on Monday 14 September with an intraday high near $109, $108.75 on Tuesday (up 2.9% on the day and more than 20% higher in September), traded at $107.56 at Wednesday's Indian close, settled at $104.82 on Thursday, and traded roughly between $103 and $105 on Friday with no settlement price available when we wrote. So on the week Brent finished within a dollar or two of where it started, having spiked to a four-month high in the middle. Our own price feed printed two bars this week that we rejected, one showing Brent at $102.24 on Tuesday, which contradicted three independent sources and was captured after India's close, and one showing $100.00 on Friday, which is an artefact of the price series switching from one expiring futures contract to the next, a switch that can print a price change no market made. Neither is used anywhere in this brief. What oil did to the week: it was the first leg of Tuesday's fall and the relief behind Friday's rupee and VIX. The Hormuz reopening talks that had eased oil the previous week were postponed indefinitely on Wednesday; the Saudi pipeline's phased restart, if it happens as reported, restores the bypass route. Both of those are reported, and the second is an expectation, not an event.

**The Indian bond yield.** Every Indian government bond yield in this brief is a single-source web reading, because our own bond feed has failed on 33 consecutive runs. On that basis the ten-year yield, the annual interest the market demands to lend the government money for ten years, went from 7.02% on 11 September to about 7.08% on Tuesday, a four-month high, on the announcement of the bond sale; eased to about 7.05% on Wednesday in relief before the sale; and sat at about 7.06% on Friday after the first ₹50,000 crore had been sold to ₹66,590 crore of bids. The exchange's own bond indices corroborate the Wednesday direction. In plain words: the central bank said it would sell a trillion rupees of bonds; bond prices fell and yields rose in advance; then the first sale was over-bid and the yield stopped rising. The shares that trade on the cost of money, property companies and public-sector banks, fell on Tuesday and rose on Wednesday and Friday in step with that path. Gold, for the record, fell 1.79% to $4,330 across the two American sessions before Tuesday, because the dollar rather than gold was the safe asset when the US yield hit 5%; it recovered to $4,385 on Wednesday and closed the week at $4,387. Silver rose 1.97% on Friday to $66.76, which is the reason Hindustan Zinc rose 4.14% that day.

## 4. Earnings: nobody of consequence reported, and that is the calendar, not an oversight

**No company of any size reported quarterly results this week.** Indian listed companies report four times a year, and the season for the quarter that ended on 30 June ran through July and into mid-August. The quarter now running ends on 30 September, and nobody can report it before it ends. Mid-September is the trough between two seasons, and no price move anywhere in this brief is attributed to a quarterly result. The first large-company marker of the next season on our record is Infosys, whose board meets on its second-quarter results on 22 and 23 October; the rest arrive through late October and November.

Our automated count did tag nine companies as filing results this week. We checked every one, and none is a result anyone should read as this season's: they are June-quarter numbers arriving four to five weeks late from very small companies, a company in insolvency catching up on four overdue sets of results at once, one annual result for the year to March 2026 filed six months late, and one re-filing of an August result in a more legible format. Every one was filed after the close of its day. Late filers are, as a group, the smallest and most stressed companies on the exchange. The count exists; it says nothing about the week.

What fills a September gap is annual general meetings, the once-a-year shareholder meeting that votes on directors, dividends and the accounts, and this week one of them was worth reading properly. **Engineers India**, the state-owned engineering consultancy, held its meeting on Friday. The chairman's speech restated a record year to March 2026, revenue ₹3,849 crore (up 27.1%), profit ₹638 crore (up 37.3%), and an order book, meaning work already won but not yet done, of ₹15,109 crore. Those are last year's numbers, already published. What was not said is the story: on its August results call the company had promised ₹8,000 crore of new orders this year, at least 10% growth and a 16% margin, meaning 16 rupees of every hundred of sales kept as profit after costs, and the speech repeated none of the three, nor withdrew them. The next chance to hear them again is the second-quarter call in late October. The vote count, filed after the close, was the other thing worth reading. Institutional shareholders, meaning the mutual funds, insurers and foreign funds whose votes are counted separately, voted 60.0% against re-appointing one director and 51.2% against appointing another as an independent director. Every resolution passed anyway, because the President of India holds 51.32% and voted for. Oil India's meeting the day before had shown the same shape, with 38.1%, 26.7% and 25.1% against on its director resolutions. In plain words, professional investors are voting against government-nominated directors at state-owned companies and losing, because the government's block outvotes them. ## 6. Three things worth understanding properly

### 6a. Why a record foreign-reserves number and a five-month low in property shares are the same story

This is the week's best teaching chain, and it runs in four steps.

**Step one: the swap window.** Over the summer Indian banks raised about US$136 billion, mostly foreign-currency deposits from Indians living abroad, and swapped those dollars with the Reserve Bank of India under a special window that closed at the end of August. A swap here means the bank hands the Reserve Bank dollars today and gets rupees, with an agreement to reverse the exchange later. The dollars now sit on the Reserve Bank's books; the rupees sit in the banking system.

**Step two: the reserves jump that is not what it looks like.** India's foreign-exchange reserves rose a record US$44.9 billion in the week to 4 September, to US$785.7 billion. That is the swap window settling onto the central bank's books. It is not money India owns outright, because the Reserve Bank has to hand those dollars back later. A record reserves headline reads like strength, and this one is a loan.

**Step three: the rupee side of the swap is a cash flood.** The rupees the Reserve Bank paid out for those dollars showed up as a surplus of roughly ₹10.5 trillion in the banking system, about ₹9.85 trillion by Wednesday. Too much cash chasing too few borrowers pushed the overnight rate at which banks lend to each other below the Reserve Bank's 5.25% policy rate, which is the rate at which it lends to banks and the anchor for every other interest rate in the country. If overnight market rates sit below it, monetary policy is looser in practice than on paper, at a time when consumer inflation has risen ten months in a row.

**Step four: the Reserve Bank takes the cash back by selling bonds.** On 11 September it announced it would sell ₹1 trillion of government bonds outright: ₹50,000 crore on Thursday 17 September, then ₹25,000 crore on each of Monday 21 and Monday 28 September. It is the first large net sale of this kind in two years. An outright sale means the Reserve Bank hands bonds to banks and keeps the rupees permanently, which is different from the reverse-repo auctions it also ran this week, where banks park cash with it for a short period and get it back. The difference showed on Wednesday: banks parked ₹2.90 trillion at one such auction, but a second auction for ₹1 trillion drew only ₹40,302 crore because banks would rather hold the cash, which is why the surplus is sticky and the bond sale is what removes it. This is liquidity management, not a rate rise; the policy rate is unchanged.

**Why that hurts share prices.** More bonds for sale means bond prices fall, and when a bond's price falls its yield rises. The ten-year government bond yield rose to about 7.1% on Tuesday, a four-month high, up roughly a quarter of a percentage point in three weeks. That yield is the risk-free rupee rate, what an investor can earn lending to the government with no practical risk of not being paid, and every other investment is compared against it. When it rises, a property developer's future sales are worth less today, a lender's cost of funds goes up, and a bank's or insurer's bond holdings lose value. So on Tuesday realty fell 4.04%, banks and lenders sold together, and public-sector banks fell 2.29%. The same sectors rose on Wednesday when the yield eased in advance of the sale, and rose again on Friday after Thursday's sale drew ₹66,590 crore of bids for ₹50,000 crore and left the yield flat. In a bond sale the cut-off is the yield at which the last accepted bid clears, and a sale that clears without buyers demanding a higher yield is a sale the market absorbed comfortably. Two tranches remain, on the two Mondays ahead.

**Where the American leg joins.** A foreign fund can lend to the US government for ten years and earn about 5% a year in dollars with essentially no risk, the highest such return since 2007. To hold Indian shares instead it needs to expect more than that after allowing for the risk that the rupee weakens and eats its return. When the safe dollar return rises and the rupee is at a seven-week low, the extra reward for holding India shrinks, and some of that money goes home; the ₹2,977.86 crore of foreign selling on Tuesday and the ₹3,208.76 crore on Thursday are what that looks like. The Fed has now raised once and 16 of its 18 committee members expect one more this year. The Reserve Bank's own rate-setting committee meets on 5 to 7 October, and the GST Council, moved from 12 September because of the BRICS summit in Delhi, now meets on the same 7 October. It walks in with consumer inflation at 4.82%, wholesale inflation at 9.92%, a bond-sale programme in progress and a higher American rate. No Reserve Bank official has said the word "hike", and we are not saying it for them.

### 6b. Tata Sons, in two acts: the regulator closed the exit door on Tuesday, and the owners split in public on Thursday

Tata Sons is the unlisted holding company at the top of the Tata group, the company through which the group holds its stakes in TCS, Titan, Tata Steel, Voltas and the rest. Nobody can buy its shares. This week it moved listed shares twice, in opposite directions.

**Act one.** The Reserve Bank told Tata Sons, in a letter dated 11 September and reported over the weekend, that it cannot surrender its registration as a core investment company, which is the regulator's label for a company whose business is holding shares in other group companies, and that it should proceed to a stock-market listing. Tata Sons had applied to give up that registration; the Reserve Bank said no. The letter itself is not public, and the reasoning is not on our record. On Tuesday, the first session that could price it, Tata Chemicals rose 19.99% and closed locked at its upper price band, the exchange's daily limit on how far a share may rise, meaning there were more buyers than the limit allowed through; Tata Investment Corporation rose 10.36%. The reason is arithmetic. Tata Chemicals owns 2.53% of Tata Sons, and at the roughly US$200 billion private valuations cited in the press for Tata Sons, that stake alone would be worth more than Tata Chemicals' entire market value of ₹18,722 crore. If Tata Sons lists, that holding becomes a visible, sellable asset priced by the market rather than a line in an annual report. No listing had been announced and there was no date; what had changed was that the regulator had closed the exit door.

**Act two.** On Thursday evening the Tata Sons board re-appointed N. Chandrasekaran as executive chairman for five years by a four-to-one vote, with Noel Tata voting against. The same evening Tata Trusts, the charitable trusts that own about 66% of Tata Sons, said the resolution was a legal nullity under the company's articles of association, the company's own rulebook on how decisions get made. In plain words, the majority owner says the board's vote does not count. That is an assertion; no court, regulator or filing has ruled on it. A wire line that the board also approved a listing came from a single anonymous source and was contradicted by the Trusts the same day, so it is a claim and not a fact, and this brief does not write that Tata Sons has approved a listing. On Friday the group's shares fell: Tata Chemicals −11.04%, TCS −3.88%, Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles −3.40%, Voltas −2.12%, Tata Steel −0.93%, Titan −0.88%. Tata Chemicals' Tuesday rise was a bet that a listing had become more likely; Thursday's split, with the owner calling the board's decision void and denying a listing was approved, made the path to it less clear, and Friday reversed the bet. Voltas and Titan fell as Tata companies on the group's bad day, not on anything of their own. About ₹30 lakh crore of listed group value, which is ₹30 trillion, now trades on two statements and no order. The next dated forum, the Tata Sons annual general meeting, has no date on our record.

### 6c. UPI's first merchant fee: what changes on 15 October, and for whom

On Tuesday the steering committee of the National Payments Corporation of India, the body that runs UPI, decided to levy a merchant discount rate, which is the fee a shop pays its own bank on a digital payment, of 0.4% on person-to-merchant UPI payments above ₹2,000, from 15 October. The fee is capped at ₹300 per transaction, and telecom, rail, fuel and insurance payments carry a flat ₹5 instead. Payments up to ₹2,000, and all person-to-person transfers, stay free under a government notification issued on Monday 14 September, which is the one primary document in this story; the 0.4% figure itself is reported by several outlets and held as firm until the payments corporation's own circular is on file.

What it means for you: sending money to a friend changes nothing at any amount; paying a shop ₹2,000 or less changes nothing; paying a shop more than ₹2,000 means the shop's bank charges the shop 0.4%, up to ₹300, and whether shops pass that on in their prices is not something anyone has established. Why it matters more than the small numbers suggest: UPI has run without a merchant fee until now, and payments above ₹2,000 were only 4% of transactions by count last year but two thirds of the money moved. Who gets the fee is not settled. An estimate of a ₹17,000 crore annual pool split between banks, payment apps and payment aggregators was carried by one outlet from unnamed analysts, and it is a claim, not a fact. No listed company has filed anything on the fee. On Thursday the Finance Ministry called an allegation that the decision came under any external influence "patently false", a statement carried by one outlet; the fee is a political object before it is a revenue line.

### Other corporate news that reached a price

**The exchange is selling its own shares.** The NSE's offer, at ₹1,700 to ₹1,785 a share, opened for bidding on Thursday and closes on Monday 21 September, with allotment on Tuesday 22 and listing on Thursday 24. On Wednesday the anchor book was cut to ₹6,250 crore from ₹9,000 crore because, in the issuer's own framing, demand of about ₹22,569 crore was too large. By the end of Friday, day two, the ₹22,561.57 crore offer was covered 1.04 times, meaning bids slightly exceeded the shares on offer: retail investors 0.69 times, wealthy individuals 1.46 times, institutions excluding the anchors 1.32 times, on figures relayed by brokers. Listed companies that own NSE shares and are selling into the offer traded as proxies for it all week: IFCI and New India Assurance fell about 6.5% each on Tuesday on the cut in the offer's size, and New India Assurance, IFCI and GIC Re rose as much as 9% on Thursday.

**Institutions voted "no" a great deal this week, and it changed nothing.** Beyond Engineers India (Section 4), institutional shareholders at Fineotex Chemical voted 60.90% against an equity raise and 69.39% against a related-party transaction, a deal between the company and someone connected to it, and both passed, the raise on promoter votes and the transaction on the non-institutional public's votes, since promoters may not vote on a related-party matter. At Gandhar Oil Refinery, 65.52% of voting institutions rejected a change to the company's objects clause, the part of its constitution that says what businesses it may carry on, and it passed. Reading the scrutiniser's report, the document that records how each class of shareholder voted, is the only way to see this; the headline "resolution passed" hides it.

**Coforge's board is litigating a departing director's letter inside a filing.** The chairman left on 9 September. An independent director who chaired the nominations committee resigned on 10 September, in a filing published after Friday 11 September's close, alleging differences and tension between independent and executive directors after an internal-audit review; the board's reply in the same filing calls his claims unfounded. Two committee offices turned over in four days, with no accounting signal. The stock fell 3.85% on Tuesday, the only red name in an IT index up 2.19%, and rose 2.68% on Friday, the only IT name up on the day TCS fell. An interim chairman is in place to 31 January 2027.

**India's first corporate bonds settled on a blockchain.** On 10 September the market regulator and the Reserve Bank launched a pilot in which bonds are issued and settled on a blockchain using the central bank's wholesale digital rupee. REC and Larsen & Toubro raised ₹500 crore each and IIFL Finance ₹25 crore through it, from institutional buyers. It is a pilot with three phases, institutions first and retail later, and nothing about how an individual buys a bond has changed yet.
