## 1. The week in plain English

The Nifty 50 finished the week at 24,175.65, down 0.31% across five sessions. The Nifty 500, the broadest index we track and the one that comes nearest to describing the whole listed market, finished down 0.01%. After a full week of trading, that is dead flat.

It was not a quiet week. It was a round trip. The index fell on Monday, rose on Tuesday, fell on Wednesday, fell harder on Thursday and recovered part of it on Friday: 24,219.05, then 24,334.55, then 24,207.75, then 24,090.85, then 24,175.65. Tuesday's close was the week's high, Thursday's was its low, and Friday gave back a little under half of the fall. The Nifty 500's best and worst closes of the week sit 129 points apart, and the week's net change is under two points.

Three separate forces pushed in three separate directions and roughly cancelled. None of them had anything to do with the other two.

**Technology moved on an American clock.** Nifty IT, the index of the country's software services companies, fell 1.47% on Wednesday after the United States paused visa appointments at its embassies and consulates worldwide while it re-screens consular officers, which constrains how quickly Indian firms can move staff to client sites. It then fell a further 0.32% on Thursday, and rose 3.51% on Friday alone after an overnight American technology rally. Over the week it gained 2.45%, the second-best sector reading we hold.

**Crude oil fell on every single session.** Brent, the global benchmark grade of oil against which India prices most of what it imports, closed the week around US$88.36 against US$94.19 the previous Friday, a fall of roughly 6%. The cause was diplomacy rather than demand: Iran and Oman resumed talks over the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow sea passage through which the bulk of India's crude arrives, discussing a temporary navigational corridor and agreeing to work on clearing mines. Cheaper oil is a tailwind for a country that imports about 85% of what it refines.

**And the domestic-demand complex sold off quietly all week.** Consumer staples, meaning the everyday products Indian households buy from Indian companies, fell on almost every session we can measure. Nifty FMCG lost 1.46% on the week, Nifty Consumption 0.95% and Nifty Auto 0.94%. No headline named any of them. Those three falls are the largest weekly sector moves in the market and they went unreported everywhere, including in our own daily briefs on most days.

Now the number that actually describes the week. **Across roughly 704 shares we can price reliably, 316 rose over the week and 388 fell.** So the typical Indian share lost money in a week the headline called flat, and the Nifty Smallcap 250 index still finished at a 52-week high on Friday. Day by day it is starker: **on three of the five sessions the index moved one way and the typical share moved the other**, and on a fourth the index understated the fall by nearly half. Exactly one session, Thursday, had a headline that honestly described what happened to the average shareholder.

That gap is the week. Anyone who read this week off the index read almost none of it.

## 2. How each day moved, and why

Two words before the table. **Breadth** is nothing more complicated than the count of shares that rose against the count that fell, out of the roughly 705 companies we can price reliably each day. The **median share** is the one exactly in the middle of that list, so it describes the typical company's day rather than the biggest company's day. An index cannot tell you either, because it weights every company by size.

| Session | Nifty 50 | Rose | Fell | Median share | Did the index describe the market? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon 24 Aug | 24,219.05, **−0.14%** | 302 | 400 | −0.24% | Direction yes, scale no |
| Tue 25 Aug | 24,334.55, **+0.48%** | 320 | 386 | −0.12% | **No, opposite** |
| Wed 26 Aug | 24,207.75, **−0.52%** | 383 | 322 | +0.14% | **No, opposite** |
| Thu 27 Aug | 24,090.85, **−0.48%** | 253 | 449 | −0.46% | **Yes, cleanly** |
| Fri 28 Aug | 24,175.65, **+0.35%** | 343 | 361 | −0.04% | **No, opposite** |

### Monday: a flat index sitting on a falling market

The Nifty lost 32.95 points, which on 24,219 is 0.14% and is not worth an explanation in itself. Underneath it, 400 shares fell against 302 that rose, the second consecutive session of a falling market under a flat headline. The Sensex fell 0.22%, Bank Nifty 0.41% and the Smallcap 250 index 0.21%.

The day's named cause was geopolitical and it pointed the wrong way. The United States Treasury Secretary announced what he called the single greatest financial offensive ever mounted against Iran, and the part that reaches India is that Washington says it will sanction any country that keeps trading with Iran. Iran's Persian Gulf Strait Authority replied that vessels breaching its rules face fines, seizure or confiscation, and both sides let the ceasefire lapse. And yet Brent crude fell about 1.8% on the day to roughly US$92.68, after a week in which it had risen more than 5%.

A threat to oil supply that leaves oil cheaper has not been priced as a threat to oil supply. What the market appears to have priced instead is the payments and trade channel, meaning the risk to how India settles and routes purchases, rather than the size of the import bill. It is worth keeping the scale in view: a 0.14% close is a very small reaction for a country that imports 85% of its crude through that waterway.

One further point on Monday, because it is the rule that runs through the whole week. The American sanctions announcement itself landed between roughly 17:30 and 19:00 India time, after the Indian market had already shut. Monday traded the anticipation, which had been pre-announced on Sunday. It did not trade the contents.

### Tuesday: the index rose and most shares fell

The Nifty rose 0.48% to 24,334.55, the week's high, while 386 shares fell and 320 rose. This is the cleanest single explanation of the week and it has nothing to do with sentiment.

Tuesday was the National Stock Exchange's monthly derivatives expiry, the day on which the month's futures and options contracts settle and positions in them have to be closed or rolled forward. The date was checked against the calendar rather than assumed: 1 August was a Saturday, which puts the month's Tuesdays on the 4th, 11th, 18th and 25th, and the convention is the last Tuesday. Settlement demand concentrates in a narrow band of very large, heavily traded companies, which is exactly the band an index weights most. So expiry propped up the index through a handful of large companies while the rest of the market did not participate.

India VIX, the index measuring what traders are paying for protection against a sharp market move over the coming month, fell 3.95% on a day the index rose. That is not the usual pairing.

Two events from Tuesday belong to Wednesday, not to Tuesday. A United States sanctions expansion on Iran was announced without being imposed, with no countries named, no effective date and time given to comply, and crude fell to a one-week low. And Varun Beverages' board approved, at 16:21 after the close, a wholly owned Indian subsidiary to enter ready-to-drink alcoholic beverages plus a Tunisia joint venture. A filing after the close is the next session's cause, never the current one.

### Wednesday: the index fell and two-thirds of shares rose

The Nifty fell 0.52% and 383 shares rose against 322 that fell. The median share gained 0.14%. This is the exact reverse of Tuesday, on consecutive days.

The dated cause behind the weakness at the top was real and narrow. The United States has paused visa appointments at its embassies and consulates worldwide while it re-screens and retrains consular officers, with no announced date for normal availability to resume. That constrains the throughput of Indian technology firms who move staff onto client sites, and it is the one dated cause behind Nifty IT falling 1.47%.

But check that against the shares underneath, because the index badly overstates it. Across the IT index's 34 constituents the median share fell only 0.39%, twelve of them rose, and Cyient gained 7.01%. Five very large companies were effectively the whole index move. The gap between the sector's best and worst performer that day was 12.41 percentage points, the widest in the market, and that is the opposite of what a sector-wide shock looks like. One related caution: the six-figure H-1B visa fee circulating alongside this story is a proposal, not a rule in force.

The genuinely broad falls on Wednesday were the ones nobody named. Consumption fell 0.82% with six of 27 names higher, and FMCG fell 0.70% with six of 22 higher. Against them, metals rose 1.75% with sixteen of eighteen higher, pharma 0.89% with seventeen of 24 higher, and public-sector banks 0.62% with all thirteen higher.

The size gradient reads as one line: Nifty 50 constituents down 0.66%, Nifty 100 down 0.43%, Midcap 50 down 0.17%, Smallcap 250 up 0.59%. The bigger the company, the worse the day. Seven of the twelve biggest risers closed at 52-week highs.

Crude fell again, on Iran and Oman resuming talks over the Strait of Hormuz, discussing a temporary navigational corridor and agreeing to work on clearing mines, with Oman's foreign minister hopeful of an announcement soon and a permanent route negotiated within 30 to 60 days. Two cautions travel with that. A corridor has been discussed, not announced. And a political claim that all mines are already cleared sits in the same news cycle as an agreement to clear them, and both cannot be true.

India VIX fell 4.65% to 10.565, near its 52-week low, through a session that opened with an unresolved Hormuz blockage and a visa pause on the country's largest export-services sector. The option market had stopped pricing either as a live risk.

### Thursday: the one session where the index told the truth

The Nifty fell 0.48%, 449 shares fell against 253 that rose, and the median share fell 0.46%. It was the week's worst day for the typical shareholder and the only day the headline matched the experience. It was also broad rather than shallow at the tail: 238 shares fell more than 1% against 120 that rose more than 1%, so the whole distribution shifted.

There are two separate answers to why, and the first is arithmetic rather than sentiment. **HDFC Bank fell 2.23% to ₹711.00, and on roughly a 13% weight in the index that single share takes about 0.29 percentage points out of a 0.48% fall.** Its heavier weight in the Sensex is why the Sensex fell 0.70%. Nobody should write that banks sold off: Kotak Mahindra Bank rose 1.80%, ICICI Bank 0.91% and Axis Bank 0.08%, and the private-bank group's median of minus 0.34% beat the market.

What is wearing HDFC Bank down is its own arithmetic. Its net interest margin, the gap between what a bank earns on its loans and what it pays on its deposits, is at a record low 3.26%. Low-cost deposits are 32% of the book against 38% two years ago, and foreign holding has fallen for a fifth straight quarter. Two dated items sat alongside on the day: a United States lawsuit disclosed that day on a single credible report we did not second-source, and an escalation dated 26 August in which more than 75 investors holding over US$13.5 million of principal in a Luxembourg life fund sold through the bank's Dubai operations are writing to the Prime Minister's Office and approaching the Reserve Bank. That escalation is reported investor intent, not a filed complaint, and it should be read at that grade.

Second, Thursday was the BSE Sensex's monthly derivatives expiry, on the last-Thursday convention, while the Nifty's own monthly expiry had already gone on Tuesday. So the Sensex falling harder than the Nifty needs no sentiment explanation at all.

Taking the positives first: pharma was the day's one real advance and it was genuinely broad, the index up 0.84% with fourteen of nineteen constituents higher and a median constituent up 0.50%. Removing the two largest risers still leaves the remaining seventeen positive. Underneath it sat a split with no established cause: pharmaceutical manufacturing and hospitals rose while diagnostics were sold hard, Thyrocare down 3.92%, Concord Biotech down 3.91% and Aris down 2.75%. No same-day catalyst was established for either leg.

Cement was the casualty. Not one of eleven cement names rose, and the median cement share fell 1.03%. UltraTech's flat close was the best in the group, Ambuja fell 1.94%, Shree Cement 1.55%, Dalmia Bharat 1.15%. No dated cement demand, pricing or policy item from Thursday turned up anywhere. Cause not established in our briefs, and after a full search that is a finding rather than a gap. Metals were next at 1.12% down at the median, then state-owned banks at 0.90% down with exactly one advance in twelve.

India VIX rose 4.71% to 11.0675 alongside a lower index and negative breadth, the textbook pairing that Tuesday and Wednesday both lacked. The qualifier matters as much as the number: 11.07 is 60.3% below its own 52-week high, so it corroborates the direction and establishes nothing about stress.

One last Thursday finding, because it cuts against the usual story. Midcap 50 fell 0.10% and Smallcap 250 fell 0.12%, against a small-company median of minus 0.54%, the worst of any group measured. Meanwhile the Nifty 50's own median constituent fell 0.27%, shallower than the market. The small-company indices understated the damage in exactly the way the large-company indices overstated it.

### Friday: a green headline on a flat market

The Nifty rose 0.35% and the Sensex 0.43%, and 361 shares fell against 343 that rose. The median share lost 0.04%.

It was one sector. **Nifty IT rose 3.51%, with every one of its nine priced constituents higher, and on that sector's weight it accounts for effectively the whole of the Nifty's gain.** Coforge rose 5.93%, Tata Consultancy Services 4.16%, Persistent Systems 3.91%, Tech Mahindra 3.53%, Infosys 2.99% and Mphasis 1.72%. Strip the technology names out of the Nifty 50 and its own constituents were 18 up, 18 down, one flat, with a median move of 0.00%.

Why technology, and why on Friday rather than Thursday, is section 6's second explainer. The short version is a clock. Nvidia reported after the American close on Wednesday. On Thursday India traded only the press release, because the American market had not yet opened to vote on it, and Nifty IT actually fell 0.32% that day. The session that voted closed at 01:30 India time on Friday morning, with the Nasdaq up 1.57%, Nvidia up 8.7% and technology the only advancing S&P 500 sector. India's 09:15 open on Friday was its first chance to price it.

Two facts that belong side by side. The Nifty Smallcap 250 closed at a 52-week high on a session where 361 of 706 shares fell, so the index was at a record and the median small-company shareholder made nothing. And not one share in the 707-name universe fell 5% or more; the worst close anywhere was minus 4.66%.

India VIX fell 3.52% to 10.68, leaving it 1.04% above its own 52-week low, going into a weekend containing a growth release, an index rebalance and a policy deadline. The market was charging almost nothing to insure against a surprise.

What was missing was our writing, not the observation, and it is worse for the public reader than for the private one.

### The typical share had a losing week and the small-company index hit a high

Over the five sessions, **316 shares rose and 388 fell out of 704**. Set that against where the indices finished and the picture is a genuine puzzle rather than a rounding difference.

The small and mid-sized company indices rose, and yet more small and mid-sized companies fell than rose. Both statements are true, and the reason is the same weighting arithmetic that runs through the whole week: an index is an average weighted by company size, so within the small-company universe the larger small companies can carry the index while the count of shares goes the other way.

There is a second week of evidence for this. The breadth counts for the two most recent weeks are almost identical.

Three more shares rose and three more fell than in the week before, out of roughly seven hundred. That is two consecutive weeks in which slightly more than half the market fell by nearly the same margin, and the second consecutive week the Nifty itself declined. Against the week ending 7 August, when 427 rose and 259 fell, the change in character is unmistakable. There is no row for the week ending 14 August, so the series itself has a hole in it and this comparison skips a week.

The shape is clear enough without a total. **Domestic institutions bought on every one of the four days we hold, without exception.** Foreign investors bought modestly on Monday and Wednesday, sold slightly on Thursday, and then sold heavily on Friday. Friday's sale is roughly an order of magnitude larger than either of the two buying days. Whatever else Friday was, it was not foreign money returning.

**Friday's pairing is the single most instructive fact in this section.** Foreign investors sold ₹5,039.80 crore and domestic institutions bought ₹5,183.93 crore, absorbing it almost exactly, on a day the index closed higher. Two nearly equal and opposite flows, and a green screen on top of them. And Bank Nifty, the usual destination for domestic institutional money, closed at minus 0.02%. When five thousand crore of domestic buying leaves no mark on the largest domestic sector, either the money went somewhere else or someone else was selling the same names. Cause not established in our briefs.

### What protection cost

India VIX closed the week at 10.68, down 4.64% over the five sessions, having run 11.525, about 11.07, 10.565, 11.0675 and 10.68. It measures what traders are paying for insurance against a sharp index move over the next month, so a low number is a statement about complacency rather than about safety. **It ended the week 1.04% above its own 52-week low, three days before a growth release with a forecast spread of more than a full percentage point on it.** Low volatility is not a forecast of calm. It is what protection costs when almost nobody is buying any.

## 4. Earnings: the week that was not one

**Six Indian companies actually reported results this week, and all six filed on Tuesday and Wednesday.** Not one of them is a company we can price. Not one price move in any of the week's seven briefs is attributed to a result. The one large earnings event that did move Indian shares was reported in another country.

### A company being asked a question is not a company answering one

Our own scan flagged KEI Industries and Gallantt Ispat as reporters on Friday. Both filings were the exact opposite of a result: the exchange asking each company for a clarification on its June-quarter numbers, reply awaited, with no figures attached to either. Reading the underlying feed, that mislabel was five times larger than the two names we caught. **The exchange broadcast ten clarification requests in a single block between 12:08 and 12:21 on Friday, under thirteen minutes, every one of them for the quarter ended 30 June 2026**: Amir Chand Jagdish Kumar Exports, KEI Industries, BEML Land Assets, Jyoti Structures, Atcom Technologies, Gallantt Ispat, Glottis, Royal Orchid Hotels, Confidence Petroleum India and Kriti Industries.

Regulation 33 is the listing rule that sets what a company must file with its quarterly accounts and by when. A clarification request under it means the exchange thinks something is missing or unclear. Ten of them inside thirteen minutes is one compliance sweep by the exchange, not ten separate company events.

The point generalises, and it is worth carrying. **The file on this desk named `financial_results.csv` contained no financial results at all this week.** All seventeen of its rows are either the exchange asking for a clarification, thirteen of them, or a company replying to one, four of them. One of those replies concerned the quarter that ended 31 March 2021, five years ago.

### The six, and why the count elsewhere says fifteen

Our own weekly data file counts seventeen filings from fifteen companies. Reading each row, the fifteen split into three classes: companies reporting for the first time, the same result arriving a second time in a different wrapper, and things that are not results at all. Monday's single "reporter" is a newspaper advertisement of results already published. Thursday's seven are all machine-readable re-filings, press releases, call recordings, a revision of results approved on 22 August, and one copy of a board meeting held on 11 August, sixteen days earlier.

**The six that genuinely reported.** On Tuesday: Ardee Industries at 14:35, MV Electrosystems at 16:33, Vineet Laboratories at 19:35 and again at 19:52, and State Trading Corporation of India at 21:51. On Wednesday: Simbhaoli Sugars at 18:00 and Juniper Green Energy at 19:53.

Vineet Laboratories is a small finding on its own. It filed two separate sets of results seventeen minutes apart, the June 2026 quarter and then **the full year ended 31 March 2026**, an annual account published five months after the year finished. Why is not established anywhere in our briefs.

### Why none of it moved anything, and the reason is mechanical

The desk prices 707 Indian shares. **Not one of the fifteen names on the roster is among them.** The only two symbols in this week's entire earnings conversation that we can price are KEI Industries and Gallantt Ispat, and those are the two that were not reporters at all. Their non-reaction is measurable precisely because they are in the universe: KEI rose 0.61% and Gallantt 0.17% on Friday, against an index up 0.35%.

So the honest statement is not that we failed to explain a results reaction. **There was no reaction to explain.** This was a week of very small companies filing very late, and almost nobody was looking.

Two further points a reader should have. **Only one of the six filings landed while the market was open.** Continuous trading ends at 15:15, and Ardee Industries filed at 14:35. That is the single result in the entire week that could have moved its own share on its own day, and it arrived on Tuesday, the session whose pipeline died. **Four of the six genuine reporters filed on Tuesday, and a fifth and sixth on Wednesday evening, so two-thirds of the week's real earnings news landed on the one day we published nothing, and the rest on a day the public feed did not cover either.** We hold every one of those filings. We published no same-day read on any of them.

And we do not hold their numbers. The revenue and profit figures for all six sit inside PDF attachments to those filings, and nothing in our chain parsed them. Growth against what, margin against what: for all six, cause not established in our briefs.

### The results still moving Indian shares this week were reported before it

Positives first.

**LT Foods rose 7.34% to ₹459 on Monday on a competitor's numbers, not its own.** KRBL's June quarter showed basmati realisations, meaning the average price a tonne of rice actually fetched, holding up at about 20% higher than a year earlier despite West Asian shipping disruption, with management guiding export volumes to recover from the September quarter. LT Foods finished the week up 10.30%. The give-back is worth naming honestly: the share touched ₹482.85 during Monday and closed at ₹459, handing back roughly half the move. There is also a tension inside it. The rice read and the oil read price opposite outcomes of the same event, because the rice case depends on West Asian shipping normalising while the week's oil fall was itself a bet on the same thing.

**A two-week-old result was still doing the work on Friday.** The exchange asked NMDC Steel why its trading volume had spiked. The company replied before Friday's open that nothing new had happened, pointing back to results filed on 14 August and a credit-rating upgrade. The share then rose another 8.96%, taking it to plus 23.20% over the week. A company formally stating it has no fresh news, into a run like that, is worth more than a guess at a cause.

Now the negatives.

**KPI Green Energy's June quarter shows the business and the balance sheet moving in opposite directions.** Operating profit rose 19% and the operating margin, the share of every hundred rupees of sales kept as profit after running costs, improved by 1.3 percentage points. Net profit still fell 15%, because finance costs, the interest the company pays on what it has borrowed, more than doubled at plus 109%. One quarter is not a pattern; the test is whether it persists into the September quarter.

**IRCTC's June quarter is a mix problem, and our two readings of it do not agree.** Revenue grew 18.1% to ₹1,370 crore while operating profit fell 2.77% and the operating margin dropped by 5.1 percentage points, because the ticketing business, which earns margins above 80%, grew 0.5% while catering grew 33.8%. Growing fast in the low-margin half and standing still in the high-margin half will do that to a profit line even when sales rise by nearly a fifth. A second reading later in the week put the fall at 6.1 percentage points to 28.2%, from a single aggregator with no second source obtained. **Both describe the June quarter's margin falling year on year, and 5.1 and 6.1 percentage points are not the same number.** They may be different measures rather than a contradiction, since operating margin and EBITDA margin are not the same line, but nothing in our chain reconciles them. The 5.1 figure carries the stronger provenance and is the one used here.

**Garware Hi-Tech Films carries an open question rather than a result:** management guides its margin about 5 percentage points below the quarter it has just delivered, meaning the company itself expects the next quarter to be materially less profitable than the one reported.

**And one earnings-adjacent event with no earnings in it.** After Hexaware's AI Day presentation on Friday 21 August, six houses published price views on identical disclosed facts: CLSA at ₹730, Kotak ₹660, IIFL ₹620, Axis ₹610, HSBC ₹575 at Hold and Jefferies ₹500 at Underperform. That is quoted with attribution, and it is not our view. The share fell 2.16% on the day of the presentation and rose 5.64% on Monday, the first session after the notes landed. A spread that wide published the same afternoon off the same slides is the market admitting it has no settled method for pricing an "AI-powered revenue" claim, and that claim, covering about half of revenue, is the chief executive's own and is defined nowhere. A presentation is not a result and should not be read as one.

### The season's missing half

Results are only half of reporting. The call, where management takes questions, is the other half, and this week it is largely absent. **Apar Industries' June-quarter call transcript was 34 days pending by Thursday**, 20 days past its own timeout, and the company filed on both Wednesday and Thursday without filing that. **Varun Beverages' June-quarter transcript is 28 days pending**, which has a direct consequence: the company approved a new subsidiary to enter ready-to-drink alcoholic beverages this week, and nobody has been able to ask whether its bottling agreements permit the parent to build one. Five specific quantified commitments made on Transrail Lighting's 3 February 2026 call are all past their own check dates, including full-year order intake of ₹9,500 to ₹10,000 crore and an operating margin held at 11.5 to 12%, and none can be tested because the transcripts that would settle them cannot be read.

Against that, the genuinely positive process finding of the week. **Several of the smallest companies on the exchange ran a better disclosure calendar than several much larger ones.** Juniper Green filed a press release, an investor presentation, a call recording and a newspaper advertisement around its result, and had pre-announced the call. Ardee and MV Electrosystems each filed presentations and call material. Lohia Corp filed the transcript of its 20 August call on Thursday, and Patel Retail filed a call recording, an investor presentation and its quarterly transcript inside the same week.

## 5. The sectors, and why they moved

### Read this before the table

**For all five sessions of this week, the exchange's own sector indices were almost entirely unreadable on our feed.** Nine of the eleven sector indices produced no usable daily move on Monday, and the same nine were dark again on Wednesday, Thursday and Friday. On several days four more failed to arrive at all, including Consumer Durables, Healthcare, Oil and Gas and Private Bank. **Only Nifty IT and Nifty Pharma produced a live reading on any day this week.**

That has two consequences, and both belong to the reader rather than to us.

**First, our own weekly sector figures were wrong and we caught them.** The automated weekly file this brief is built from published a sector table showing seven sectors up and three down. Recomputing every sector by hand from the closing prices we hold for 21 August and 28 August, **nine of its eleven rows are wrong, including four where the sign is reversed.** Auto, Media, Consumption and PSU Bank were all reported higher on the week and all four actually fell. The true count is three sectors up and eight down. This is the third consecutive week the same block has failed in the same way, and this week's row was published without the correction note the previous week carried.

**Second, everything below that is not IT or Pharma is our own reconstruction, and it is labelled as one.** Where the index was missing, we built sector direction from the individual shares instead: take every company in that sector we can price, find the middle one, and report that. It answers "what did the typical share in this sector do," which is not the same question as "what did the sector index do," because an index weights by company size and a median treats every member alike. We tested the substitute against the two live indices every session. ## 6. Three things worth understanding properly

These are in a deliberate order: what the number means, then when the reaction happens, then what to do when neither answers the question.

### One. An index is a weighted average, not a vote

This is the single most useful idea available from this week, because the week broke on it four times.

**The Nifty 50 is not a count of how many of its fifty companies rose.** It is an average in which every company is weighted by its size, so a very large company moves the number far more than a small one does, and the biggest handful can outvote everybody else. That is why an index and its own member companies can point in different directions on the same afternoon. The index is answering "how much money moved." Breadth is answering "how many companies moved." Those are different questions, and this week they gave different answers four times out of five.

The same arithmetic runs one level up. A whole sector can be heavy enough inside an index to carry it alone.

**Friday is the clean demonstration, because there is nothing to argue about.** Nifty IT rose 3.51% and all nine of its priced constituents rose, and on that sector's weight it accounts for effectively the entire 0.35% gain in the Nifty 50. Now remove those nine: inside the Nifty 50, the remaining shares were 18 up, 18 down, one flat, and the typical one moved 0.00%. Across the whole market, 343 rose and 361 fell. So the honest sentence for Friday is not that the market rose. It is that **one sector rose and was heavy enough to make the scoreboard green while slightly more shares fell than rose.**

**Thursday is the same demonstration one level down, at a single company.** HDFC Bank fell 2.23%, and on roughly 13% of the index that one share is about 0.29 percentage points of a 0.48% fall, well over half of it. Its weight in the Sensex is heavier, which is most of why the Sensex fell 0.70% on the same afternoon. And the part that makes this worth teaching rather than merely reporting: the banks did not sell off. Kotak rose 1.80%, ICICI 0.91%, Axis 0.08%, and the private-bank group's typical share fell 0.34%, better than the market. A reader who saw "banks drag index lower" and concluded "banks are in trouble" would have drawn precisely the wrong conclusion from a true headline. One share did it.

**Tuesday and Wednesday prove it is not a one-off.** Tuesday the index rose 0.48% while 386 shares fell. Wednesday it fell 0.52% while 383 rose. Two consecutive sessions where the index and the typical share pointed opposite ways, in opposite directions. Wednesday also gives the clearest single view of the weighting at work, and it reads as one line: Nifty 50 constituents down 0.66%, Nifty 100 down 0.43%, Midcap 50 down 0.17%, Smallcap 250 up 0.59%. The bigger the company, the worse the day.

**Thursday is also the exception, and it belongs here rather than hidden.** The index and the market agreed on direction, so the index is not always lying. What it still got wrong was depth: the Nifty 50's own typical constituent fell just 0.27%, shallower than the market, while the large-company index fell harder than the market. Large companies as a group had a milder day than the market, and the large-company index still fell more. The whole difference is weight.

The version a friend can be told: *the Nifty counts big companies much more than small ones, so on Friday nine technology shares turned the whole index green even though more shares fell than rose, and on Thursday one bank caused over half the index's fall while most other banks went up.*

### Two. A market can only react when it is open, and the clock is not the one you assume

**Indian continuous trading ends at 15:15, and the closing auction, the short final window in which the official closing price of every share is set, ends at 15:35.** Nothing published after that can have moved that day's closing price. A company filing at 18:33 on a Thursday evening is not Thursday's news. It is Friday's.

The rule runs both ways, and the reverse direction is the one that surprises people. A document filed on a Friday night or over a weekend does not disappear. It queues, and Monday is the first session that can price it. So a Monday move can be caused by something that happened three days earlier.

Then there is the second clock, the one that matters for Indian technology shares. American markets open after India has closed and their session ends around 01:30 India time. **So the American trading day India reacts to is always the previous night's, and India's 09:15 open is its first chance to price it.**

**The strongest proof of this in the week is a mistake we made and corrected.** Nvidia reported after the American close on Wednesday: revenue of US$96.2 billion, up 106% on the year, with guidance, meaning the company's own forecast for the coming quarter, of US$108 billion against roughly US$104.2 billion expected. Indian traders had all of that before Thursday's 09:15 open.

On Thursday, India did nothing with it. Nifty IT fell 0.32%, with 13 of 20 constituents lower, while Korea's KOSPI rose 1.53%, Shanghai 1.13% and Taiwan 0.31%. **Thursday's brief read that as India sitting out the biggest company result in the world**, and offered a reason why the chip-to-services link might be one-sided, labelling that reasoning rather than a sourced finding.

On Friday the picture inverted and we said plainly what had been wrong. The American session that actually voted on those results closed at 01:30 India time on Friday morning: Nasdaq up 1.57%, S&P 500 up 0.72%, Nvidia up 8.7%, technology the only S&P 500 sector to advance. India opened against that close and Nifty IT gained 3.51%. **There was never a lag. We measured India against a press release, when the clock Indian technology shares track is the American close.**

**The reverse direction, from Monday, three examples in one session.** Vishal Mega Mart re-appointed its founder chief executive for five years, in a filing sent at 20:14 on Friday 21 August, after the close; the share rose 9.74% on Monday. Urban Company disclosed a Delhi High Court undertaking by Kent RO to withdraw disparaging advertising, filed at 14:08 on Sunday 23 August; the share rose 6.46% on Monday. Welspun Corp filed a US$1.8 billion pipe order, about ₹17,200 crore, at 22:57 on Thursday 20 August, and the following Friday was the first session that could price it.

**And the same rule ruling a cause out, which is the harder half.** The American sanctions announcement on Iran that much of Monday's coverage attached to Monday's Indian session landed between roughly 17:30 and 19:00 India time, after the market had shut. Monday traded the anticipation, pre-announced on Sunday. It did not trade the contents.

**One honest qualifier on Friday's rally, because it is easy to over-read.** Four things say the move was imported price movement rather than a change in Indian earnings. Participation was uniform, including Wipro at plus 2.58% and Mphasis at plus 1.72%, companies with no artificial-intelligence story at all. The leaders were mid-sized, higher-swing names rather than the largest ones, and Infosys, the name most people would assume led, rose 2.99% and lagged its own sector. The rupee firmed 0.07%, which is a small headwind for companies that earn in dollars and spend in rupees, not a tailwind. And not one brokerage published an Indian software sector note all day, which is not what research desks do when they believe a sector's earnings power has changed. Thursday's American session was a software rally as much as a chip one, with Salesforce up 22% and Okta up 28%, and Indian services companies sell into enterprise software budgets rather than into silicon.

The usable rule a reader can carry away: **before believing any sentence of the form "shares fell because of X", check whether X happened while the market was open.**

### Three. "Nobody established why" is a finding, and this week it has a name and a price

The phrase "cause not established" appears throughout this brief. A reader could reasonably conclude one of two things from that: either the desk did not try, or markets are random. Both are wrong, and the reason is specific.

Companies must tell the exchange about anything price-sensitive, and those filings are timestamped, so a filing can be matched to a move. But **not everything that moves a share is a filing.** The largest single category that is not is a **block deal**: a big pre-arranged trade in which one holder sells a large parcel of shares directly to a buyer, off the ordinary order book. It shows up in the exchange's record of large negotiated trades, and it very frequently explains a share that jumps with nothing filed.

**That record has been missing from our data feed for the whole week and beyond**, called out as unavailable for six runs on Monday, nine or more on Thursday, and ten or more by Friday. So when a mid-sized company rises 8% with nothing filed, we can name the shape of the missing answer and cannot read it.

**The clean proof is the one case where the answer was found by hand.** Ather Energy rose 8.09% on Friday to ₹1,616.30, a genuine 52-week closing high, and the cause is fully sourced: **Hero MotoCorp lifted its fully diluted holding from 29.88% to about 32.8% through a block deal of 1,18,80,000 shares at ₹1,480, worth ₹1,758 crore, completing on 3 September.** That is exactly the instrument described above, and the only reason we have it is that somebody went and searched for it, because our own feed had been blank for ten runs.

Two things worth glossing in the same breath. **This was a secondary transaction**, meaning Hero bought existing shares from existing owners, so no new money entered the company. That is a different thing from a company raising funds. A separate share issue, of about ₹1,200 crore in total, was allotted on 25 August, and that one was a genuine fundraise. The two are different events with different dates and should not be run together.

**So the argument writes itself.** On one Friday name the missing record was recovered by hand and it explained an 8% move completely. On the several names where it could not be recovered, the most likely explanation sits in a file nobody could open. "We do not know why" is therefore a statement about a specific missing instrument, not about the market being unknowable.

**The scale of the blank, dated.** On Thursday, nine of the ten biggest risers had no cause we could establish, including INOX India up 13.04% at a 52-week high with no filing on the day or the evening before, and Apar Industries up 4.91% to ₹17,900, by far the biggest company on that list, whose four filings in the window were annual-meeting and dividend housekeeping. On Friday, six shares rose between 6.75% and 11.24% with no cause established; J.G. Chemicals rose 11.24% to a 52-week high having filed nothing at all across 3,716 exchange rows for 26 to 28 August. On Monday, five of the twelve biggest movers had no cause anyone could point to.

**The best corroboration is that the exchange could not establish a cause either.** On Friday the exchange formally asked NMDC Steel why its trading volume had spiked. The company answered that nothing new had happened. The share then rose another 8.96%. When the exchange asks the question and the company says there is no answer, "cause not established" stops being one desk's limitation and becomes a description of the market.

### The discipline that makes a blank honest

An unexplained move is only a finding if the search was real. Two things say it was.

**We name what was checked before writing the blank:** filings, dated broker actions, index and derivatives events, and search, described as five named checks on the Friday entries and a full deep search on Thursday's.

**And we keep catching plausible explanations that turn out to be stale**, which is the trap a reader will personally fall into. Four were caught in one session on Thursday: INOX India's ₹939 crore order aggregate is dated 8 July and moved the share about 5% then; Whirlpool's stake-sale talks collapsed in December 2025; NMDC Steel's credit-rating upgrade is dated 21 August and moved the share about 2% that day; Apar's 77.8% profit jump is dated 18 August, seven sessions old. Two more on Friday: a search result reported an 8% jump in Newgen Software at a price of ₹1,316 when the share actually closed at ₹567.10, so it is an old article re-dated by the search layer, and a ₹110 crore titanium-order story attached to PTC Industries carries a 2025 dateline. On Monday a year-old article about Yatharth Hospital's rally was killed on its date rather than published as a reason.

**And there is a size test a reader can apply without any data at all.** Coromandel International fell 3.48% on Monday, and the only document in the window was a ₹1.24 crore tax order plus a ₹12.35 lakh penalty. That cannot move a ₹1,926 share by 3.5%. Aegis Logistics fell 5.39% on a ₹11,62,128 tax penalty, at a company worth ₹47,371 crore. **If the number in the explanation is thousands of times smaller than the company, it is not the explanation.**

Finally, the week's highest-quality blank, where both numbers are solid and the conclusion still refuses to arrive. Foreign investors sold ₹5,039.80 crore on Friday and domestic institutions bought ₹5,183.93 crore, near-perfectly matched, on a day the index closed up, **and Bank Nifty, the usual destination for domestic institutional money, closed at minus 0.02%.** When five thousand crore of domestic buying leaves no mark on the largest domestic sector, either the money went somewhere else or someone else was selling the same names. Two verified numbers, one honest blank.

### Elsewhere in the corporate week

**Eight shares fell on Friday for a reason that is not a market reason.** When a company pays a dividend it hands out cash, and there is a cut-off date after which a new buyer no longer receives that payment. On the first morning past the cut-off, the share is worth exactly the dividend less than it was the night before, because the cash has left the company. The price on the screen drops and nothing has gone wrong. **The value did not vanish. It moved from the share price into the shareholder's bank account.**

Eight companies went past that cut-off on Friday: Cantabil, Honasa, MCX, Mufti, NBCC, Poly Medicure, Vardhman Textiles and Whirlpool. Seven are confirmed against exchange filings we hold; MCX has no corporate-action record anywhere and is supported rather than confirmed. The arithmetic is what makes this worth reading: Whirlpool's raw fall of 3.32% was really 2.74%, Honasa's 2.93% was really 2.32%, and **Mufti, which a raw price screen shows falling 2.55%, did not move at all.** None of the eight is a market event. And the correction runs both ways: Whirlpool rose 9.00% on Thursday with no cause established, and its ₹5 dividend, worth about 0.6% of the price, was correctly sized and correctly rejected as an explanation. We do not ingest the exchange's corporate-actions record, which is why six of the eight needed archived filings to resolve and why we cannot date the next one in advance.

Lumax Auto Technologies drew 29.14% against a resolution authorising lending to entities in which its directors are interested, in a document where the counterparty, the amount and the pricing are all absent. Sagility drew 61.91% against a director's re-appointment. Ather Energy drew 29.50%. Jay Bharat Maruti drew **100% against the chairman's pay, on 6.11% turnout**, and that turnout must be printed in the same breath as the 100%, because a percentage without its turnout is not information: 28.82% on 98% turnout is the heavier number of the two.

**What makes it a story is what those same investors approved.** Accounts, auditors, dividends and ordinary related-party transactions passed with negligible opposition in the same ballots. At Gland, the institutions casting 45% to 47% against two directors voted 100% for the accounts and the dividend. **This is not a protest against everything. It is a targeted refusal, aimed at the resolutions where the controlling family stands to benefit.** Two controls were run on it. Four of the five landed within thirty hours because a two-working-day filing clock discharged after a common meeting date, so annual meeting season explains the timing and not the content. And Lumax Industries, the sister company under the same controlling group, recorded 8.17%, well below the threshold, which makes the 29.14% a judgement about one resolution rather than about a family.

**Two companies transferred assets to a connected party in one week, and neither document contained a valuation.** Waaree Renewable Technologies told the exchange at 18:29 on Thursday that its board had approved transferring its entire shareholding in a wholly owned subsidiary to Waaree Energies, which is both its holding company and its controlling shareholder. The stated price is ₹1,00,000, at a face value of ₹10 a share, and the pricing basis offered is the subsidiary's contribution to turnover and net worth last year, given as nil or negligible. The subsidiary had just been renamed to Waaree Green Data Centers. The word valuation appears nowhere in the document. Shareholder approval is scheduled for 23 September and completion is due by 31 October. Separately, Aegis Logistics sold a ₹525 crore ammonia terminal to its own step-down subsidiary fourteen days after commissioning it, with no external valuation in the disclosure and a one-sentence definitional claim that the shareholder-approval provision does not apply because the terminal is not an "undertaking".

**Both filings are compliant on their face and both declared the connected-party status openly.** Neither is alleged to break a rule and neither is described here as wrongdoing. The finding is a specific thing absent from two documents in five sessions, which is a statement about the documents. **When both sides of a sale sit inside the same group, no market sets the price, so the valuation is the only evidence an outside reader has, and in these two it is not there.** The market did nothing with either: Waaree Renewable closed up 0.43% through a full session with the document public.

**And one competitive event with a date attached.** Ola Electric launched the S1Z scooter at ₹79,999 during Friday's session, with the S1Z Plus at ₹99,999 and deliveries from December 2026, on its first range built with its own lithium-iron-phosphate cell. That lands roughly 20% to 35% below the ₹1.0 lakh to ₹1.25 lakh bracket the market expected a rival launch to occupy. **It reads as a price war rather than a product cycle.** The range figures quoted around it are the manufacturer's own test-cycle claims and are not repeated here as facts.

## 8. What to watch next week

### Monday 31 August is four things landing in one session

**A growth number before the open, an index reshuffle transacting in the closing auction, a foreign-currency funding window shutting, and the market's first chance to price a product event held over the weekend.** A fifth item, a 15-day auction to absorb surplus bank cash, sits in the same day.

That is worth saying in advance because it is much harder to say afterwards. **When Monday's number appears, nobody will be able to say cleanly which of the four caused it, and anyone who tells you they can is guessing.**

**India's April to June national accounts** are scheduled for Monday 31 August. This is the single number that says how fast the economy actually grew in the three months to June, against 7.8% in the March quarter. **Carry the forecasts as a spread, because there is no consensus to collapse them into.** India Ratings is at 6.9%, the Reserve Bank at 7.0%, Bank of Baroda at 7.0 to 7.2%, a 58-economist poll median near 7.1%, CareEdge at 7.3%, and SBI Research alone at 8.0%. **The cluster between 6.9% and 7.3% would be a four-quarter low. The 8.0% outlier would be an acceleration. Those are not the same story and no published median reconciles them.** The stated causes behind the low cluster are a delayed monsoon onset and softer services.

**And there is a trap inside the release itself.** The same publication switches the industrial-production and price indices to a revised 2022-23 base year, so some series will not be directly comparable with what came before. The yardstick changes in the same document as the measurement. **A number that looks like a jump or a drop against last quarter may be partly the new yardstick.**

**The MSCI August review transacts at Monday's close**, with the new composition applying from 1 September. Adani Energy Solutions, Billionbrains Garage Ventures, Laurus Labs and Lenskart Solutions join; Astral, Balkrishna Industries and SBI Cards and Payment Services leave. **Funds whose whole job is to hold exactly what the index holds do not get an opinion.** When the index changes they must buy the joiners and sell the leavers, and they do it in the closing auction because that is the price the index will use. That is mechanical buying and selling by people with no view, which is why it can move a share on a day when nothing about the company changed.

**We are printing no single figure for how much money that moves, and the reason is instructive.** Four estimates are circulating and they measure different things. Roughly US$600 million covers only the joining shares; US$2.3 to 3.2 billion covers the whole review; one desk's figure of over US$1.5 billion carries no scope at all. And one house's per-stock estimates sum to about US$1.5 billion for the four additions alone, which makes the "roughly US$600 million" line look like one share's inflow rather than four. **Every one of those estimates is dated about 13 August and none has been refreshed.** They are not a range of the same quantity. They are different quantities that got compared.

**The Reserve Bank's concessional foreign-currency deposit window shuts on Monday, and it is three dates rather than one.** These are dollar accounts that Indians living abroad hold with Indian banks. The central bank ran a temporary scheme letting banks swap those dollars into rupees on favourable terms, which made chasing the deposits attractive. It worked fast enough that the deadline was pulled forward from 30 September on 14 August. **New deposits stop on 31 August, swaps against already-eligible deposits run to 11 September, and the related borrowing scheme runs to 31 December. The scheme does not end on Monday. One of its three legs does.**

Why it matters. India's foreign-exchange reserves have risen four weeks running, the latest by US$12.42 billion to US$729.33 billion. **That build is largely why reserves rose about US$34.5 billion in three weeks while the rupee sat near three-week lows: swap-funded, not strength.** Monday is the day that tap stops. When it does, the marginal dollar for bank funding gets more expensive, and that reaches lenders before it reaches anything else.

### The imported input Monday has to absorb

**The American central bank's Jackson Hole keynote landed around 19:30 India time on Friday, after our market had closed, so it explains nothing in this brief.** It is Monday's input.

Kevin Warsh, in his first keynote as Chair, said inflation is running above the 2% target and that the Fed's predominant focus should be on prices; that summer readings came in better than expected but do not tell him the underlying trend has meaningfully improved; and that the Fed must be confident inflation is moving toward its objective clearly and at sufficient speed, "otherwise, we have work to do." **He declined to commit to any forward guidance.** American inflation is 3.4% and the 30-year American government bond yield is near its highest since 2007.

**Why an Indian reader should care.** Markets here have been positioned for American rates to come down. A Fed chair saying he may have to put them up is the opposite setting. India does not set global rates and imports the consequences anyway, through the rupee and through what foreign investors do with Indian bonds. **Higher American rates make American assets more attractive relative to Indian ones.** We are printing no probability figure for a September rate rise: the number in circulation could not be checked at source and a pre-speech reading of it was very different.

### The rest of the calendar

- **Tuesday 1 September:** monthly vehicle sales, the first read on whether the electric two-wheeler price war is shifting anything, landing two days after a new sub-₹1.25 lakh scooter reveal and three days after a two-wheeler maker raised its stake in an electric-scooter maker. Also the weekly Nifty settlement, and a sugar stockholding limit for bulk buyers taking effect and running to 30 November.
- **Thursday 3 September:** the Hero MotoCorp purchase of Ather shares completes. A secondary purchase from an existing shareholder; no new capital enters the company.
- **Friday 4 September:** the Reserve Bank's weekly statistical release, and whether a fifth consecutive reserve build arrives. This edition covers the week in which the deposit window shut, so it is the first read on what that window raised. **A standing caution travels with the number: a rising reserve pile is not evidence of the central bank defending the rupee. That would show as a fall.**
- **Monday 7 September:** a scheduled change to the exchange's auction sessions takes effect. **Our own wording and our outward check disagree about what changes on that date**, and neither outward source clears our source bar, so we are naming the date and not the content until the exchange's own circular settles it.
- **The comment window on the Reserve Bank's draft rules for non-bank lenders closed on Friday 28 August.** Say the word draft again. A closed comment window is the end of consultation, not the start of obligation. **Nothing was enacted and nothing binds anybody.** That is exactly the moment a reader starts assuming a rule exists. Separately, seven Reserve Bank supervisory actions landed on non-bank lenders and co-operative banks in the six business days ending on that deadline, six fines and one set of Directions restricting what a bank may do. **That timing is an observation, not a claim of cause.**

### One event we deliberately cannot report

**Ather Energy's Community Day was scheduled for Saturday 29 August, after every brief in this week was written, with a new scooter expected.** As of this writing **no dated report of the outcome exists in anything we can reach.** Every retrievable source is pre-event copy written in the future tense, two of them headlined "to launch tomorrow", and the manufacturer's own model page says the scooter has not been launched and no official price has been announced. **The sources disagree with each other on the expected price, which is itself the proof that no official price exists.**

**A search summary will tell you confidently that it launched, at a price. That is pre-event copy rewritten in the past tense.** We do not know the price, the specification or the reception, and Monday 31 August is the first Indian session that can price any of it. Friday's Ather move was the block deal, not the scooter.

**And one observation about the price of insurance, which is not a forecast.** India VIX closed at 10.68 on Friday, 1.04% above its own 52-week low, going into a weekend containing a growth release, an index rebalance, a policy deadline and a product event. The market is charging close to nothing to insure against a surprise, into a weekend with four of them in it.

## 9. What we could not explain, and where we got it wrong

Last week's brief published a list of ten things to watch and promised that a watch list published in a weekly gets graded in the next weekly. This is the first grade under that rule, so the rule is on trial as much as the list.

### What worked

**Four items fired and were covered properly.** The monthly derivatives settlement showed up exactly as described in advance, an index rising on falling breadth on Tuesday and reversing on Wednesday. The Jio Financial shareholder meeting fired, and the reason for watching held: over four sessions the share moved minus 0.82%, minus 0.88%, minus 1.20% and plus 0.13%, which is a market pricing a ₹7,200 crore related-party authorisation, worth about twice a year's revenue, at approximately nothing. The Jackson Hole slot was pinned in advance, correctly dated, and the speech was confirmed at the central bank's own page. The comment deadline on the draft non-bank lender rules fired, and it turned up something better than the item anticipated: seven supervisory actions on the same class of lender in the six business days ending on that deadline.

**And a correction we issued last week held all week.** We had written that the index review is effective 1 September, and corrected that the date that matters is the close on Monday 31 August. Every brief that carried it this week stated it that way.

### The miss, and it is the one that matters

**Last week's brief flagged three companies to watch on Monday: Lloyds Luxuries, Manipal Hospitals and Winsome Interiors.** They had filed results on Friday 21 August, we published no brief that day, and Monday was the first session that could price them. The brief added a sentence which now convicts it: **it said it was flagging these precisely because they were "the same shape as the miss above."**

**None of the three appears anywhere in any of this week's seven briefs.** A full-text search of all of them returns zero hits for each name.

So the sequence is this. We identified a specific failure mode, which is pointing readers at named reactions and then never reporting them. We named it. We wrote a new item in exactly that shape. We warned in writing that it was the same shape. **And then we reproduced it exactly.** This is the second consecutive weekly in which a pointed reaction went unreported, and the first in which it was pre-flagged. **Naming a failure was not a control against it.** The check is three ticker lookups in a file this desk builds every evening anyway.

**Two more items were quietly dropped and never graded.** A reported American trade delegation due in New Delhi, carried with the correct warning that it was single-sourced and unconfirmed, appears in no brief after the day it was raised. Nobody went back. That is the same shape again: the failure is not flagging something unverified, it is flagging something unverified and never returning to it. **And one item was half-graded.** We told readers this was the first monthly settlement since the regulator published evidence that retail participation in derivatives is shrinking, and then published nothing about whether that showed up. Half an item graded is not an item graded.

### An error in last week's brief that is still in print

**Last week's brief said a ₹36,000 crore figure circulating against the Jio Financial vote "traces to a May 2024 vote at a different company." That is wrong.** It traces to a May 2024 postal ballot at a **subsidiary in the same group**, concerning a different transaction in a different year.

So this is a correction of a correction, and the wrong version is the one that is published. The number in circulation is still wrong, and the figure to hold is still the ₹7,200 crore confirmed from the primary meeting notice. **But our stated reason for rejecting it was itself inaccurate.** The figure surfaced a third time in search results during this week, dressed with extra detail about entity and years, which is why the trace was worth doing. **Added specificity is not added evidence.**

### Nine self-corrections in one week, across four briefs

We corrected ourselves more often than we were corrected, which is the right direction, and we corrected one thing three times in three days, which is not.

**The one to lead with is the clock.** Thursday's brief led on India having sat out the biggest company result in the world. Friday's brief corrected it in its own lead paragraph rather than a footnote, and did not disown the original reporting: **the reporting was right and the framing was wrong. There was never a lag. We measured against a press release, when the clock Indian technology shares track is the American close.** That is the model for how a correction should be handled, and the general rule it establishes is the same rule that governs Jackson Hole.

**A second was reversed cleanly.** Monday's public brief said the central bank was draining cash for longer, and that its absorption of surplus rupees was being termed out rather than rolled. Read directly off the central bank's own release index, the sequence of auctions ran overnight, three days, seven days, then two days on 25 August, then back to overnight. **The tenor reversed. We are not dropping that line quietly and we are not repeating it.** What is still not known is how much is being absorbed, because the individual result pages were not opened.

**And a third was itself withdrawn the next day, which should read as low confidence rather than as a fix.** A reader following the commodity question across this week was given three different reasons in three days. On Tuesday, the crude move was real and not an artefact. On Thursday, the move was unpublishable because the two snapshots being compared were stamped nineteen hours and fifty-two minutes apart. On Friday, that explanation was withdrawn, on the ground that moving the fetch later would not have fixed it, and replaced with a contract-roll fault breaking eighteen of nineteen commodity series. **Each step was an improvement and each was labelled. The net effect is that our confidence in our own commodity data this week should read as low.** The operational consequence is that we published no single-day change for any commodity from Wednesday onward, and our two Brent sources sat about a dollar apart on the same day. **The weekly direction and the two endpoint levels are solid. Treat the roughly 6% figure as approximate.**

**Three further corrections were issued on one night**: a self-manufactured claim that the growth forecasts were irreconcilable, which they are not; a framing that a 61.91% institutional dissent at Sagility met a governance blackout, which was over-read from a search failure, so keep the number and retire the framing; and the crude item above. **Three more the next night**: a risk-off hypothesis refuted on intra-day breadth and restored on closing breadth, with the closing numbers governing; a continuity sweep that had been double-counting shares, 708 unique symbols and three failures rather than 1,410 and six; and the commodity reason later withdrawn.
