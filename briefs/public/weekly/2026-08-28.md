## 1. The week in plain English

The Nifty 50 finished the week at 24,175.65, down 0.31% across five sessions. The Nifty 500, the broadest index we track and the one that comes nearest to describing the whole listed market, finished down 0.01%. After a full week of trading, that is dead flat.

It was not a quiet week. It was a round trip. The index fell on Monday, rose on Tuesday, fell on Wednesday, fell harder on Thursday and recovered part of it on Friday: 24,219.05, then 24,334.55, then 24,207.75, then 24,090.85, then 24,175.65. Tuesday's close was the week's high, Thursday's was its low, and Friday gave back a little under half of the fall. The Nifty 500's best and worst closes of the week sit 129 points apart, and the week's net change is under two points.

Three separate forces pushed in three separate directions and roughly cancelled. None of them had anything to do with the other two.

**Technology moved on an American clock.** Nifty IT, the index of the country's software services companies, fell 1.47% on Wednesday after the United States paused visa appointments at its embassies and consulates worldwide while it re-screens consular officers, which constrains how quickly Indian firms can move staff to client sites. It then fell a further 0.32% on Thursday, and rose 3.51% on Friday alone after an overnight American technology rally. Over the week it gained 2.45%, the second-best sector reading we hold.

**Crude oil fell on every single session.** Brent, the global benchmark grade of oil against which India prices most of what it imports, closed the week around US$88.36 against US$94.19 the previous Friday, a fall of roughly 6%. The cause was diplomacy rather than demand: Iran and Oman resumed talks over the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow sea passage through which the bulk of India's crude arrives, discussing a temporary navigational corridor and agreeing to work on clearing mines. Cheaper oil is a tailwind for a country that imports about 85% of what it refines.

**And the domestic-demand complex sold off quietly all week.** Consumer staples, meaning the everyday products Indian households buy from Indian companies, fell on almost every session we can measure. Nifty FMCG lost 1.46% on the week, Nifty Consumption 0.95% and Nifty Auto 0.94%. No headline named any of them. Those three falls are the largest weekly sector moves in the market and they went unreported everywhere, including in our own daily briefs on most days.

Now the number that actually describes the week. **Across roughly 704 shares we can price reliably, 316 rose over the week and 388 fell.** So the typical Indian share lost money in a week the headline called flat, and the Nifty Smallcap 250 index still finished at a 52-week high on Friday. Day by day it is starker: **on three of the five sessions the index moved one way and the typical share moved the other**, and on a fourth the index understated the fall by nearly half. Exactly one session, Thursday, had a headline that honestly described what happened to the average shareholder.

That gap is the week. Anyone who read this week off the index read almost none of it.

## 2. How each day moved, and why

Two words before the table. **Breadth** is nothing more complicated than the count of shares that rose against the count that fell, out of the roughly 705 companies we can price reliably each day. The **median share** is the one exactly in the middle of that list, so it describes the typical company's day rather than the biggest company's day. An index cannot tell you either, because it weights every company by size.

| Session | Nifty 50 | Rose | Fell | Median share | Did the index describe the market? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon 24 Aug | 24,219.05, **−0.14%** | 302 | 400 | −0.24% | Direction yes, scale no |
| Tue 25 Aug | 24,334.55, **+0.48%** | 320 | 386 | −0.12% | **No, opposite** |
| Wed 26 Aug | 24,207.75, **−0.52%** | 383 | 322 | +0.14% | **No, opposite** |
| Thu 27 Aug | 24,090.85, **−0.48%** | 253 | 449 | −0.46% | **Yes, cleanly** |
| Fri 28 Aug | 24,175.65, **+0.35%** | 343 | 361 | −0.04% | **No, opposite** |

### Monday: a flat index sitting on a falling market

The Nifty lost 32.95 points, which on 24,219 is 0.14% and is not worth an explanation in itself. Underneath it, 400 shares fell against 302 that rose, the second consecutive session of a falling market under a flat headline. The Sensex fell 0.22%, Bank Nifty 0.41% and the Smallcap 250 index 0.21%.

The day's named cause was geopolitical and it pointed the wrong way. The United States Treasury Secretary announced what he called the single greatest financial offensive ever mounted against Iran, and the part that reaches India is that Washington says it will sanction any country that keeps trading with Iran. Iran's Persian Gulf Strait Authority replied that vessels breaching its rules face fines, seizure or confiscation, and both sides let the ceasefire lapse. And yet Brent crude fell about 1.8% on the day to roughly US$92.68, after a week in which it had risen more than 5%.

A threat to oil supply that leaves oil cheaper has not been priced as a threat to oil supply. What the market appears to have priced instead is the payments and trade channel, meaning the risk to how India settles and routes purchases, rather than the size of the import bill. It is worth keeping the scale in view: a 0.14% close is a very small reaction for a country that imports 85% of its crude through that waterway.

One further point on Monday, because it is the rule that runs through the whole week. The American sanctions announcement itself landed between roughly 17:30 and 19:00 India time, after the Indian market had already shut. Monday traded the anticipation, which had been pre-announced on Sunday. It did not trade the contents.

### Tuesday: the index rose and most shares fell

The Nifty rose 0.48% to 24,334.55, the week's high, while 386 shares fell and 320 rose. This is the cleanest single explanation of the week and it has nothing to do with sentiment.

Tuesday was the National Stock Exchange's monthly derivatives expiry, the day on which the month's futures and options contracts settle and positions in them have to be closed or rolled forward. The date was checked against the calendar rather than assumed: 1 August was a Saturday, which puts the month's Tuesdays on the 4th, 11th, 18th and 25th, and the convention is the last Tuesday. Settlement demand concentrates in a narrow band of very large, heavily traded companies, which is exactly the band an index weights most. So expiry propped up the index through a handful of large companies while the rest of the market did not participate.

India VIX, the index measuring what traders are paying for protection against a sharp market move over the coming month, fell 3.95% on a day the index rose. That is not the usual pairing.

Two events from Tuesday belong to Wednesday, not to Tuesday. A United States sanctions expansion on Iran was announced without being imposed, with no countries named, no effective date and time given to comply, and crude fell to a one-week low. And Varun Beverages' board approved, at 16:21 after the close, a wholly owned Indian subsidiary to enter ready-to-drink alcoholic beverages plus a Tunisia joint venture. A filing after the close is the next session's cause, never the current one.

### Wednesday: the index fell and two-thirds of shares rose

The Nifty fell 0.52% and 383 shares rose against 322 that fell. The median share gained 0.14%. This is the exact reverse of Tuesday, on consecutive days.

The dated cause behind the weakness at the top was real and narrow. The United States has paused visa appointments at its embassies and consulates worldwide while it re-screens and retrains consular officers, with no announced date for normal availability to resume. That constrains the throughput of Indian technology firms who move staff onto client sites, and it is the one dated cause behind Nifty IT falling 1.47%.

But check that against the shares underneath, because the index badly overstates it. Across the IT index's 34 constituents the median share fell only 0.39%, twelve of them rose, and Cyient gained 7.01%. Five very large companies were effectively the whole index move. The gap between the sector's best and worst performer that day was 12.41 percentage points, the widest in the market, and that is the opposite of what a sector-wide shock looks like. One related caution: the six-figure H-1B visa fee circulating alongside this story is a proposal, not a rule in force.

The genuinely broad falls on Wednesday were the ones nobody named. Consumption fell 0.82% with six of 27 names higher, and FMCG fell 0.70% with six of 22 higher. Against them, metals rose 1.75% with sixteen of eighteen higher, pharma 0.89% with seventeen of 24 higher, and public-sector banks 0.62% with all thirteen higher.

The size gradient reads as one line: Nifty 50 constituents down 0.66%, Nifty 100 down 0.43%, Midcap 50 down 0.17%, Smallcap 250 up 0.59%. The bigger the company, the worse the day. Seven of the twelve biggest risers closed at 52-week highs.

Crude fell again, on Iran and Oman resuming talks over the Strait of Hormuz, discussing a temporary navigational corridor and agreeing to work on clearing mines, with Oman's foreign minister hopeful of an announcement soon and a permanent route negotiated within 30 to 60 days. Two cautions travel with that. A corridor has been discussed, not announced. And a political claim that all mines are already cleared sits in the same news cycle as an agreement to clear them, and both cannot be true.

India VIX fell 4.65% to 10.565, near its 52-week low, through a session that opened with an unresolved Hormuz blockage and a visa pause on the country's largest export-services sector. The option market had stopped pricing either as a live risk.

### Thursday: the one session where the index told the truth

The Nifty fell 0.48%, 449 shares fell against 253 that rose, and the median share fell 0.46%. It was the week's worst day for the typical shareholder and the only day the headline matched the experience. It was also broad rather than shallow at the tail: 238 shares fell more than 1% against 120 that rose more than 1%, so the whole distribution shifted.

There are two separate answers to why, and the first is arithmetic rather than sentiment. **HDFC Bank fell 2.23% to ₹711.00, and on roughly a 13% weight in the index that single share takes about 0.29 percentage points out of a 0.48% fall.** Its heavier weight in the Sensex is why the Sensex fell 0.70%. Nobody should write that banks sold off: Kotak Mahindra Bank rose 1.80%, ICICI Bank 0.91% and Axis Bank 0.08%, and the private-bank group's median of minus 0.34% beat the market.

What is wearing HDFC Bank down is its own arithmetic. Its net interest margin, the gap between what a bank earns on its loans and what it pays on its deposits, is at a record low 3.26%. Low-cost deposits are 32% of the book against 38% two years ago, and foreign holding has fallen for a fifth straight quarter. Two dated items sat alongside on the day: a United States lawsuit disclosed that day on a single credible report we did not second-source, and an escalation dated 26 August in which more than 75 investors holding over US$13.5 million of principal in a Luxembourg life fund sold through the bank's Dubai operations are writing to the Prime Minister's Office and approaching the Reserve Bank. That escalation is reported investor intent, not a filed complaint, and it should be read at that grade.

Second, Thursday was the BSE Sensex's monthly derivatives expiry, on the last-Thursday convention, while the Nifty's own monthly expiry had already gone on Tuesday. So the Sensex falling harder than the Nifty needs no sentiment explanation at all.

Taking the positives first: pharma was the day's one real advance and it was genuinely broad, the index up 0.84% with fourteen of nineteen constituents higher and a median constituent up 0.50%. Removing the two largest risers still leaves the remaining seventeen positive. Underneath it sat a split with no established cause: pharmaceutical manufacturing and hospitals rose while diagnostics were sold hard, Thyrocare down 3.92%, Concord Biotech down 3.91% and Aris down 2.75%. No same-day catalyst was established for either leg.

Cement was the casualty. Not one of eleven cement names rose, and the median cement share fell 1.03%. UltraTech's flat close was the best in the group, Ambuja fell 1.94%, Shree Cement 1.55%, Dalmia Bharat 1.15%. No dated cement demand, pricing or policy item from Thursday turned up anywhere. Cause not established in our briefs, and after a full search that is a finding rather than a gap. Metals were next at 1.12% down at the median, then state-owned banks at 0.90% down with exactly one advance in twelve.

India VIX rose 4.71% to 11.0675 alongside a lower index and negative breadth, the textbook pairing that Tuesday and Wednesday both lacked. The qualifier matters as much as the number: 11.07 is 60.3% below its own 52-week high, so it corroborates the direction and establishes nothing about stress.

One last Thursday finding, because it cuts against the usual story. Midcap 50 fell 0.10% and Smallcap 250 fell 0.12%, against a small-company median of minus 0.54%, the worst of any group measured. Meanwhile the Nifty 50's own median constituent fell 0.27%, shallower than the market. The small-company indices understated the damage in exactly the way the large-company indices overstated it.

### Friday: a green headline on a flat market

The Nifty rose 0.35% and the Sensex 0.43%, and 361 shares fell against 343 that rose. The median share lost 0.04%.

It was one sector. **Nifty IT rose 3.51%, with every one of its nine priced constituents higher, and on that sector's weight it accounts for effectively the whole of the Nifty's gain.** Coforge rose 5.93%, Tata Consultancy Services 4.16%, Persistent Systems 3.91%, Tech Mahindra 3.53%, Infosys 2.99% and Mphasis 1.72%. Strip the technology names out of the Nifty 50 and its own constituents were 18 up, 18 down, one flat, with a median move of 0.00%.

Why technology, and why on Friday rather than Thursday, is section 6's second explainer. The short version is a clock. Nvidia reported after the American close on Wednesday. On Thursday India traded only the press release, because the American market had not yet opened to vote on it, and Nifty IT actually fell 0.32% that day. The session that voted closed at 01:30 India time on Friday morning, with the Nasdaq up 1.57%, Nvidia up 8.7% and technology the only advancing S&P 500 sector. India's 09:15 open on Friday was its first chance to price it.

Two facts that belong side by side. The Nifty Smallcap 250 closed at a 52-week high on a session where 361 of 706 shares fell, so the index was at a record and the median small-company shareholder made nothing. And not one share in the 707-name universe fell 5% or more; the worst close anywhere was minus 4.66%.

India VIX fell 3.52% to 10.68, leaving it 1.04% above its own 52-week low, going into a weekend containing a growth release, an index rebalance and a policy deadline. The market was charging almost nothing to insure against a surprise.

### One thing the reader is owed about this week's record

**Two of the last seven trading sessions never got their own evening write-up.** Friday 21 August and Tuesday 25 August both died mid-run, and Tuesday's died at a 600-second stall after it had written 91 signals onto 36 nodes and before it could compute any of them. What was missing was our writing, not the observation, and it is worse for the public reader than for the private one.

### The typical share had a losing week and the small-company index hit a high

Over the five sessions, **316 shares rose and 388 fell out of 704**. Set that against where the indices finished and the picture is a genuine puzzle rather than a rounding difference.

The small and mid-sized company indices rose, and yet more small and mid-sized companies fell than rose. Both statements are true, and the reason is the same weighting arithmetic that runs through the whole week: an index is an average weighted by company size, so within the small-company universe the larger small companies can carry the index while the count of shares goes the other way.

There is a second week of evidence for this. The breadth counts for the two most recent weeks are almost identical.

Three more shares rose and three more fell than in the week before, out of roughly seven hundred. That is two consecutive weeks in which slightly more than half the market fell by nearly the same margin, and the second consecutive week the Nifty itself declined. Against the week ending 7 August, when 427 rose and 259 fell, the change in character is unmistakable. There is no row for the week ending 14 August, so the series itself has a hole in it and this comparison skips a week.

**Do not add that column up, and we have not.** Monday's pair is explicitly not final, Tuesday's is missing entirely, and two of the four days come from aggregators rather than the exchange. A weekly total built on that mixture would be false precision dressed as a fact.

The shape is clear enough without a total. **Domestic institutions bought on every one of the four days we hold, without exception.** Foreign investors bought modestly on Monday and Wednesday, sold slightly on Thursday, and then sold heavily on Friday. Friday's sale is roughly an order of magnitude larger than either of the two buying days. Whatever else Friday was, it was not foreign money returning.

**Friday's pairing is the single most instructive fact in this section.** Foreign investors sold ₹5,039.80 crore and domestic institutions bought ₹5,183.93 crore, absorbing it almost exactly, on a day the index closed higher. Two nearly equal and opposite flows, and a green screen on top of them. And Bank Nifty, the usual destination for domestic institutional money, closed at minus 0.02%. When five thousand crore of domestic buying leaves no mark on the largest domestic sector, either the money went somewhere else or someone else was selling the same names. Cause not established in our briefs.

### What protection cost

India VIX closed the week at 10.68, down 4.64% over the five sessions, having run 11.525, about 11.07, 10.565, 11.0675 and 10.68. It measures what traders are paying for insurance against a sharp index move over the next month, so a low number is a statement about complacency rather than about safety. **It ended the week 1.04% above its own 52-week low, three days before a growth release with a forecast spread of more than a full percentage point on it.** Low volatility is not a forecast of calm. It is what protection costs when almost nobody is buying any.

## 4. Earnings: the week that was not one

**Six Indian companies actually reported results this week, and all six filed on Tuesday and Wednesday.** Not one of them is a company we can price. Not one price move in any of the week's seven briefs is attributed to a result. The one large earnings event that did move Indian shares was reported in another country.

### A company being asked a question is not a company answering one

Our own scan flagged KEI Industries and Gallantt Ispat as reporters on Friday. Both filings were the exact opposite of a result: the exchange asking each company for a clarification on its June-quarter numbers, reply awaited, with no figures attached to either. Reading the underlying feed, that mislabel was five times larger than the two names we caught. **The exchange broadcast ten clarification requests in a single block between 12:08 and 12:21 on Friday, under thirteen minutes, every one of them for the quarter ended 30 June 2026**: Amir Chand Jagdish Kumar Exports, KEI Industries, BEML Land Assets, Jyoti Structures, Atcom Technologies, Gallantt Ispat, Glottis, Royal Orchid Hotels, Confidence Petroleum India and Kriti Industries.

Regulation 33 is the listing rule that sets what a company must file with its quarterly accounts and by when. A clarification request under it means the exchange thinks something is missing or unclear. Ten of them inside thirteen minutes is one compliance sweep by the exchange, not ten separate company events.

The point generalises, and it is worth carrying. **The file on this desk named `financial_results.csv` contained no financial results at all this week.** All seventeen of its rows are either the exchange asking for a clarification, thirteen of them, or a company replying to one, four of them. One of those replies concerned the quarter that ended 31 March 2021, five years ago.

### The six, and why the count elsewhere says fifteen

Our own weekly data file counts seventeen filings from fifteen companies. Reading each row, the fifteen split into three classes: companies reporting for the first time, the same result arriving a second time in a different wrapper, and things that are not results at all. Monday's single "reporter" is a newspaper advertisement of results already published. Thursday's seven are all machine-readable re-filings, press releases, call recordings, a revision of results approved on 22 August, and one copy of a board meeting held on 11 August, sixteen days earlier.

**The six that genuinely reported.** On Tuesday: Ardee Industries at 14:35, MV Electrosystems at 16:33, Vineet Laboratories at 19:35 and again at 19:52, and State Trading Corporation of India at 21:51. On Wednesday: Simbhaoli Sugars at 18:00 and Juniper Green Energy at 19:53.

Vineet Laboratories is a small finding on its own. It filed two separate sets of results seventeen minutes apart, the June 2026 quarter and then **the full year ended 31 March 2026**, an annual account published five months after the year finished. Why is not established anywhere in our briefs.

### Why none of it moved anything, and the reason is mechanical

The desk prices 707 Indian shares. **Not one of the fifteen names on the roster is among them.** The only two symbols in this week's entire earnings conversation that we can price are KEI Industries and Gallantt Ispat, and those are the two that were not reporters at all. Their non-reaction is measurable precisely because they are in the universe: KEI rose 0.61% and Gallantt 0.17% on Friday, against an index up 0.35%.

So the honest statement is not that we failed to explain a results reaction. **There was no reaction to explain.** This was a week of very small companies filing very late, and almost nobody was looking.

Two further points a reader should have. **Only one of the six filings landed while the market was open.** Continuous trading ends at 15:15, and Ardee Industries filed at 14:35. That is the single result in the entire week that could have moved its own share on its own day, and it arrived on Tuesday, the session whose pipeline died. **Four of the six genuine reporters filed on Tuesday, and a fifth and sixth on Wednesday evening, so two-thirds of the week's real earnings news landed on the one day we published nothing, and the rest on a day the public feed did not cover either.** We hold every one of those filings. We published no same-day read on any of them.

And we do not hold their numbers. The revenue and profit figures for all six sit inside PDF attachments to those filings, and nothing in our chain parsed them. Growth against what, margin against what: for all six, cause not established in our briefs.

### The results still moving Indian shares this week were reported before it

Positives first.

**LT Foods rose 7.34% to ₹459 on Monday on a competitor's numbers, not its own.** KRBL's June quarter showed basmati realisations, meaning the average price a tonne of rice actually fetched, holding up at about 20% higher than a year earlier despite West Asian shipping disruption, with management guiding export volumes to recover from the September quarter. LT Foods finished the week up 10.30%. The give-back is worth naming honestly: the share touched ₹482.85 during Monday and closed at ₹459, handing back roughly half the move. There is also a tension inside it. The rice read and the oil read price opposite outcomes of the same event, because the rice case depends on West Asian shipping normalising while the week's oil fall was itself a bet on the same thing.

**A two-week-old result was still doing the work on Friday.** The exchange asked NMDC Steel why its trading volume had spiked. The company replied before Friday's open that nothing new had happened, pointing back to results filed on 14 August and a credit-rating upgrade. The share then rose another 8.96%, taking it to plus 23.20% over the week. A company formally stating it has no fresh news, into a run like that, is worth more than a guess at a cause.

Now the negatives.

**KPI Green Energy's June quarter shows the business and the balance sheet moving in opposite directions.** Operating profit rose 19% and the operating margin, the share of every hundred rupees of sales kept as profit after running costs, improved by 1.3 percentage points. Net profit still fell 15%, because finance costs, the interest the company pays on what it has borrowed, more than doubled at plus 109%. One quarter is not a pattern; the test is whether it persists into the September quarter.

**IRCTC's June quarter is a mix problem, and our two readings of it do not agree.** Revenue grew 18.1% to ₹1,370 crore while operating profit fell 2.77% and the operating margin dropped by 5.1 percentage points, because the ticketing business, which earns margins above 80%, grew 0.5% while catering grew 33.8%. Growing fast in the low-margin half and standing still in the high-margin half will do that to a profit line even when sales rise by nearly a fifth. A second reading later in the week put the fall at 6.1 percentage points to 28.2%, from a single aggregator with no second source obtained. **Both describe the June quarter's margin falling year on year, and 5.1 and 6.1 percentage points are not the same number.** They may be different measures rather than a contradiction, since operating margin and EBITDA margin are not the same line, but nothing in our chain reconciles them. The 5.1 figure carries the stronger provenance and is the one used here.

**Garware Hi-Tech Films carries an open question rather than a result:** management guides its margin about 5 percentage points below the quarter it has just delivered, meaning the company itself expects the next quarter to be materially less profitable than the one reported.

**And one earnings-adjacent event with no earnings in it.** After Hexaware's AI Day presentation on Friday 21 August, six houses published price views on identical disclosed facts: CLSA at ₹730, Kotak ₹660, IIFL ₹620, Axis ₹610, HSBC ₹575 at Hold and Jefferies ₹500 at Underperform. That is quoted with attribution, and it is not our view. The share fell 2.16% on the day of the presentation and rose 5.64% on Monday, the first session after the notes landed. A spread that wide published the same afternoon off the same slides is the market admitting it has no settled method for pricing an "AI-powered revenue" claim, and that claim, covering about half of revenue, is the chief executive's own and is defined nowhere. A presentation is not a result and should not be read as one.

### The season's missing half

Results are only half of reporting. The call, where management takes questions, is the other half, and this week it is largely absent. **Apar Industries' June-quarter call transcript was 34 days pending by Thursday**, 20 days past its own timeout, and the company filed on both Wednesday and Thursday without filing that. **Varun Beverages' June-quarter transcript is 28 days pending**, which has a direct consequence: the company approved a new subsidiary to enter ready-to-drink alcoholic beverages this week, and nobody has been able to ask whether its bottling agreements permit the parent to build one. Five specific quantified commitments made on Transrail Lighting's 3 February 2026 call are all past their own check dates, including full-year order intake of ₹9,500 to ₹10,000 crore and an operating margin held at 11.5 to 12%, and none can be tested because the transcripts that would settle them cannot be read.

Against that, the genuinely positive process finding of the week. **Several of the smallest companies on the exchange ran a better disclosure calendar than several much larger ones.** Juniper Green filed a press release, an investor presentation, a call recording and a newspaper advertisement around its result, and had pre-announced the call. Ardee and MV Electrosystems each filed presentations and call material. Lohia Corp filed the transcript of its 20 August call on Thursday, and Patel Retail filed a call recording, an investor presentation and its quarterly transcript inside the same week.

## 5. The sectors, and why they moved

### Read this before the table

**For all five sessions of this week, the exchange's own sector indices were almost entirely unreadable on our feed.** Nine of the eleven sector indices produced no usable daily move on Monday, and the same nine were dark again on Wednesday, Thursday and Friday. On several days four more failed to arrive at all, including Consumer Durables, Healthcare, Oil and Gas and Private Bank. **Only Nifty IT and Nifty Pharma produced a live reading on any day this week.**

That has two consequences, and both belong to the reader rather than to us.

**First, our own weekly sector figures were wrong and we caught them.** The automated weekly file this brief is built from published a sector table showing seven sectors up and three down. Recomputing every sector by hand from the closing prices we hold for 21 August and 28 August, **nine of its eleven rows are wrong, including four where the sign is reversed.** Auto, Media, Consumption and PSU Bank were all reported higher on the week and all four actually fell. The true count is three sectors up and eight down. This is the third consecutive week the same block has failed in the same way, and this week's row was published without the correction note the previous week carried.

**Second, everything below that is not IT or Pharma is our own reconstruction, and it is labelled as one.** Where the index was missing, we built sector direction from the individual shares instead: take every company in that sector we can price, find the middle one, and report that. It answers "what did the typical share in this sector do," which is not the same question as "what did the sector index do," because an index weights by company size and a median treats every member alike. We tested the substitute against the two live indices every session. It reproduced Pharma to within six basis points on Monday and matched both indices on Thursday and Friday, and **it failed visibly on Wednesday**, when Nifty IT fell roughly four times harder than its own middle member and Nifty Pharma rose while its middle member rose four times as much. The method works most days and it broke once, in the open.

### There is one story here, not two

The best and worst sectors share a single explanation, and it was written down twice on two different days.

On Wednesday, money moved out of consumer-facing companies and into metals, pharma and public-sector banks. Consumption fell 0.82% with six of 27 names higher, FMCG fell 0.70% with six of 22. Against them metals rose 1.75% with sixteen of eighteen higher, pharma 0.89% with seventeen of 24, and public-sector banks 0.62% with all thirteen higher. Those were the two weakest corners of the market that day and not one headline mentioned either.

On Friday the same shape returned. FMCG, consumption, autos and financials all fell on two-to-one decline breadth or worse, inside an index that closed up, while money went to technology and metals.

**In plain words: companies that make their money selling to Indian households were sold, and companies whose fortunes are set outside India were bought.** That is one story, and it rests on counts of named shares rather than on the index levels that failed us.

**Two honest qualifications travel with it.**

The metals half has a hole in it that was never filled. On Monday the one base-metals price we could honestly read was copper at plus 0.20%, which does not support a metals rally, and the aluminium reading was one of six figures suspected of a futures-contract-roll error and was not used in either direction. Commodity daily changes were suppressed all week for the same fault. So Friday's description of metals as tracking global prices goes further than Monday and Wednesday could support. **Indian metal shares rose through most of the week and we could not, on any session, tie that to a metal price we trusted.** That disagreement is inside our own record and it is reported rather than resolved.

And we explicitly refused to call autos weak on Wednesday. One outlet did; our own 35-name build read plus 0.37% with 20 shares up. The auto index was dark, so neither party can settle it, and no auto direction is asserted here for that session. The week's reconstruction has autos down 0.94%, which is consistent with weakness overall and does not adjudicate that one day.

### Technology, the one sector move that is fully dated and index-confirmed

Nifty IT finished the week up 2.45%, and inside that sit two opposite events.

**Wednesday's fall of 1.47%** has one dated cause, the American visa-appointment pause described in section 2, and the index badly overstated it. Across the 34 constituents the middle share fell 0.39%, twelve rose and Cyient gained 7.01%. Five very large companies were effectively the whole move.

**Friday's rise of 3.51%** is the largest single sector session of the week, all nine priced constituents higher, and it is fully sourced to the previous night's American close. The mechanism is section 6's second explainer.

The second event was larger than the first, which is how a sector spends a week going down on domestic-facing news and still finishes up.

### One corner of the market where there was no reading at all

Nifty Consumer Durables was among the indices that failed outright on Wednesday, Thursday and Friday, meaning it produced nothing and could not even be reconstructed. That has a visible cost. Blue Star closed at a 52-week low inside what looked like the weakest sector of a day, and we could not settle that cleanly because the index that would have contextualised it did not exist. **For one part of the market this week there was neither an index nor a reconstruction, only a hole.**
