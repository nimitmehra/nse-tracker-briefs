## 1. The week in plain English

The Nifty 50 finished the week at 24,175.65, down 0.31% across five sessions. The Nifty 500, the broadest index we track and the one that comes nearest to describing the whole listed market, finished down 0.01%. After a full week of trading, that is dead flat.

It was not a quiet week. It was a round trip: 24,219.05, then 24,334.55, then 24,207.75, then 24,090.85, then 24,175.65. Tuesday's close was the week's high, Thursday's was its low, and Friday gave back a little under half of the fall. The Nifty 500's best and worst closes sit 129 points apart and the week's net change is under two points.

Three separate forces pushed in three directions and roughly cancelled. None of them had anything to do with the other two.

**Technology moved on an American clock.** Nifty IT, the index of the country's software services companies, fell 1.47% on Wednesday after the United States paused visa appointments at its embassies and consulates worldwide, which constrains how quickly Indian firms move staff to client sites. It fell a further 0.32% on Thursday and rose 3.51% on Friday alone, after an overnight American technology rally. Over the week it gained 2.45%.

**Crude oil fell on every single session.** Brent, the global benchmark grade against which India prices most of what it imports, closed around US$88.36 against US$94.19 the previous Friday, a fall of roughly 6%. The cause was diplomacy rather than demand: Iran and Oman resumed talks over the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow sea passage through which most of India's crude arrives, discussing a temporary navigational corridor and agreeing to work on clearing mines. Cheaper oil is a tailwind for a country that imports about 85% of what it refines.

**And the domestic-demand complex sold off quietly all week.** Consumer staples, meaning the everyday products Indian households buy from Indian companies, fell on almost every session we can measure. On our own recomputed figures, Nifty FMCG lost 1.46% on the week, Nifty Consumption 0.95% and Nifty Auto 0.94%. Those are the three largest weekly sector falls in the market and no headline named any of them.

Now the number that describes the week. **Across roughly 704 shares we can price reliably, 316 rose over the week and 388 fell.** The typical Indian share lost money in a week the headline called flat, and the Nifty Smallcap 250 index still finished Friday at a 52-week high. Day by day it is starker: **on three of the five sessions the index moved one way and the typical share moved the other**, and on a fourth the index understated the fall by nearly half. Exactly one session, Thursday, had a headline that honestly described what happened to the average shareholder.

Anyone who read this week off the index read almost none of it.

## 2. How each day moved, and why

Two words first. **Breadth** is the count of shares that rose against the count that fell, out of roughly 705 companies we can price each day. The **median share** is the one exactly in the middle of that list, so it describes the typical company's day rather than the biggest company's day. An index cannot tell you either, because it weights every company by size.

| Session | Nifty 50 | Rose | Fell | Median share | Did the index describe the market? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon 24 Aug | 24,219.05, **−0.14%** | 302 | 400 | −0.24% | Direction yes, scale no |
| Tue 25 Aug | 24,334.55, **+0.48%** | 320 | 386 | −0.12% | **No, opposite** |
| Wed 26 Aug | 24,207.75, **−0.52%** | 383 | 322 | +0.14% | **No, opposite** |
| Thu 27 Aug | 24,090.85, **−0.48%** | 253 | 449 | −0.46% | **Yes, cleanly** |
| Fri 28 Aug | 24,175.65, **+0.35%** | 343 | 361 | −0.04% | **No, opposite** |

**Monday: a flat index sitting on a falling market.** The Nifty lost 32.95 points, which on 24,219 is not worth an explanation in itself, and 400 shares fell against 302 that rose, the second consecutive session of a falling market under a flat headline. The day's named cause was geopolitical and it pointed the wrong way. The United States Treasury Secretary announced what he called the single greatest financial offensive ever mounted against Iran, and the part that reaches India is that Washington says it will sanction any country that keeps trading with Iran. Iran's Persian Gulf Strait Authority replied that vessels breaching its rules face fines, seizure or confiscation. **And yet Brent fell about 1.8% on the day, to roughly US$92.68.** A threat to oil supply that leaves oil cheaper has not been priced as a threat to oil supply. What appears to have been priced instead is the payments and trade channel, meaning the risk to how India settles and routes purchases, rather than the size of the import bill. Keep the scale in view: a 0.14% close is a very small reaction for a country that imports 85% of its crude through that waterway. And the sanctions announcement itself landed between roughly 17:30 and 19:00 India time, after our market had shut. **Monday traded the anticipation, pre-announced on Sunday. It did not trade the contents.**

**Tuesday: the index rose and most shares fell.** The Nifty rose 0.48% to the week's high while 386 shares fell and 320 rose. This is the cleanest explanation of the week and it has nothing to do with sentiment. Tuesday was the National Stock Exchange's monthly derivatives expiry, the day the month's futures and options contracts settle and positions in them must be closed or rolled forward. The date was checked against the calendar rather than assumed: 1 August was a Saturday, which puts the month's Tuesdays on the 4th, 11th, 18th and 25th, and the convention is the last Tuesday. Settlement demand concentrates in a narrow band of very large, heavily traded companies, which is exactly the band an index weights most. **Expiry propped the index through a handful of large companies while the rest of the market did not participate.** India VIX, the index measuring what traders pay for protection against a sharp move over the coming month, fell 3.95% on a day the index rose, which is not the usual pairing.

**Wednesday: the index fell and two-thirds of shares rose.** The Nifty fell 0.52% while 383 shares rose, the exact reverse of Tuesday on consecutive days. The dated cause of the weakness at the top was real and narrow: the United States paused visa appointments at embassies and consulates worldwide while it re-screens and retrains consular officers, with no announced date for normal availability to resume. That is the one dated cause behind Nifty IT falling 1.47%. **But the index badly overstates it.** Across the IT index's 34 constituents the median share fell only 0.39%, twelve rose, and Cyient gained 7.01%. Five very large companies were effectively the whole move, and the gap between the sector's best and worst performer was 12.41 percentage points, the widest in the market, which is the opposite of what a sector-wide shock looks like. One caution: the six-figure H-1B visa fee circulating alongside this story is a proposal, not a rule in force.

The genuinely broad falls on Wednesday went unnamed everywhere. Consumption fell 0.82% with six of 27 names higher, FMCG 0.70% with six of 22. Against them metals rose 1.75% with sixteen of eighteen higher, pharma 0.89% with seventeen of 24, public-sector banks 0.62% with all thirteen higher. **The size gradient reads as one line: Nifty 50 constituents down 0.66%, Nifty 100 down 0.43%, Midcap 50 down 0.17%, Smallcap 250 up 0.59%.** The bigger the company, the worse the day. Crude fell again on the Iran and Oman corridor talks, with two cautions attached: a corridor has been discussed, not announced; and a political claim that all mines are already cleared sits in the same news cycle as an agreement to clear them, and both cannot be true. India VIX fell 4.65% to 10.565, near its 52-week low, through a session that opened with an unresolved Hormuz blockage and a visa pause on the country's largest export-services sector. **The option market had stopped pricing either as a live risk.**

**Thursday: the one session where the index told the truth.** The Nifty fell 0.48%, 449 shares fell against 253, and the median share fell 0.46%. It was the week's worst day for the typical shareholder and the only day the headline matched the experience: 238 shares fell more than 1% against 120 that rose more than 1%, so the whole distribution shifted rather than just its tail.

There are two answers to why, and the first is arithmetic. **HDFC Bank fell 2.23% to ₹711.00, and on roughly a 13% weight in the index that single share takes about 0.29 percentage points out of a 0.48% fall.** Its heavier weight in the Sensex is why the Sensex fell 0.70%. Nobody should write that banks sold off: Kotak Mahindra Bank rose 1.80%, ICICI Bank 0.91%, Axis Bank 0.08%, and the private-bank group's median of minus 0.34% beat the market. What is wearing HDFC Bank down is its own arithmetic: its net interest margin, the gap between what a bank earns on loans and pays on deposits, is at a record low 3.26%, low-cost deposits are 32% of the book against 38% two years ago, and foreign holding has fallen for a fifth straight quarter. Two dated items sat alongside: a United States lawsuit disclosed that day on a single credible report we did not second-source, and an escalation dated 26 August in which more than 75 investors holding over US$13.5 million of principal in a Luxembourg life fund, sold through the bank's Dubai operations, are writing to the Prime Minister's Office and approaching the Reserve Bank. **That is reported investor intent, not a filed complaint, and it should be read at that grade.**

Second, Thursday was the BSE Sensex's monthly expiry, on the last-Thursday convention, while the Nifty's own had gone on Tuesday. So the Sensex falling harder needs no sentiment explanation at all.

Positives first on the sectors. Pharma was the day's one real advance and it was broad: the index rose 0.84%, fourteen of nineteen constituents rose, and removing the two largest risers still leaves the remaining seventeen positive. Underneath it sat a split with no established cause, as pharmaceutical manufacturing and hospitals rose while diagnostics were sold hard, Thyrocare down 3.92%, Concord Biotech down 3.91%. **Cement was the casualty: not one of eleven cement names rose and the median cement share fell 1.03%.** No dated cement demand, pricing or policy item from Thursday turned up anywhere, and after a full search that is a finding rather than a gap. India VIX rose 4.71% to 11.0675, the textbook pairing Tuesday and Wednesday both lacked, though at 60.3% below its own 52-week high it corroborates direction and establishes nothing about stress.

One last Thursday finding, because it cuts against the usual story. Midcap 50 fell 0.10% and Smallcap 250 fell 0.12%, against a small-company median of minus 0.54%, the worst of any group measured, while the Nifty 50's own median constituent fell 0.27%, shallower than the market. **The small-company indices understated the damage in exactly the way the large-company indices overstated it.**

**Friday: a green headline on a flat market.** The Nifty rose 0.35% and 361 shares fell against 343. The median share lost 0.04%. It was one sector: **Nifty IT rose 3.51% with all nine of its priced constituents higher, and on that sector's weight it accounts for effectively the whole index gain.** Coforge rose 5.93%, Tata Consultancy Services 4.16%, Persistent Systems 3.91%, Tech Mahindra 3.53%, Infosys 2.99%, Mphasis 1.72%. Strip the technology names out of the Nifty 50 and its own constituents were 18 up, 18 down, one flat, median 0.00%. Why technology and why Friday rather than Thursday is section 6's second explainer.

Two facts belong side by side. The Smallcap 250 closed at a 52-week high on a session where 361 of 706 shares fell, so the index was at a record and the median small-company shareholder made nothing. And not one share in the 707-name universe fell 5% or more; the worst close anywhere was minus 4.66%.

**One thing the reader is owed about this week's record.** Two of the last seven trading sessions never got their own evening write-up. Friday 21 August and Tuesday 25 August both died mid-run, Tuesday's at a 600-second stall after it had written 91 signals onto 36 nodes and before it could compute any of them. Neither is a blank: Monday's brief covered two sessions and Wednesday's covered two, and everything above dated to Tuesday comes from Wednesday's brief, written a day late and dated honestly. **What is genuinely missing is the public record. There is no public brief for Tuesday and none for Wednesday, so a reader who follows only the public feed went from Monday evening to Thursday morning with nothing.** The prices for all five sessions exist and are used throughout. What was missing was our writing, not the observation.

The small and mid-sized company indices rose, and yet more small and mid-sized companies fell than rose. Both statements are true, and the reason is the weighting arithmetic that runs through the whole week: within the small-company universe, the larger small companies can carry the index while the count of shares goes the other way.

**Bank Nifty, at minus 0.46%, is the weakest row and it is only half explained.** The one large bank move of the week is the one described in section 2: HDFC Bank fell 2.23% on Thursday on its own arithmetic, a record-low margin and a shrinking share of cheap deposits, while Kotak Mahindra, ICICI and Axis all rose that day and the private-bank group's median beat the market. That is the visible weight in the number. **The other half we could not establish, and we said so at the time.** Friday's brief recorded that foreign investors sold ₹5,039.80 crore and domestic institutions bought ₹5,183.93 crore, and that Nifty Bank, the usual destination for domestic institutional money, still closed minus 0.02%. Its words were: **"We could not establish why."** That sentence was earned in the daily and it stands in the weekly. When five thousand crore of domestic buying leaves no mark on the largest domestic sector, either the money went somewhere else or somebody else was selling the same names, and we cannot tell you which.

There is a second week of evidence. **The weekly breadth counts for the two most recent weeks are almost identical**: 313 up and 385 down in the week to 21 August, then 316 up and 388 down this week, out of roughly seven hundred. Two consecutive weeks in which slightly more than half the market fell by nearly the same margin, and the second consecutive week the Nifty itself declined. Against the week ending 7 August, when 427 rose and 259 fell, the change in character is unmistakable. There is no row in our record for the week ending 14 August, so that comparison skips a week.

India VIX closed the week at 10.68, down 4.64%, having run 11.525, about 11.07, 10.565, 11.0675 and 10.68. It measures what traders pay to insure against a sharp index move over the next month, so a low number is a statement about complacency rather than about safety. **It ended 1.04% above its own 52-week low, three days before a growth release with a forecast spread of more than a full percentage point on it.** Low volatility is not a forecast of calm. It is what protection costs when almost nobody is buying any.

## 4. Earnings: the week that was not one

**Six Indian companies actually reported results this week, and all six filed on Tuesday and Wednesday.** Not one of them is a company we can price, and not one price move in any of the week's seven briefs is attributed to a result.

### A company being asked a question is not a company answering one

Our own scan flagged KEI Industries and Gallantt Ispat as reporters on Friday. Both filings were the opposite of a result: the exchange asking each company for a clarification on its June-quarter numbers, reply awaited, with no figures attached. Reading the underlying feed, that mislabel was five times larger than the two names we caught. **The exchange broadcast ten clarification requests in a single block between 12:08 and 12:21 on Friday, under thirteen minutes, every one for the quarter ended 30 June 2026.** Regulation 33 is the listing rule setting what a company must file with its quarterly accounts and by when; a clarification request under it means the exchange thinks something is missing or unclear. **Ten inside thirteen minutes is one compliance sweep by the exchange, not ten company events.**

The point generalises. **The file on this desk named for financial results contained no financial results at all this week.** All seventeen of its rows are the exchange asking for a clarification, thirteen of them, or a company replying to one, four. One of those replies concerned the quarter that ended 31 March 2021, five years ago.

### The six, and why our own count said fifteen

Our weekly data file counts seventeen filings from fifteen companies. Reading each row, they split into three classes: companies reporting for the first time, the same result arriving again in a different wrapper, and things that are not results. Monday's single "reporter" is a newspaper advertisement of results already published. Thursday's seven are machine-readable re-filings, press releases, call recordings, a revision of results approved on 22 August, and one copy of a board meeting held on 11 August.

**The six that genuinely reported.** Tuesday: Ardee Industries at 14:35, MV Electrosystems at 16:33, Vineet Laboratories at 19:35 and again at 19:52, State Trading Corporation of India at 21:51. Wednesday: Simbhaoli Sugars at 18:00 and Juniper Green Energy at 19:53. Vineet Laboratories is a finding on its own: it filed two sets of results seventeen minutes apart, the June 2026 quarter and then **the full year ended 31 March 2026**, an annual account published five months after the year finished. Why is not established anywhere in our briefs.

### Why none of it moved anything

We price 707 Indian shares. **Not one of the fifteen names on the roster is among them.** The only two symbols in this week's entire earnings conversation that we can price are KEI and Gallantt, and those are the two that were not reporters. Their non-reaction is measurable precisely because they are in the universe: KEI rose 0.61% and Gallantt 0.17% on Friday, against an index up 0.35%.

**So we did not fail to explain a results reaction. There was no reaction to explain.** This was a week of very small companies filing very late, and almost nobody was looking.

Two further points. **Only one of the six landed while the market was open.** Continuous trading ends at 15:15 and Ardee filed at 14:35, the single result all week that could have moved its own share on its own day, and it arrived on Tuesday, the session whose pipeline died. **Four of the six filed on Tuesday and two more on Wednesday evening, so two-thirds of the week's real earnings news landed on the day we published nothing and the rest on a day the public feed did not cover either.** We hold every one of those filings. We published no same-day read on any. And we do not hold their numbers: the revenue and profit figures sit in PDF attachments nothing in our chain parsed.

### The results still moving Indian shares were reported before this week

Positives first. **LT Foods rose 7.34% to ₹459 on Monday on a competitor's numbers, not its own.** KRBL's June quarter showed basmati realisations, meaning the average price a tonne of rice actually fetched, holding about 20% higher than a year earlier despite West Asian shipping disruption, with management guiding export volumes to recover from the September quarter. LT Foods finished the week up 10.30%, though it touched ₹482.85 during Monday and closed at ₹459, handing back roughly half the move. There is a tension inside it: the rice read and the oil read price opposite outcomes of the same event, because the rice case depends on West Asian shipping normalising while the week's oil fall was a bet on the same thing.

**A two-week-old result was still doing the work on Friday.** The exchange asked NMDC Steel why its trading volume had spiked. The company replied before Friday's open that nothing new had happened, pointing back to results of 14 August and a credit-rating upgrade. The share then rose another 8.96%, taking it to plus 23.20% over the week.

Now the negatives. **KPI Green Energy's June quarter shows the business and the balance sheet moving in opposite directions.** Operating profit rose 19% and the operating margin, the share of every hundred rupees of sales kept as profit after running costs, improved by 1.3 percentage points. Net profit still fell 15%, because finance costs, the interest paid on borrowings, more than doubled at plus 109%. One quarter is not a pattern; the test is whether it persists into the September quarter.

**IRCTC's June quarter is a mix problem, and our two readings of it do not agree.** Revenue grew 18.1% to ₹1,370 crore while operating profit fell 2.77% and the operating margin dropped 5.1 percentage points, because ticketing, which earns margins above 80%, grew 0.5% while catering grew 33.8%. Growing fast in the low-margin half and standing still in the high-margin half does that to a profit line even when sales rise by nearly a fifth. A second reading later in the week put the fall at 6.1 percentage points to 28.2%, from a single aggregator with no second source. **Both describe the June quarter's margin falling year on year, and 5.1 and 6.1 percentage points are not the same number.** They may be different measures rather than a contradiction, since operating margin and EBITDA margin are not the same line, but nothing in our chain reconciles them. The 5.1 figure carries the stronger provenance and is the one used here.

**Garware Hi-Tech Films carries an open question rather than a result:** management guides its margin about 5 percentage points below the quarter it has just delivered.

**And one earnings-adjacent event with no earnings in it.** After Hexaware's AI Day presentation on Friday 21 August, six houses published price views on identical disclosed facts: CLSA at ₹730, Kotak ₹660, IIFL ₹620, Axis ₹610, HSBC ₹575 at Hold, Jefferies ₹500 at Underperform. That is quoted with attribution and is not our view. The share fell 2.16% on the day of the presentation and rose 5.64% on Monday. **A spread that wide published off the same slides is the market admitting it has no settled method for pricing an "AI-powered revenue" claim**, and that claim, covering about half of revenue, is the chief executive's own and is defined nowhere.

### The season's missing half

The call, where management takes questions, is the other half of reporting, and this week it is largely absent. **Apar Industries' June-quarter transcript was 34 days pending by Thursday**, 20 days past its own timeout, and the company filed on Wednesday and Thursday without filing that. **Varun Beverages' is 28 days pending**, which has a direct consequence: the company approved a new subsidiary to enter ready-to-drink alcoholic beverages this week, and nobody has been able to ask whether its bottling agreements permit the parent to build one. Five quantified commitments made on Transrail Lighting's 3 February call are all past their own check dates and none can be tested, because the transcripts that would settle them cannot be read.

Against that, the week's one positive process finding. **Several of the smallest companies on the exchange ran a better disclosure calendar than several much larger ones.** Juniper Green filed a press release, an investor presentation, a call recording and a newspaper advertisement around its result, and pre-announced the call. Ardee and MV Electrosystems each filed presentations and call material. Lohia Corp filed the transcript of its 20 August call, and Patel Retail filed a recording, a presentation and its quarterly transcript inside the same week.

## 5. The sectors, and why they moved

### Read this before the table

**For all five sessions, the exchange's own sector indices were almost entirely unreadable on our feed.** Nine of the eleven produced no usable daily move on Monday, and the same nine were dark again on Wednesday, Thursday and Friday. On several days four more failed to arrive at all, including Consumer Durables, Healthcare, Oil and Gas and Private Bank. **Only Nifty IT and Nifty Pharma produced a live reading on any day this week.**

**That has two consequences, and both belong to the reader.**

**First, our own weekly sector figures were wrong, and we caught them.** The automated file this brief is built from published a table showing seven sectors up and three down. Recomputing every sector by hand from the closing prices we hold for 21 and 28 August, **nine of its eleven rows are wrong, including four where the sign is reversed.** Auto, Media, Consumption and PSU Bank were all reported higher and all four fell. The true count is three up and eight down. This is the third consecutive week the same block has failed the same way, and this week's row was published without the correction note the previous week carried.

**Second, everything below that is not IT or Pharma is our own reconstruction.** Where the index was missing we built sector direction from the individual shares: take every company in the sector we can price, find the middle one, report that. It answers "what did the typical share in this sector do", which is not the same question as "what did the sector index do". We tested the substitute against the two live indices every session. It reproduced Pharma to within six basis points on Monday and matched both indices on Thursday and Friday, and **it failed visibly on Wednesday**, when Nifty IT fell roughly four times harder than its own middle member. The method works most days and it broke once, in the open.

### The four rows the story below does not cover

Metals, technology, pharma, FMCG, consumption, autos and financials are all accounted for in the paragraphs that follow. Four rows are not, and a number printed without either a cause or an admission is not a finding. **Here is each of the four, and two of them are causes while two are blanks.**

**Nifty PSU Bank, minus 0.18% — a cause, and it corrects an impression the next section could otherwise leave.** Public-sector banks did not fall steadily. They were the single strongest corner of Wednesday, up 0.62% with **all thirteen constituents higher**, and they gave it back on the sessions either side. Monday they fell about 0.93%, on the one sector reading all week we took from outside our own pipeline, with Bank of Baroda down 2.23%, Canara Bank down 2.05%, Jammu and Kashmir Bank down 1.88% and State Bank of India down 0.88%. Thursday they fell about 0.90%, and Business Standard's own close wrap independently named public-sector banks a weak spot that day. Friday, minus 0.14%. **One strong session against three weak ones nets to almost nothing, which is exactly what the minus 0.18% is.**

**Nifty Energy, minus 0.65% — a cause, and it is the connection to this brief's own headline.** Crude fell about 6% on the week, and the honest finding is that **the relief reached Indian energy shares on Monday and had stopped reaching them by Thursday.** On Monday the refiners and gas distributors moved the way the arithmetic says they should: Indian Oil rose 1.91%, GAIL 1.02% and Bharat Petroleum 0.29%, and we used that at the time as evidence the market was not pricing a supply shock. By Thursday, with Brent lower still, the same names had turned: Bharat Petroleum flat at 0.00%, Indian Oil down 1.37%, GAIL down 1.17%. Thursday's brief printed the conclusion plainly — **"Not one of them delivered the input-cost relief the arithmetic says they should have."** Two limits belong with it. Nifty Energy holds crude producers as well as crude buyers, and cheaper crude cuts a producer's realisations in exactly the way it cuts a refiner's costs, so the index nets two opposite effects. And **Nifty Oil and Gas, the index that would separate them, failed to download on every single session of the week**, so we cannot show you the split. **The cause is real, it is two-sided, and the instrument that would size the two sides was dark.**

**Nifty Media, minus 0.61% — no cause we can date, and the sample is too small to carry the number.** Our three daily reconstructions read minus 0.42%, minus 0.47% and minus 0.42%, which is a sector drifting rather than reacting, and no media filing, policy item or dated broker action turned up in any session. **The caveat matters more than the blank: the desk called this sector "too thin to quote," because the reconstruction rests on six or seven priced names.** A median of six shares is not a sector reading, and we are printing the row with that warning attached rather than dropping it.

**Nifty Realty, minus 0.30% — no cause we can date, and we refused an available one on the record.** Realty was one of Monday's two outperforming corners, ten of thirteen names higher, led by Ventive at plus 3.82%, Raymond Realty 2.82%, Brigade 2.32% and DLF 1.61%. It then drifted for three sessions: minus 0.42%, minus 0.21%, minus 0.22%. ## 6. Three things worth understanding properly

In a deliberate order: what the number means, then when the reaction happens, then what to do when neither answers the question.

### One. An index is a weighted average, not a vote

**The Nifty 50 is not a count of how many of its fifty companies rose.** It is an average in which every company is weighted by its size, so a very large company moves the number far more than a small one does, and the biggest handful can outvote everybody else. That is why an index and its own member companies can point in different directions on the same afternoon. The index answers "how much money moved". Breadth answers "how many companies moved". Those are different questions, and this week they gave different answers four times out of five. The same arithmetic runs one level up: a whole sector can be heavy enough inside an index to carry it alone.

**Friday is the clean demonstration.** Nifty IT rose 3.51%, all nine priced constituents higher, and on that weight it accounts for effectively the entire 0.35% gain in the Nifty 50. Remove those nine and the remaining Nifty 50 shares were 18 up, 18 down, one flat, typical move 0.00%. **So the honest sentence is not that the market rose. One sector rose and was heavy enough to make the scoreboard green while slightly more shares fell than rose.** Thursday is the same thing one level down, at a single company: HDFC Bank's 2.23% fall is over half the index's 0.48% decline, and the banks did not sell off. **A reader who saw "banks drag index lower" and concluded "banks are in trouble" would have drawn precisely the wrong conclusion from a true headline.**

**Thursday is also the exception, and it belongs here rather than hidden.** The index and the market agreed on direction, so the index is not always lying. What it still got wrong was depth: the Nifty 50's own typical constituent fell 0.27%, shallower than the market, while the large-company index fell harder than the market. Large companies as a group had a milder day and their index still fell more. The whole difference is weight.

The version a friend can be told: *the Nifty counts big companies much more than small ones, so on Friday nine technology shares turned the whole index green even though more shares fell than rose, and on Thursday one bank caused over half the index's fall while most other banks went up.*

### Two. A market can only react when it is open, and the clock is not the one you assume

**Indian continuous trading ends at 15:15, and the closing auction, the short final window in which the official closing price of every share is set, ends at 15:35.** Nothing published after that can have moved that day's closing price. A company filing at 18:33 on a Thursday evening is not Thursday's news. It is Friday's.

The rule runs both ways. A document filed on a Friday night or over a weekend does not disappear; it queues, and Monday is the first session that can price it. **So a Monday move can be caused by something that happened three days earlier.** Then there is the second clock: American markets open after India has closed and their session ends around 01:30 India time, so the American trading day India reacts to is always the previous night's.

**The strongest proof in this week is a mistake we made and corrected.** Nvidia reported after the American close on Wednesday: revenue of US$96.2 billion, up 106% on the year, with guidance, meaning the company's own forecast for the coming quarter, of US$108 billion against roughly US$104.2 billion expected. Indian traders had all of it before Thursday's open. **On Thursday, India did nothing with it.** Nifty IT fell 0.32%, 13 of 20 constituents lower, while Korea's KOSPI rose 1.53%, Shanghai 1.13% and Taiwan 0.31%. **Thursday's brief read that as India sitting out the biggest company result in the world**, and offered a reason why the chip-to-services link might be one-sided, labelling that reasoning rather than a sourced finding.

On Friday the picture inverted and we said plainly what had been wrong. The American session that actually voted closed at 01:30 India time on Friday morning: Nasdaq up 1.57%, S&P 500 up 0.72%, Nvidia up 8.7%, technology the only S&P 500 sector to advance. India opened against that close and Nifty IT gained 3.51%. **There was never a lag. We measured India against a press release, when the clock Indian technology shares track is the American close.**

**The reverse direction, three examples from Monday.** Vishal Mega Mart re-appointed its founder chief executive, filed at 20:14 on Friday 21 August; the share rose 9.74% on Monday. Urban Company disclosed a Delhi High Court undertaking by Kent RO to withdraw disparaging advertising, filed at 14:08 on Sunday 23 August; the share rose 6.46% on Monday. Welspun Corp filed a US$1.8 billion pipe order, about ₹17,200 crore, at 22:57 on Thursday 20 August, and the following session was the first that could price it.

**One honest qualifier on Friday's rally.** Four things say it was imported price movement rather than a change in Indian earnings. Participation was uniform, including Wipro at plus 2.58% and Mphasis at plus 1.72%, companies with no artificial-intelligence story. The leaders were mid-sized, higher-swing names, and Infosys lagged its own sector. The rupee firmed 0.07%, a small headwind for companies that earn in dollars and spend in rupees. And not one brokerage published an Indian software sector note all day, which is not what research desks do when they believe a sector's earnings power has changed. Thursday's American session was a software rally as much as a chip one, with Salesforce up 22% and Okta up 28%, and Indian services companies sell into enterprise software budgets rather than into silicon.

The rule to carry away: **before believing any sentence of the form "shares fell because of X", check whether X happened while the market was open.**

### Three. "Nobody established why" is a finding, and this week it has a name and a price

The phrase "cause not established" appears throughout this brief. A reader could reasonably conclude either that we did not try or that markets are random. Both are wrong.

Companies must tell the exchange about anything price-sensitive, and those filings are timestamped, so a filing can be matched to a move. But **not everything that moves a share is a filing.** The largest category that is not is a **block deal**: a big pre-arranged trade in which one holder sells a large parcel of shares directly to a buyer, off the ordinary order book. It shows up in the exchange's record of large negotiated trades, and it very frequently explains a share that jumps with nothing filed. **That record has been missing from our feed for the whole week and beyond**, called out as unavailable for six runs on Monday and ten or more by Friday.

**The clean proof is the one case where the answer was found by hand.** Ather Energy rose 8.09% on Friday to ₹1,616.30, a genuine 52-week closing high, and the cause is fully sourced: **Hero MotoCorp lifted its fully diluted holding from 29.88% to about 32.8% through a block deal of 1,18,80,000 shares at ₹1,480, worth ₹1,758 crore, completing on 3 September.** The only reason we have it is that somebody went and searched for it.

Two things worth glossing in the same breath. **This was a secondary transaction**: Hero bought existing shares from existing owners, so no new money entered the company. That is a different thing from a company raising funds. A separate share issue, of about ₹1,200 crore in total, was allotted on 25 August, and that one was a genuine fundraise. **Different events, different dates, opposite plumbing, and the same headline shape.**

So the argument writes itself. On one Friday name the missing record was recovered by hand and it explained an 8% move completely. **On the several names where it could not be recovered, the most likely explanation sits in a file nobody could open.** "We do not know why" is a statement about a specific missing instrument, not about the market being unknowable.

**The scale of the blank, dated.** On Thursday, nine of the ten biggest risers had no cause we could establish, including INOX India up 13.04% at a 52-week high with nothing filed on the day or the evening before, and Apar Industries up 4.91% to ₹17,900, by far the biggest company on that list, whose four filings in the window were annual-meeting and dividend housekeeping. On Friday, six shares rose between 6.75% and 11.24% with no cause established; J.G. Chemicals rose 11.24% to a 52-week high having filed nothing at all across 3,716 exchange rows for 26 to 28 August. On Monday, five of the twelve biggest movers had no cause anyone could point to. **And the best corroboration is that the exchange could not establish one either.** It formally asked NMDC Steel why its volume had spiked; the company answered that nothing new had happened; the share then rose another 8.96%. When the exchange asks the question and the company says there is no answer, "cause not established" stops being one desk's limitation and becomes a description of the market.

**An unexplained move is only a finding if the search was real, and two things say it was.** We name what was checked before writing the blank: filings, dated broker actions, index and derivatives events, and search. And we keep catching plausible explanations that turn out to be stale, which is the trap a reader will personally fall into. Four were caught in one session on Thursday: INOX India's ₹939 crore order aggregate is dated 8 July; Whirlpool's stake-sale talks collapsed in December 2025; NMDC Steel's rating upgrade is dated 21 August; Apar's 77.8% profit jump is dated 18 August. Two more on Friday: a search result reported an 8% jump in Newgen Software at a price of ₹1,316 when the share closed at ₹567.10, so it is an old article re-dated by the search layer, and a ₹110 crore titanium-order story attached to PTC Industries carries a 2025 dateline. On Monday a year-old article about Yatharth Hospital's rally was killed on its date rather than published as a reason.

**And there is a size test a reader can apply without any data at all.** Coromandel International fell 3.48% on Monday, and the only document in the window was a ₹1.24 crore tax order plus a ₹12.35 lakh penalty. That cannot move a ₹1,926 share by 3.5%. Aegis Logistics fell 5.39% on a ₹11,62,128 tax penalty, at a company worth ₹47,371 crore. **If the number in the explanation is thousands of times smaller than the company, it is not the explanation.**

### Elsewhere in the corporate week

**Eight shares fell on Friday for a reason that is not a market reason.** When a company pays a dividend it hands out cash, and there is a cut-off date after which a new buyer no longer receives that payment. On the first morning past the cut-off the share is worth exactly the dividend less than the night before, because the cash has left the company. The price on the screen drops and nothing has gone wrong. **The value did not vanish. It moved from the share price into the shareholder's bank account.** Cantabil, Honasa, MCX, Mufti, NBCC, Poly Medicure, Vardhman Textiles and Whirlpool all passed that cut-off on Friday. The arithmetic is the point: Whirlpool's raw fall of 3.32% was really 2.74%, Honasa's 2.93% was really 2.32%, and **Mufti, which a raw screen shows falling 2.55%, did not move at all.** The correction runs both ways: Whirlpool rose 9.00% on Thursday with no cause established, and its ₹5 dividend, worth about 0.6% of the price, was correctly sized and correctly rejected as an explanation. We do not ingest the exchange's corporate-actions record, which is why six of the eight needed archived filings to resolve and why MCX remains supported rather than confirmed.

**Institutions voted against controlling shareholders at seven companies in nine days, and were selective about it.** At Gland Pharma about 45% and 47% of institutional votes went against re-appointing two directors nominated by the controlling shareholder, on 92.5% turnout, and the controlling shareholder's 51.76% carried them anyway. Red Tape drew 28.82% against a managing-director appointment on 98% turnout, Nykaa 27.88% on 91.5%, Sagility 61.91% against a director, Ather Energy 29.50%, and Lumax Auto Technologies 29.14% against a resolution authorising lending to entities in which its directors are interested, in a document where counterparty, amount and pricing are all absent. Jay Bharat Maruti drew **100% against the chairman's pay, on 6.11% turnout**, and that turnout must be printed in the same breath, because **a percentage without its turnout is not information**: 28.82% on 98% turnout is the heavier number of the two.

**What makes it a story is what those same investors approved.** Accounts, auditors, dividends and ordinary related-party transactions passed with negligible opposition in the same ballots; at Gland, the institutions casting 45% to 47% against two directors voted 100% for the accounts and the dividend. **This is not a protest against everything. It is a targeted refusal, aimed at the resolutions where the controlling family stands to benefit.** Two controls were run on it. Four of the five landed within thirty hours because a two-working-day filing clock discharged after a common meeting date, so annual meeting season explains the timing and not the content. And Lumax Industries, the sister company under the same controlling group, recorded 8.17%, which makes the 29.14% a judgement about one resolution rather than about a family.

**Two companies transferred assets to a connected party in one week, and neither document contained a valuation.** Waaree Renewable Technologies told the exchange at 18:29 on Thursday that its board had approved transferring its entire shareholding in a wholly owned subsidiary to Waaree Energies, which is both its holding company and its controlling shareholder. The stated price is ₹1,00,000, at a face value of ₹10 a share; the pricing basis offered is the subsidiary's contribution to turnover and net worth last year, given as nil or negligible; the subsidiary had just been renamed to Waaree Green Data Centers; and the word valuation appears nowhere in the document. Separately, Aegis Logistics sold a ₹525 crore ammonia terminal to its own step-down subsidiary fourteen days after commissioning it, with no external valuation and a one-sentence definitional claim that the shareholder-approval provision does not apply because the terminal is not an "undertaking".

**Both filings are compliant on their face and both declared the connected-party status openly.** Neither is alleged to break a rule and neither is described here as wrongdoing. The finding is a specific thing absent from two documents in five sessions, which is a statement about the documents. **When both sides of a sale sit inside the same group, no market sets the price, so the valuation is the only evidence an outside reader has, and in these two it is not there.** The market did nothing with either: Waaree Renewable closed up 0.43% through a full session with the document public.

**And one competitive event with a date attached.** Ola Electric launched the S1Z scooter at ₹79,999 during Friday's session, with the S1Z Plus at ₹99,999 and deliveries from December 2026, roughly 20% to 35% below the ₹1.0 lakh to ₹1.25 lakh bracket the market expected a rival launch to occupy. **We are not characterising that as a price war, and the reason is in section 8:** the sources disagree with each other about what the rival scooter will actually cost, so the bracket being undercut is not itself settled. The launch, the price and the timing are facts. What they add up to is not yet one. The range figures quoted around it are the manufacturer's own test-cycle claims and are not repeated here as facts.

## 8. What to watch next week

### Monday 31 August is four things landing in one session

**A growth number before the open, an index reshuffle transacting in the closing auction, a foreign-currency funding window shutting, and the market's first chance to price a product event held over the weekend.** A fifth item, a 15-day auction to absorb surplus bank cash, sits in the same day. That is worth saying in advance because it is much harder to say afterwards. **When Monday's number appears, nobody will be able to say cleanly which of the four caused it, and anyone who tells you they can is guessing.**

**India's April to June national accounts** are scheduled for Monday. This is the single number saying how fast the economy actually grew in the three months to June, against 7.8% in the March quarter. **Carry the forecasts as a spread, because there is no consensus to collapse them into.** India Ratings is at 6.9%, the Reserve Bank at 7.0%, Bank of Baroda at 7.0 to 7.2%, a 58-economist poll median near 7.1%, CareEdge at 7.3%, and SBI Research alone at 8.0%. **The cluster between 6.9% and 7.3% would be a four-quarter low. The 8.0% outlier would be an acceleration. Those are not the same story and no published median reconciles them.** The stated causes behind the low cluster are a delayed monsoon onset and softer services.

**There is a trap inside the release itself.** The same publication switches the industrial-production and price indices to a revised 2022-23 base year, so some series will not be directly comparable with what came before. The yardstick changes in the same document as the measurement. **A number that looks like a jump or a drop against last quarter may be partly the new yardstick.**

**The MSCI August review transacts at Monday's close**, with the new composition applying from 1 September. Adani Energy Solutions, Billionbrains Garage Ventures, Laurus Labs and Lenskart Solutions join; Astral, Balkrishna Industries and SBI Cards and Payment Services leave. **Funds whose whole job is to hold exactly what the index holds do not get an opinion.** When the index changes they must buy the joiners and sell the leavers, in the closing auction, because that is the price the index will use. That is mechanical buying and selling by people with no view, which is why it can move a share on a day when nothing about the company changed.

**We are printing no single figure for how much money that moves, and the reason is instructive.** Four estimates are circulating and they measure different things. Roughly US$600 million covers only the joining shares; US$2.3 to 3.2 billion covers the whole review; one desk's figure of over US$1.5 billion carries no scope at all. And one house's per-stock estimates sum to about US$1.5 billion for the four additions alone, which makes the "roughly US$600 million" line look like one share's inflow rather than four. **Every one of those estimates is dated about 13 August and none has been refreshed. They are not a range of the same quantity. They are different quantities that got compared.**

**The Reserve Bank's concessional foreign-currency deposit window shuts on Monday, and it is three dates rather than one.** These are dollar accounts Indians living abroad hold with Indian banks. The central bank ran a temporary scheme letting banks swap those dollars into rupees on favourable terms, which made chasing the deposits attractive; it worked fast enough that the deadline was pulled forward from 30 September on 14 August. **New deposits stop on 31 August, swaps against already-eligible deposits run to 11 September, and the related borrowing scheme runs to 31 December. The scheme does not end on Monday. One of its three legs does.** Why it matters: reserves have risen four weeks running, the latest by US$12.42 billion to US$729.33 billion, and that build is largely why reserves rose about US$34.5 billion in three weeks while the rupee sat near three-week lows. **Swap-funded, not strength.** When the window closes, the marginal dollar for bank funding gets more expensive, and that reaches lenders before it reaches anything else.

### The imported input Monday has to absorb

**The American central bank's Jackson Hole keynote landed around 19:30 India time on Friday, after our market had closed, so it explains nothing in this brief.** It is Monday's input. Kevin Warsh, in his first keynote as Chair, said inflation is running above the 2% target and the Fed's predominant focus should be on prices; that summer readings came in better than expected but do not tell him the underlying trend has meaningfully improved; and that the Fed must be confident inflation is moving toward its objective clearly and at sufficient speed, "otherwise, we have work to do". **He declined to commit to any forward guidance.** American inflation is 3.4% and the 30-year American government bond yield is near its highest since 2007.

**Why an Indian reader should care.** Markets here have been positioned for American rates to come down. A Fed chair saying he may have to put them up is the opposite setting. India does not set global rates and imports the consequences anyway, through the rupee and through what foreign investors do with Indian bonds. **Higher American rates make American assets more attractive relative to Indian ones.** We are printing no probability figure for a September rate rise: the number in circulation could not be checked at source and a pre-speech reading of it was very different.

### The rest of the calendar

- **Tuesday 1 September:** monthly vehicle sales, the first read on whether the electric two-wheeler price war is shifting anything, landing two days after a new sub-₹1.25 lakh scooter reveal and three days after a two-wheeler maker raised its stake in an electric-scooter maker. Also the weekly Nifty settlement, and a sugar stockholding limit for bulk buyers taking effect and running to 30 November.
- **Thursday 3 September:** the Hero MotoCorp purchase of Ather shares completes. A secondary purchase from an existing shareholder; no new capital enters the company.
- **Friday 4 September:** the Reserve Bank's weekly statistical release, and whether a fifth consecutive reserve build arrives. This edition covers the week the deposit window shut, so it is the first read on what that window raised. **A standing caution travels with the number: a rising reserve pile is not evidence of the central bank defending the rupee. That would show as a fall.**
- **Monday 7 September:** a scheduled change to the exchange's auction sessions takes effect. **Our own wording and our outward check disagree about what changes on that date**, and neither outward source clears our source bar, so we name the date and not the content until the exchange's own circular settles it.
- **The comment window on the Reserve Bank's draft rules for non-bank lenders closed on Friday 28 August.** Say the word draft again. A closed comment window is the end of consultation, not the start of obligation. **Nothing was enacted and nothing binds anybody.** That is exactly the moment a reader starts assuming a rule exists. Separately, seven Reserve Bank supervisory actions landed on non-bank lenders and co-operative banks in the six business days ending on that deadline, six fines and one set of Directions restricting what a bank may do. **That timing is an observation, not a claim of cause.**

### One event we deliberately cannot report

**Ather Energy's Community Day was scheduled for Saturday 29 August, after every brief in this week was written, with a new scooter expected.** As of this writing **no dated report of the outcome exists in anything we can reach.** Every retrievable source is pre-event copy written in the future tense, two of them headlined "to launch tomorrow", and the manufacturer's own model page says the scooter has not been launched and no official price has been announced. **The sources disagree with each other on the expected price, which is itself the proof that no official price exists.**

**A search summary will tell you confidently that it launched, at a price. That is pre-event copy rewritten in the past tense.** We do not know the price, the specification or the reception, and Monday 31 August is the first Indian session that can price any of it. **Friday's Ather move was the block deal, not the scooter.**

And one observation about the price of insurance, which is not a forecast. India VIX closed at 10.68 on Friday, 1.04% above its own 52-week low, going into a weekend containing a growth release, an index rebalance, a policy deadline and a product event. **The market is charging close to nothing to insure against a surprise, into a weekend with four of them in it.**

## 9. What we could not explain, and where we got it wrong

Last week's brief published ten things to watch and promised that a watch list published in a weekly gets graded in the next weekly. This is the first grade under that rule, so the rule is on trial as much as the list.

### What worked

**Four items fired and were covered properly.** The monthly derivatives settlement showed up exactly as described in advance, an index rising on falling breadth on Tuesday and reversing on Wednesday. The Jio Financial shareholder meeting fired and the reason for watching held: over four sessions the share moved minus 0.82%, minus 0.88%, minus 1.20% and plus 0.13%, which is a market pricing a ₹7,200 crore related-party authorisation, worth about twice a year's revenue, at approximately nothing. The Jackson Hole slot was pinned in advance, correctly dated, and confirmed at the central bank's own page. The comment deadline on the draft non-bank lender rules fired and turned up something better than the item anticipated. **And a correction we issued last week held all week**: we had written that the index review is effective 1 September, and corrected that the date that matters is the close on Monday 31 August. Every brief that carried it this week stated it that way.

### The miss, and it is the one that matters

**Last week's brief flagged three companies to watch on Monday: Lloyds Luxuries, Manipal Hospitals and Winsome Interiors.** They had filed results on Friday 21 August, we published no brief that day, and Monday was the first session that could price them. The brief added a sentence which now convicts it: **it said it was flagging these precisely because they were "the same shape as the miss above."**

**None of the three appears anywhere in any of this week's seven briefs.** A full-text search of all of them returns zero hits for each name.

So the sequence is this. We identified a specific failure mode, which is pointing readers at named reactions and then never reporting them. We named it. We wrote a new item in exactly that shape. We warned in writing that it was the same shape. **And then we reproduced it exactly.** This is the second consecutive weekly in which a pointed reaction went unreported, and the first in which it was pre-flagged. **Naming a failure was not a control against it.** The check is three ticker lookups in a file this desk builds every evening anyway.

**Two more items were quietly dropped and never graded.** A reported American trade delegation due in New Delhi, carried with the correct warning that it was single-sourced and unconfirmed, appears in no brief after the day it was raised. Nobody went back. **The failure is not flagging something unverified. It is flagging something unverified and never returning to it.** And one item was half-graded: we told readers this was the first monthly settlement since the regulator published evidence that retail participation in derivatives is shrinking, and then published nothing about whether that showed up. Half an item graded is not an item graded.

### An error in last week's brief that is still in print

**Last week's brief said a ₹36,000 crore figure circulating against the Jio Financial vote "traces to a May 2024 vote at a different company". That is wrong.** It traces to a May 2024 postal ballot at a **subsidiary in the same group**, concerning a different transaction in a different year.

So this is a correction of a correction, and the wrong version is the one that is published. The circulating number is still wrong and the figure to hold is still the ₹7,200 crore confirmed from the primary meeting notice. **But our stated reason for rejecting it was itself inaccurate.** The figure surfaced a third time in search results this week, dressed with extra detail about entity and years, which is why the trace was worth doing. **Added specificity is not added evidence.**

### Nine self-corrections in one week, across four briefs

We corrected ourselves more often than we were corrected, which is the right direction, and we corrected one thing three times in three days, which is not.

**The one to lead with is the clock.** Thursday's brief led on India having sat out the biggest company result in the world. Friday's brief corrected it in its own lead paragraph rather than a footnote, and did not disown the original reporting: **the reporting was right and the framing was wrong. There was never a lag. We measured against a press release, when the clock Indian technology shares track is the American close.** That is the model for how a correction should be handled.

**A second was reversed cleanly.** Monday's public brief said the central bank was draining cash for longer and terming out its absorption of surplus rupees rather than rolling it. Read directly off the central bank's own release index, the sequence ran overnight, three days, seven days, then two days on 25 August, then back to overnight. **The tenor reversed. We are not dropping that line quietly and we are not repeating it.** What is still not known is how much is being absorbed, because the individual result pages were not opened.

**And a third was itself withdrawn the next day, which should read as low confidence rather than as a fix.** A reader following the commodity question across this week was given three different reasons in three days. On Tuesday the crude move was real and not an artefact. On Thursday it was unpublishable because the two snapshots being compared were stamped nineteen hours and fifty-two minutes apart. On Friday that explanation was withdrawn, on the ground that moving the fetch later would not have fixed it, and replaced with a contract-roll fault breaking eighteen of nineteen commodity series. **Each step was an improvement and each was labelled. The net effect is that our confidence in our own commodity data this week should read as low.** We published no single-day change for any commodity from Wednesday onward, and our two Brent sources sat about a dollar apart on the same day. **The weekly direction and the two endpoint levels are solid. Treat the roughly 6% figure as approximate.**

**Three further corrections were issued on one night**: a self-manufactured claim that the growth forecasts were irreconcilable, which they are not; a framing that Sagility's 61.91% institutional dissent met a governance blackout, over-read from a search failure, so keep the number and retire the framing; and the crude item above. **Three more the next night**: a risk-off hypothesis refuted on intra-day breadth and restored on closing breadth, with the closing numbers governing; a continuity sweep that had been double-counting shares, 708 unique symbols and three failures rather than 1,410 and six; and the commodity reason later withdrawn.

### On one day, our two chains printed different states of knowledge

On Friday 28 August our private run said the Reserve Bank's weekly release had not resolved and left reserves two weeks stale at US$716,907 million as at 14 August. Our public brief the same calendar day carried a fresh US$729.33 billion for the week to 21 August. **Those are not contradictory numbers. They are different weeks.** The public brief was written on Saturday morning and had the figure; the private run the night before did not. **The promise was kept in one chain and failed in the other on the same day**, and a reader with access to only one was told two different things about the state of our own knowledge.

**A related disagreement, reported and not resolved.** India's 10-year government bond yield has been dark on our feed all week, on a seven-week outage the private chain describes as a dead source rather than an incident, and that chain published no yield at all. **The Friday public brief published a figure of around 6.9%, calling it a two-month high on a heavy supply calendar, graded honestly as reported and single-sourced from outside our pipeline. Both chains flagged their own grade honestly and reached opposite decisions on the same day.** Anywhere a bond yield appears in this brief it is press-sourced, not our measurement. **And our own outage counter went backwards**: the twenty-first consecutive dark run on 24 August, twenty-third on 26 August, twenty-fourth on 27 August, and twenty-third again on 28 August. That affects no market fact and it is still a same-measure disagreement inside our own record.

### The instruments that failed us

**Our weekly sector table was wrong for nine of eleven sectors, four of them sign reversals**, and it is the third consecutive week the same block has failed the same way. The recomputed table is in section 5. The reason is clean: the only two sectors it gets right are the only two whose indices resolved live all week; the other nine chain off a stale prior base while the current level is correct, so the level is right and the percentage is fiction. **The previous week's row carries a correction note. This week's was published without one.**
