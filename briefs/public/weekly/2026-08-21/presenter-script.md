# Presenter script: week ending Friday, 21 August 2026

*Read to camera. Weekend recording. Source: `briefs/public/weekly/2026-08-21.md`.*

---

## 1. Hook

The Nifty had a bad week. Most of the market did not.

That sounds like a contradiction, and it is the most useful thing that happened all week. India's headline index fell about half a per cent. The index of small companies rose. Mid-sized companies rose. Banks rose. And Indian institutions, mostly ordinary people's mutual fund money, bought into the fall.

So the fall was real, and it was a big-company fall, not a market fall. Let me show you how to see that for yourself.

## 2. Before we begin

Before we begin: this is general market commentary, not investment advice. I am not a SEBI-registered Research Analyst.

## 3. The week, and the one idea worth keeping

The Nifty 50 closed the week at 24,252, down 0.47 per cent. Three falling days, one rising day, and one day that did almost nothing.

Now here is the ordering that carries the whole story. We track seven headline indices and three of them went up. The Smallcap 250, which holds 250 smaller companies, rose about three quarters of a per cent. The Midcap 50 rose about a third. Bank Nifty, the twelve large banks, rose about half.

The four that fell are the four most crowded with giants. The Nifty 500, the broadest one we track, fell 0.27 per cent. The Nifty 50 fell 0.47. The Nifty 100 half a per cent. And the Sensex, thirty of the very largest companies, was worst of all at down 0.60.

Line them up and the pattern is clean. The more an index leans on the very biggest companies, the worse its week was. An index is weighted by company size, so a handful of enormous members can drag it down while hundreds of smaller ones quietly rise.

Two more facts belong here. Indian institutions bought hard, roughly eleven thousand six hundred crore rupees across three sessions, while foreign funds were close to flat. Those institutions are mutual funds, insurers and pension funds, so mostly ordinary people's monthly savings. And the index still fell.

The second fact cuts the other way. Of roughly seven hundred companies we price, 313 rose over the week and 385 fell. So the small-company index had the best week of the seven, and most individual companies still went down. A minority of larger members inside it did the lifting.

## 4. How the five days moved

Monday fell about a third of a per cent, and most of the market went up. One sector did nearly all the damage. Nifty IT, the big software exporters, fell 1.75 per cent, more than four times the benchmark, and we could not establish why.

Tuesday fell 0.55 per cent, the sixth straight down session, and we found no cause. The story on the wires was that crude is high and American bond yields are high. Both describe a level, not a move. Brent that day changed by one hundredth of a per cent.

Wednesday fell only a third of a per cent and was the worst day of the week for the ordinary company. Underneath the index, 477 companies fell and 225 rose, and the middle company was down half a per cent, against eight hundredths on Monday. The smallest fall, and the most damage. Crude rising for a fourth session explains the energy shares. The rest we cannot source.

Thursday rose 0.64 per cent and ended a seven-session losing run. That one was made in Washington.

Friday rose 0.08 per cent. The Sensex moved three points in a whole session. And I have to say this plainly: we published no brief for Friday. The price data is complete, so we know what happened. We do not know why.

## 5. Thursday was made in Washington

On Wednesday evening American time, the United States Treasury said it would at least double its buybacks of long-dated government bonds, from two billion dollars an operation to four. A buyback here just means the government buying its own older bonds back off the market.

American long-term interest rates fell on the news. The thirty-year yield dropped nine basis points, and a basis point is one hundredth of one per cent. When long-term rates fall, the profits a company will earn years from now are worth more in today's money, so share prices rise almost everywhere. Asia opened up on that alone.

India got the smallest slice of that rally in Asia, and the reason is oil.

One caveat. Those buybacks are not the Federal Reserve, not new money and not stimulus, and the enlarged operations do not begin until the ninth of September, three weeks away.

## 6. Oil went up every single session

Brent crude, the benchmark most of the world's oil trades against, rose from about eighty-nine dollars on Monday to about ninety-four by Friday, up every single session. That is just under six per cent, and India buys nearly all the oil it burns.

Why did it rise? A shipping story. The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively shut since late February, and eight attacks on ships were reported in August.

Now the part that surprises people. Our energy index fell while oil was rising. Nifty Energy holds refiners and gas distributors. They buy crude. They do not sell it. So a rising oil price is a cost to them, not a windfall.

## 7. This was not an earnings week. It was the paperwork after one

The June-quarter filing deadline had already passed. Our feed lists forty-three companies with an earnings-related filing this week, and almost all of it is compliance paperwork: newspaper copies of results already filed, transcripts of calls already held, delay notices.

So what moved prices was not new numbers. It was the market finishing its reading of numbers it already had. Some of the week's biggest moves came from a document containing no new figure at all.

Start with what was bought and deserved to be. Ipca Laboratories, a drugmaker, rose 8.67 per cent on Monday to an all-time high on nearly eighteen times its normal volume. Its June quarter had revenue up 21 per cent and net profit up 72 per cent. When profit grows nearly three times as fast as sales, a company is keeping more out of every rupee it sells rather than simply selling more.

Now the other direction, and this is the one to remember. NGL Fine-Chem fell nearly sixteen per cent on Monday, in the last twenty-eight minutes of trading, because the company explained its own profit margin, which is how many rupees out of every hundred of sales it gets to keep. In an investor presentation filed minutes before the close, management said part of that margin improvement came from inventory gains, meaning stock bought cheaply and sold after input prices rose. Real profit, but a one-off the business cannot repeat. Nothing about the reported numbers changed. It told the market which parts of a good-looking profit were not coming back, and the market took them out.

Engineers India, a state-owned engineering consultancy, is the same lesson from the other side. It fell on Wednesday on a headline of profit up 141 per cent. Revenue actually fell, and more than half that profit rise came from other businesses it part-owns but does not run.

## 8. The movers, risers first

Twenty-two companies moved more than six per cent this week. First, the number that matters most about them. Nine have no cause anywhere in our briefs. Eight more are explained only in part. Five have a complete, dated explanation. I would rather give you that ratio than five tidy stories.

The clean one is Balrampur Chini, a sugar mill. On the nineteenth the government cut how much sugar bulk buyers may hold, to fifteen days of use. That kind of limit is meant to push prices down, and it binds buyers, not mills. Investors read it the other way, as the government confirming the price had run far enough to need intervention. The share rose nearly eighteen per cent on Thursday, its biggest day in four years.

One riser is not really a measurement. Menon Bearings is one of the nine with no cause in our briefs, and it closed at or just under the five per cent daily limit on all four days we observed. That limit is the exchange's cap on how far a share can move in a session, so a stock pinned against it stops trading before the price clears. Its nineteen and a half per cent is the least it could have been.

The worst faller was Ahluwalia Contracts, a builder, on sales up twelve per cent and net profit down eighty per cent. That is not a demand problem. In construction the customer's price is agreed in advance, so unexpected costs come straight out of the builder's own margin.

Across sectors nothing was dramatic. The one to know is consumer goods, down 2.27 per cent, squeezed because wholesale prices are rising at nearly twice the pace of shop prices.

## 9. What to watch next week

Tuesday the twenty-fifth is the monthly derivatives expiry. Contracts settle on the last Tuesday of the month, so a large block has to be closed or rolled whether anybody's view has changed or not. Unusual volume that day can be about settlement rather than about anyone deciding anything.

Friday the twenty-eighth, comments close on the Reserve Bank's draft rules for non-bank lenders. Say the word draft. It is a proposal, it obliges nobody, and on Saturday it will still not be law. It is on this list because a document that binds no one has already taken about thirty-three thousand crore rupees off the value of one lender.

And Monday the thirty-first carries three things at once. India's national accounts for April to June, on a revised base year, so the comparison basis itself changes. The MSCI index change taking effect at the close. And the Reserve Bank's dollar-swap window stopping new deposits, which has been quietly bringing foreign currency in and supporting the rupee.

## 10. What we could not explain

A normal week produces ten of our daily briefs. This week produced six, covering four sessions, and none at all for Friday. The price data is complete. The coverage is not. Those are two different claims.

Friday held the largest single-company move of the week. It also held the Reserve Bank's weekly reserves release, which we had named in advance as the test of whether the rupee is now being held up out of the reserves. I cannot tell you what either one did.

Then two corrections to our own work. We told readers the dollar-swap window shuts on the thirtieth of September. The correct date is the thirty-first of August. And our own weekly sector calculation came back wrong. It reported Auto as the best sector of the week, when Auto actually fell. Eight of its ten figures were wrong, and four had the sign reversed. We rebuilt every sector by hand from our saved daily files. I would rather tell you we found a defect than quietly publish the fix.

And our government bond curve returned nothing all week, which removed the most natural way to test several of this week's stories.
