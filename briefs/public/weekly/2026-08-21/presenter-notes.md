## Block 1: the hook

**The mechanism behind the claim.** An index is a weighted average, not a headcount. Each member counts in proportion to its market value, so the largest few companies dominate the number. That is why the Nifty could fall while the small-company index rose. Nothing contradictory happened; two different measuring instruments were pointed at two different parts of the same market.

**Likely question: "so did the market go up or down?"** Both, and which one you mean depends on what you own. The headline index fell. Three of the seven indices we track rose. Of roughly seven hundred individual companies, 313 rose and 385 fell over the week.

**Likely question: "why does the mutual fund money matter?"** Domestic institutional flows are mostly monthly savings arriving on a schedule, through systematic plans, insurance premiums and pension contributions. That money buys largely regardless of the news, which is why it can be large and steady while the index still falls.

## Block 5: the Washington buyback

**One level deeper.** A Treasury buyback is the government repurchasing its own older, thinly traded bonds to relieve congestion at the long end of the market. It is debt management, not monetary policy. The Federal Reserve is not involved and no new money is created. The programme goes from US$2 billion to at least US$4 billion an operation, running 9 September to 4 November.

**Why falling long rates lift share prices.** A share is a claim on profits that arrive in future years. To compare a rupee arriving in 2031 with a rupee today you discount it, and the discount rate keys off long-term interest rates. Lower long rates mean a smaller discount, so distant profits are worth more today. That is a valuation effect and it applies to almost every market at once, which is why Asia rose together.

**The Asia comparison, if asked.** Korea between 5.5 and 6 per cent, Japan between 1.16 and 1.3, Hong Kong around 1.1, India 0.64. Oil and gas was India's only sector to close red that day.

**Three popular explanations for Thursday that we checked and killed.** Foreign buying: no published figure existed. The rupee: it firmed 0.13 per cent on a day the dollar index itself fell 0.14, the same size, so the rupee contributed nothing of its own. Derivatives expiry: weekly settlement on this exchange has been on Tuesdays since September 2025.

**Flag, not fact.** One report has the American 30-year yield's fall already unwinding later that day, after India had closed. It is single-sourced. Present it as something to watch if it comes up at all.

**Do not say** the buybacks are stimulus, or that the Federal Reserve did anything this week.

## Block 6: oil, and the energy index

**Precise figures.** Brent rose from US$89.01 on Monday to US$94.19 on Friday, which is 5.8 per cent across those five closes. Our data feed says 6.41 per cent because it measures from a different starting point. Both describe the same move; we quote the one a viewer can check.

**The mechanism, one level deeper.** Nifty Energy holds refiners and gas distributors, who buy crude as an input. India imports roughly 85 per cent of its oil by sea. So a rising crude price is a cost line for that index. On Wednesday, the fourth consecutive up-session in crude, energy was among the two worst sectors at about down 1.2 per cent, and Deep Industries fell 7.05. Energy finished the week down 0.78 per cent.

**The shipping premium, cut from the script and worth having ready.** Brent is priced on seaborne cargoes; the American WTI benchmark on oil already sitting inland in the United States. The gap between them widened to about US$7.16 a barrel, so the extra money is being paid for the journey rather than for the oil. That is a freight and risk problem, not a demand boom. Supporting facts: the Strait of Hormuz has been effectively shut since 28 February, eight attacks on ships were reported in August, and on 19 August the United Arab Emirates suspended all trade and banking with Iran, its second-largest commercial partner.

**Likely question: "does higher oil help oil companies?"** It helps producers who sell crude. It hurts refiners and distributors who buy it. This index is mostly the second kind.

## Block 7: the paperwork week

**Why we insist it was not an earnings week.** The June-quarter filing deadline had passed. Our exchange feed lists 43 companies with an earnings-related filing, and searching every one of them across all six of our briefs produced a single mention, which was about a company joining an index rather than about any result. The filings are newspaper-publication copies, transcripts of calls already held on 10 to 12 August, press releases, delay notices and clarifications.

**Three words to have straight if a viewer asks.** Revenue is what a company sold. Operating profit is what is left after the costs of running the business, before interest and tax, and the operating margin is that profit as a percentage of revenue. Net profit is what survives after interest, tax and everything else.

**Ipca, fuller.** Revenue up 21 per cent, operating profit up 61, net profit up 72, with branded medicines up 16, exported drug ingredients up 33 and domestic drug ingredients up 20. Rose 8.67 per cent on Monday to an all-time high on 17.8 times normal volume.

**NGL Fine-Chem, fuller.** The stock opened at a one-year high. At 15:02, twenty-eight minutes before the close, it filed an investor presentation. Management disclosed that the quarter's 6.2 percentage point margin improvement was partly inventory gains, and that a ₹7.0 crore currency swing worth about 29 per cent of pre-tax profit "may not recur". It fell 15.80 per cent that day on sixteen times normal volume, and 14.61 per cent across the week.

**Why an inventory gain is not repeatable.** The company bought raw material at one price, input prices then rose, and it sold the finished product against the higher price. The profit is real cash, but it depends on the direction of input prices in that particular quarter, so it tells you nothing about next quarter.

**Engineers India, fuller.** Net profit rose 141 per cent to ₹157.94 crore while revenue fell 5.8 per cent, and ₹49.88 crore of the ₹92.54 crore profit increase, 54 per cent of it, came from joint ventures and associates. Those are businesses the company part-owns but does not run, and they are added well below the line where its own consultancy work is recorded. The transcript went out at 16:11 on Tuesday, after the close, so Wednesday was the first session anybody could trade against it.

**Two more examples held in reserve.**
- *Ahluwalia Contracts*: sales up 12.03 per cent to ₹1,125.81 crore, consolidated net profit down 79.71 per cent to ₹10.39 crore. Operating margin fell from about ₹5.10 in every hundred rupees of sales to about 90 paise. The result was filed after the close on Friday 14 August, the call recording went up after the close on Monday, and the market delivered its verdict on Tuesday. In construction, fixed-price contracts mean the customer's payment is agreed in advance, so every rupee of unexpected cost comes out of the builder's own margin.
- *IRCTC*: revenue up 18.1 per cent to ₹1,370 crore, operating profit down 2.77 per cent, margin down 5.1 points to 28.17. Internet ticketing, which handles 89 per cent of India's reserved rail bookings and keeps above eighty rupees in every hundred it earns, grew 0.5 per cent. Catering grew 33.8 per cent and is now 53 per cent of revenue, and management named catering as the thing diluting the margin. Parent company figures only.
- *Blue Star and Voltas*: same quarter, same product, same weather. Blue Star's room-air-conditioner margin halved from 5.8 to 2.9 per cent with pre-tax profit down 23.7. Voltas expanded its core cooling margin from 3.7 to 5.3 per cent on volumes up 45. One sell-side desk puts industry volume growth at 20 to 22 per cent. Two firms cannot both be victims of the same input costs when one expands its margin on 45 per cent volume growth. Handle as a competitive observation, never as a view on either share.

**The good news that ran under the week.** Where companies disclosed order books, the order books were healthy. An order book is work won and not yet performed, so it is future revenue. Genus Power carries ₹24,020 crore, roughly four and a half years of its revenue. Asian Energy Services ₹1,754 crore with guidance unchanged. Engineers India has taken ₹2,750 crore of new work this year against an ₹8,000 crore annual target. Demand was not this quarter's problem.

**The timing point, if the conversation goes there.** Continuous trading ends at 15:15 and the closing auction at 15:35. A document published after that cannot have moved that day's closing price; it becomes the next session's cause. Several of this week's filings landed at night or in the last half hour. We allege nothing. The disclosure clock and the trading clock are simply not the same clock.

## Block 8: the movers

**The ratio, exactly.** Twenty-two companies moved more than 6 per cent. Nine have no cause anywhere in our briefs, eight are explained only in part, five carry a complete, dated, sourced explanation. Not one of the twenty-two filed results this week.

**Balrampur Chini, fuller.** On 19 August the Centre cut the sugar stockholding limit for bulk buyers to fifteen days of use, for anyone using more than ten tonnes a month, in force 1 September to 30 November. The share rose 17.87 per cent on Thursday, its biggest day in more than four years, with the sugar industry up as much as 18. Ex-mill sugar is ₹5,400 to ₹5,500 a quintal against roughly ₹3,900 a year ago. The industry was already in its third rising session before the order landed, which is why we call the reading backwards rather than wrong.

**Why a stockholding limit is a price-suppressing tool.** It caps how much a buyer may hoard, which is meant to release supply into the market and push prices down. It binds buyers, not the mills that produce sugar. Investors treated the order as official confirmation that prices had already run far, which is the opposite of its intent.

**Price bands, one level deeper.** The exchange sets a maximum daily move for many shares. Once a share reaches the band it can no longer trade beyond it, so the demand that is still unfilled simply does not print. Menon Bearings closed at or just under the 5 per cent band on all four of Monday to Thursday, and Baazar Style Retail did the same on three. The printed weekly number is a floor on the demand, not a measurement of it.

**Risers to have ready.** Asian Energy Services up 25.61 per cent (results filed 13 August, revenue up about 135, and our own brief refused to date the move to them). Welspun Corp up 23.41, cause not established, and Friday alone is 65 per cent of it. Indo Borax up 20.34, fully sourced: it told the exchange at 12:15 on Thursday it had agreed, jointly with a promoter company, to buy 64.26 per cent of Kronox Lab Sciences for ₹246.12 crore. Aegis Logistics up 12.05, where the exchange asked it to clarify a report of talks to buy a UAE business.

**Fallers to have ready.** Himadri Speciality Chemical down 16.22, cause not established. Silver Touch down 11.11, no filings at all in the window. Schneider down 11.09, the whole week is one Monday. Thyrocare down 9.37, fully sourced and instructive: on Wednesday 7.95 crore of its shares were released from pledge and it fell 4.23 per cent; on Thursday a shareholder's ₹1,700 crore debentures were fully redeemed, releasing a pledge over 60.92 per cent of the equity, and it fell a further 2.71. A pledge is a lender's lien over shares put up as security. Removing it means the borrowing was repaid, which is good news, and it also removes the constraint on a large holder who might want to sell, which is a supply problem for everybody else.

**One thing we deliberately did not do.** Welspun Corp and Welspun Living are separately listed companies in the same group and both are largely unexplained. Two related companies rising together is a co-movement worth noticing and it is not an explanation of either. Do not offer it as one.

**The FMCG squeeze, one level deeper.** July wholesale inflation was 9.78 per cent and was widely reported as easing. It eased only because fuel and power fell from 27.41 to 20.05, which is an arithmetic effect of comparing against an expensive month a year earlier. Underneath, what factories charge at their own gate accelerated 0.81 of a point to 8.29 per cent and raw materials rose 1.52 points to 8.52, while what households paid in the shops rose 4.45. That gap of roughly 3.8 points is absorbed by whoever sits in the middle and cannot raise prices. The honest limit: no brief attributes any single consumer-goods session to this. The mechanism is sourced; the day-by-day attribution is not.

## Block 9: next week

**Expiry.** Monthly contracts settle on the last Tuesday of the month. This is also the first monthly expiry since the regulator published evidence that retail participation in derivatives is shrinking.

**The regulator's study, if you want the extra beat.** In the year to March 2026, 87.7 per cent of individual traders lost money, ₹91,685 crore net against about ₹1.12 lakh crore the year before, and the number of unique active traders fell 18 per cent to 87.5 lakh, the first contraction in a decade. Put those together and the improvement disappears: aggregate losses fell because fewer people were trading, not because more of them started winning. A second, narrower count inside the same document counts differently and we do not use it. Whether Friday priced any of this is not established in our briefs.

**Jio Financial's meeting.** 14:00, by video. The related-party approvals total ₹7,200 crore, about 205 per cent of last year's consolidated revenue, split across three annexures in a 143-page notice that never adds them up. Nothing unlawful is alleged and none is implied. What makes it watchable is the mismatch: a sum worth roughly twice a year's revenue against a share price that moved 0.60 per cent six days before the vote. A ₹36,000 crore figure circulating against this item traces to a May 2024 vote at a different company; do not repeat it.

**The two drafts, kept separate.** The first, published earlier in August, would as reported stop non-bank lenders offering revolving credit; comments close 28 August. A non-bank lender is a finance company that lends without a banking licence, so it cannot take public deposits and funds itself in the markets. Revolving credit is a standing limit a borrower can draw, repay and draw again without a fresh application, which is how a credit card works, as against a term loan, which is a fixed sum on a fixed schedule. Three things remain open: whether the restriction survives, whether the credit-card exemption widens, and whether a transition period appears. The second draft, published 12 August, would change how every regulated lender sets loan rates: a maximum three-month reset on floating-rate loans from 1 April 2027, and a ceiling on the all-in annual rate on small loans, where all-in means the total cost including fees, not the headline rate. Comments close 11 September. One restricts a product; the other touches every loan book in the country. Merging them is the mistake to avoid. Our source is press summaries, not the documents themselves, so say "as reported".

**A reset, glossed.** A floating-rate loan tracks a published reference rate, and the reset is when your lender recalculates against it. Slow resets mean you keep paying the old higher rate for months after the reference rate falls. Capping the gap at three months means a cut reaches you faster, and so does an increase.

**MSCI.** Adani Energy Solutions, Billionbrains Garage Ventures, Laurus Labs and Lenskart Solutions go in. Astral, Balkrishna Industries and SBI Cards and Payment Services come out. We hold no estimate of how much money has to move and we are not inventing one.

**The swap window, if it comes up.** Indian banks take foreign-currency deposits from non-resident Indians and hedge them by swapping dollars for rupees with the Reserve Bank, with an agreement to reverse at a set rate on a set date. The Reserve Bank had been offering that swap at a discount, which made the whole deposit product cheaper to run, so banks pushed it and dollars came in. On 17 August it closed the window about six weeks early: new money ends 31 August rather than 30 September, with swaps executable to 11 September. Cumulative inflows had reached US$56.85 billion as of 13 August, of which US$52.3 billion came through those deposits. The stated reason is that inflows beat expectations and policymakers were wary of the effect on domestic cash conditions. That is a tap closed because it worked, not because it failed. The rupee closed the week 0.30 per cent weaker at 95.685.

**Also on the calendar, not in the script.** American July personal income and outlays on Wednesday, which contains the inflation measure the Federal Reserve targets, landing after India closes so India trades it on Thursday. Three regional Federal Reserve presidents dissented in favour of a rate increase in July, the first time three have done so the same way since 2016. And the Jackson Hole symposium runs Thursday to Saturday.

**Last week's scoreboard, if a regular viewer asks.** Our previous weekly was for the week ending 7 August; there was none for 14 August. It named eight things. Five turned out as flagged, one is open by design, one we cannot grade because we never went back to look, and one is a clean miss: we told readers Monday 10 August would price four post-close filers, and then published nothing on 10, 11 or 12 August. From here the baseline restarts. A watch list published in a weekly gets graded in the next weekly.

**Why owning this on camera is the right call.** A defect we found and fixed and disclosed is evidence the checking works. A defect discovered later by a viewer is evidence it does not. The brief already made this choice; the video follows it.

**Three plumbing failures, named.** We have no feed for income-tax and appellate orders, and no record of large negotiated share purchases, a gap our briefs name four separate times this week underneath a move they could not explain. And our screen for companies we do not otherwise track looks only at risers of 5 per cent or better, which is why a ₹29,000 crore company falling 11.47 per cent in a session could not appear in that day's brief at all.
