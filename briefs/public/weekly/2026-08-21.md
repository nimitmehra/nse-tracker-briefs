## 1. The week in plain English

The Nifty 50 finished the week at 24,252.00, down 0.47%. The shape of the week was three falls, one rise and one day that did almost nothing. Monday fell 0.32%, Tuesday fell 0.55%, Wednesday fell 0.32% and made it seven losing sessions in a row, Thursday rose 0.64% and ended the run, and Friday added 0.08%, which on the Sensex was a gain of three points in a full day of trading.

That headline number hides the more useful fact. **The fall was a big-company fall, not a market fall.** Three of the seven headline indices we track went up this week. The Nifty Smallcap 250, which holds 250 smaller companies, rose 0.74%. The Nifty Midcap 50 rose 0.33%. Bank Nifty, the twelve large banks, rose 0.47%. The three that fell are the three most crowded with giants: the Nifty 50 down 0.47%, the Nifty 100 down 0.50%, and the Sensex, the thirty largest companies on the Bombay exchange and the most concentrated of the lot, worst of all at down 0.60%.

Four things mattered this week, and each can be said in one sentence.

**Oil went up every single session, and India buys nearly all the oil it burns.** Brent crude, the benchmark price most of the world's oil trades against, rose from US$89.01 on Monday to US$94.19 by Friday, up 6.41% over the week and up five sessions running. For an economy that imports the overwhelming majority of its crude, that is a cost landing on everything at once: on the fuel bill, on transport, on the price of goods in the shops, and on the rupees that leave the country to pay for the barrels. It is the single thread that runs through the whole week.

**Thursday's rise was made in Washington, not in India.** On Wednesday evening American time, the United States Treasury said it would at least double its buybacks of long-dated government bonds, from US$2 billion to at least US$4 billion an operation, running from 9 September to 4 November. A buyback here simply means the government buying its own older bonds back off the market. American long-term interest rates fell on the news, the American 30-year yield by 9 basis points, a basis point being one hundredth of a percentage point. Lower long-term interest rates make the future profits of companies worth more today, so share prices rise nearly everywhere when they fall. Asia opened up on that alone.

**India got the smallest slice of that rally in Asia, and crude is the reason.** Korea's Kospi rose somewhere between 5.5% and 6% that day, Japan's Nikkei between 1.16% and 1.3%, Hong Kong's Hang Seng around 1.1%. India managed 0.64%, and oil and gas was the only sector to close red. The cleanest description of Thursday is that the rally came from Washington and the brake came from the Gulf.

**India's large software exporters fell hard for two days and we cannot tell you why.** The Nifty IT index, which holds the big software-services companies that earn in dollars, fell 1.75% on Monday and 1.93% on Tuesday, and was by a distance the worst sector of the week at down 2.63%. Two explanations were circulating and we checked both. A stronger rupee, which would shrink their dollar earnings when converted, is the wrong way round: the rupee actually weakened. A Jefferies downgrade of Infosys turned out to be dated February 2026, six months old. On Tuesday we searched again and found no dated reason at all, and we did not invent one. Then on Wednesday the same index went from the week's worst sector to the day's best, up 0.73%, and we cannot explain that either. That is three unexplained moves in one sector in three sessions, and Section 9 keeps them on the record rather than smoothing them over.

Two more facts belong up here before anything else.

**Indian institutions bought hard all week and the market still went down.** Domestic institutional investors, which is mostly Indian mutual funds, insurers and pension funds, and therefore mostly ordinary people's monthly savings, put in roughly ₹11,650 crore across the three sessions where our own briefs carry the figure. Foreign institutions, meaning overseas funds, were close to flat over the same three sessions, net sellers of about ₹476 crore, which on a market this size is nothing at all. Buying of that size absorbing selling of that size and the index still falling tells you the pressure was in the largest companies specifically.

**And we have no written record of Friday.** We publish a daily brief on the market; this week we published for Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday, and for Friday 21 August there is no brief of any kind. The price data for Friday is complete, so we know exactly what happened. We do not know why. That matters more than it sounds, because Friday held the week's biggest single stock move, the only meaningful jump in the market's fear gauge, and a session where the index rose while more companies fell than rose. Where we cannot source a cause below, the brief says so in plain words rather than reaching for a plausible one. Section 9 sets out the whole gap.

The explanation running on the wires was that Indian shares fell because crude is high and American long-term bond yields are rising. Both of those statements are true as descriptions of a level and neither is true as a description of a move. **Brent changed by 0.01% that day**, at US$90.86 on our feed and US$90.97 on an independent read, which is flat by any reading. Crude was indeed sitting about 9% above where it was on 7 August, and long American yields really were at their highest since 2007. But a level that has not changed cannot move a market on a Tuesday. Nor was the fall imported: Asia split in two, with Japan's Nikkei down 1.82%, Korea's Kospi down 1.55% and Taiwan down 1.10%, while Shanghai rose 1.61% and Hong Kong 1.41%, and the local-currency bond index India sits inside was flat at plus 0.02%. A regional flight out of risk looks nothing like that. The fall was made at home, and we could not say by whom or why.

**What Tuesday's index hides is that two government notifications organised the session and both cohorts rose against a falling market.** The electronics ministry cleared its first tranche of component-manufacturing projects, ₹7,877 crore across 31 projects, on letters dated 17 August. The defence ministry notified a sixth list of items that must be sourced from Indian suppliers, 405 of them, on 18 August. Paras Defence and Space Technologies closed 10% higher at a one-year high and Jyoti CNC Automation rose 8.30%. The arithmetic nobody else put next to that move is worth carrying: those 405 items carry an estimated ₹3,070 crore of business potential, that is a multi-year estimate spread across the entire defence public-sector supply chain, nothing has been awarded to anybody, and Paras Defence alone is worth roughly four times the whole figure. The direction of the news is real. The size of it is much smaller than the reaction.

**Wednesday 19 August fell only 0.32% and was by a distance the worst day of the week for the ordinary company.** Across our screened list of roughly 700 companies above a ₹1,000 crore size floor, 225 rose and 477 fell, and the middle company of the day was down 0.53%. On Monday the middle company was down 0.08%. So on the smallest of the three index falls, the market underneath fell about six times harder than it had on Monday. This is the clearest example this week of an index understating what actually happened.

Wednesday came in three parts and we are not going to weld them into one story, because they are not one story. First, Nifty IT went from the week's worst sector to the day's best, closing up 0.73%, and **we do not know why**. Two explanations were on offer and both fail: the rupee moved 0.05%, which is nowhere near enough to reprice a whole sector, and "buying at lower prices" describes a bounce rather than causing one. Second, and this part is explained, crude rose for a fourth straight session to US$91.74, up 0.79%, and Indian energy shares fell because of it. Nifty Energy was among the two worst sectors of the day at about down 1.18% and Deep Industries fell 7.05%. The mechanism is direct: that complex buys crude, it does not sell it. Third, India's defence shares fell roughly 1.5% to 1.6% and a dedicated search for a reason returned only articles dated 2024 and February 2026. Defence shares falling that hard days after a Gulf tanker attack is an open question, and it stays open.

The smallest companies were Wednesday's casualty, and it reversed Tuesday exactly. The Smallcap 250 was Tuesday's only green headline index and Wednesday's worst faller at down 0.63%, while technology went from worst to best in the same twenty-four hours.

**Thursday 20 August rose 0.64% and ended the seven-session losing run, on the American buyback announcement described in Section 1.** It was the week's only genuinely broad day and it was broad by a wide margin: 411 companies rose against 288 that fell on our screened list, the middle company rose 0.27%, the only positive median of the week, and across the whole exchange advances beat declines about three and a half to one, 2,148 to 600, on a live count taken during the session rather than at the close. Fifty-seven names in our roughly 700-company universe rose more than 3% and exactly one fell more than 5%.

Three popular explanations for Thursday were checked and killed, which is unusually many for one session. **Foreign buying** was written up by several desks and no published figure supports it, because on Thursday evening the only flow data in existence was Wednesday's; Section 3 explains why that is true every single day. **The rupee** firmed 0.13% to 95.695 per dollar on a day the dollar index itself fell 0.14%, and those are the same size, so the rupee contributed nothing of its own. **Derivatives expiry** cannot explain the 4.99% collapse in the fear gauge that day, because weekly settlement on the National Stock Exchange has been held on Tuesdays since September 2025.

There is one caveat on Thursday that almost nobody carried, and it is the honest one. Those Treasury buybacks are repurchases of older, thinly traded bonds meant to relieve congestion at the long end of the market. They are not the Federal Reserve, they are not new money, and they are not stimulus. **The enlarged operations do not begin until 9 September.** India rose on an announcement whose first practical effect is three weeks away. One report also has the American 30-year yield's fall already unwinding later that day, after India had closed, and that is single-sourced, so we flag it as the thing to watch rather than as a fact.

**Friday 21 August rose 0.08% and we have no brief covering it.** The Sensex moved 3.11 points in a full session, which is about as close to nothing as an index gets. Banks were the strongest of the seven indices for a second day running, up 0.46%. Underneath, 320 companies rose and 372 fell of the 703 we priced, and the middle company was down 0.08%, so the index closed higher while the typical company closed lower. A small number of larger members carried it. Which members, and why, is **not established in our briefs**, and the same sentence applies to every line of Friday's data, including the two sector figures that did come through, Nifty IT down 0.46% and Nifty Pharma down 0.21%.

What we can do honestly is name what was scheduled for Friday and say plainly that we do not know whether any of it was priced. ## 3. Breadth and flows: what the index was not telling you

**Breadth is a count, not an average.** It asks how many separate companies went up and how many went down, counting each one once. The index tells you what the biggest companies did, weighted by how big they are. When the two disagree, the disagreement is usually the more useful fact.

Here is the week, day by day, across our screened list of companies above a ₹1,000 crore size floor. The **middle company** is exactly that: half the list did better, half did worse. It is a fairer summary than an average, because one share up 18% cannot drag it.

**Positives first.** Thursday was the week's only broad day and it was broad by a wide margin, the only session in which the middle company finished higher.

**Wednesday is the one to notice.** The Nifty fell less on Wednesday than on Tuesday, and underneath it 477 companies fell against 225 that rose. Monday and Friday look identical on the middle company, both down 0.08%, and they are not the same session at all: on Monday the index was worse than the market, on Friday the index was better than the market. Same middle company, opposite relationship.

**Over the full week, 313 companies rose and 385 fell out of the 698 we priced.** That is 45 out of every 100 finishing the week higher. Put that next to Section 1 and the tension is the whole story of the week: **the Nifty Smallcap 250 had the best week of the seven indices, and most individual companies still went down.** Both are true, and they are true together because an index is weighted by company size, so a minority of larger members inside it can lift it while the rest drift lower. Anyone telling you small companies had a good week owes you that second half of the sentence.

For scale, the last weekly brief we published, for the week ending 7 August, had 427 companies up against 259 down. Participation narrowed sharply between the two. Section 9 explains why the two weeks cannot be read as a continuous trend.

### Where the money came from

Every trading day the exchanges publish two provisional net figures. **Foreign institutional investors** are overseas funds buying and selling Indian shares. **Domestic institutional investors** are Indian mutual funds, insurers and pension funds doing the same. "Net" is purchases minus sales, so a positive number is money coming in. These two pools are the largest identifiable buyers and sellers in the market, and they usually pull against each other.

**There is a hard limit on how fresh these numbers can ever be, and it explains a mistake several desks made this week.** The exchange publishes the figures at around 19:00 India time. Our own daily data run happens at about 17:00. So every evening brief carries the *previous* session's flows, never that day's. That is a publishing schedule, not a failure we can fix by trying harder, and it means **a flow number can never explain the session it is published after**. When several desks attributed Thursday's rally to foreign buying, the only flow figure in existence on Thursday evening was Wednesday's. The claim had nothing behind it.

**Domestic institutions bought on every session for which a figure exists, and they bought heavily**, putting in ₹11,654.49 crore net across Monday to Wednesday alone. **Foreign institutions were close to flat**, net sellers of ₹475.58 crore across the same three sessions, and their one large day was Monday's sale of ₹2,535.10 crore, by far the biggest single number of the week in either direction.

Set those two side by side. Indian savings institutions bought about eleven and a half thousand crore in three days, foreign funds sold about five hundred crore, and the index still fell 0.47% over the week. Buying that large absorbing selling that small without lifting the market is what it looks like when the pressure sits in a handful of the very largest companies, which is exactly what Section 2's index table shows.

Two honest limits. A figure for Thursday 20 August exists in our own data feed, but it was single-sourced and unconfirmed on the night and no brief ever verified it, so we are not printing it. For Friday there is no figure at all and there cannot be one, because it would only have been published the following Monday. And no brief this week states a cumulative weekly total; the three-session sums above are our own arithmetic on daily figures our briefs verified, which is why they are labelled as three sessions rather than as a week.

### The fear gauge

India VIX is a single number worked out from the prices of Nifty options. It estimates how much movement the options market expects in the index over the next thirty days. It is usually called the fear gauge because it rises when traders pay up for protection. **A low reading does not mean the market will be calm. It means nobody is currently paying much for insurance against it not being calm.**

It read 11.325 on Monday, 11.39 on Tuesday and 11.32 on Wednesday, which is to say it did essentially nothing across three consecutive falling sessions, sitting near the floor of its year and about 42% below where it stood three months earlier. **That is orderly selling rather than stress, and it also means nothing at all was priced for a break.** Then on Thursday, the one day the market rose, it collapsed 4.99% to 10.755. Our own market-data feed puts Friday at 11.195, up 4.04%, and no brief covers that session, so the rebound is on our record without an explanation attached to it.

One correction to our own work belongs here. Our private note for Thursday called 10.755 a fresh 52-week low. Our public brief for the same session refused to publish that and gave the reason: two independent data vendors put the one-year range at 8.72 to 28.91 against our own feed's 10.755 to 27.89, and both of our bounds sitting inside theirs points to a shortened lookback window rather than a genuine new low. Three sources also disagree on the level itself. **The fall is real. The record claim is not, and we are not making it.**

## 4. Earnings: the week the paperwork moved the prices

**This was not a reporting week, and saying so is the first honest thing about it.** The June-quarter filing deadline had passed. Our exchange feed lists 43 companies with an earnings-related filing this week, and when we searched every one of them across all six of our briefs we found a single mention, and that mention is about a company joining an index rather than about any result. The 43 are overwhelmingly compliance paperwork: newspaper-publication copies of results already filed, transcripts of calls already held on 10 to 12 August, press releases, delay notices and clarifications.

So what moved prices between Monday and Thursday was not new numbers. **It was the market finishing its reading of numbers it already had**, in the call transcript, the management commentary, the presentation footnote, the segment breakdown. Several of the week's largest single-session moves came from a document that contained no new figure at all.

Three words first, because the rest of this section leans on them. **Revenue** is what a company sold. **Operating profit** is what is left after the costs of running the business, before interest and tax, and the **operating margin** is that profit as a percentage of revenue, so it tells you how many rupees out of every hundred of sales the company actually keeps. **Net profit** is what survives at the very end, after interest, tax and everything else.

### What was bought, and deserved to be

**Ipca Laboratories rose 8.67% on Monday to an all-time high, on 17.8 times its normal trading volume.** The June quarter ran revenue up 21%, operating profit up 61% and net profit up 72% against the same quarter a year earlier, with branded medicines up 16%, exported drug ingredients up 33% and domestic drug ingredients up 20%. When profit grows nearly three times as fast as sales, the company is keeping more of every rupee it sells rather than simply selling more of them. That is the cleanest kind of good quarter and the market paid for it the same day.

**Tube Investments of India rose 7.94% on Tuesday, and the reason it rose on Tuesday and not earlier is the call.** Its results were approved on 14 August and the earnings call was held on Monday morning. The recording went up, and the share repriced on Tuesday. Revenue rose 17% to ₹6,215 crore, with engineering up 21%, mobility up 26% and metal-formed products up 12%. **We are not printing a profit figure for it**, because two public versions exist, one showing profit down 3% and another down 43%, and we could not establish which was comparing what. What we will stand behind is the shape: growth was double-digit in every segment rather than in one lucky division, which is the difference between a company growing and a company having a good quarter somewhere.

One genuine tailwind sits under this week's numbers and is worth holding on to. **Where companies disclosed their order books, the order books were healthy.** An order book is work a company has won and not yet performed, so it is future revenue rather than current revenue. Genus Power carries ₹24,020 crore, roughly four and a half years of its revenue. Asian Energy Services carries ₹1,754 crore with its full-year guidance unchanged. Engineers India has taken ₹2,750 crore of new work so far this year against an ₹8,000 crore annual target, which is on pace. **Demand was not this quarter's problem.**

### The three documents that repriced a company without changing a number

**NGL Fine-Chem lost 15.80% on Monday in the last twenty-eight minutes of trading, on sixteen times its normal volume, because the company explained its own margin.** The stock opened at a one-year high. At 15:02, twenty-eight minutes before the close, it filed an investor presentation. Inside it, management disclosed that the quarter's 6.2 percentage point margin improvement was partly inventory gains, meaning stock bought cheaply and sold after input prices rose, which is real profit but a one-time windfall the business cannot repeat, and that a ₹7.0 crore currency swing worth about 29% of pre-tax profit "may not recur". **Nothing about the reported numbers changed.** The company told the market which parts of a good-looking margin were not going to happen again, and the market removed them.

**Ahluwalia Contracts was the week's largest earnings casualty and it took two sessions to finish**, down 5.19% on Monday and 10.85% on Tuesday, down 15.14% over the week. On Tuesday it was the only faller in our entire mover set. June-quarter sales rose 12.03% to ₹1,125.81 crore and consolidated net profit fell 79.71% to ₹10.39 crore, from ₹51.21 crore. Its operating margin fell from about ₹5.10 in every hundred rupees of sales to about 90 paise. The sequence explains the timing exactly: the result was filed after the close on Friday 14 August, the recording of the management call went up after the close on Monday, and the market delivered its verdict on Tuesday. **The call made it worse, not better.** And revenue up 12% with profit down 80% is not a demand problem. It is a cost problem sitting below the revenue line, in a construction business where fixed-price contracts, meaning the customer's payment is agreed in advance so every rupee of unexpected cost comes out of the builder's own margin, decide whether there is anything left.

**Engineers India fell 3.22% on Wednesday on a headline profit rise of 141%**, and this is the cleanest good-number-bad-reception case of the week. Net profit rose 141% to ₹157.94 crore while **revenue fell 5.8%**, and ₹49.88 crore of the ₹92.54 crore profit increase, 54% of it, came from joint ventures and associates, businesses the company part owns but does not run, which are added into the accounts well below the line where its own consultancy work is recorded. So more than half the profit rise did not come from the business the company operates. The transcript went out at 16:11 on Tuesday, after the close, and Wednesday was the first session in which anybody could trade against it.

### One quarter, one product, two companies, opposite answers

This is the week's clearest competitive story and neither company reported in it. Both had reported earlier, and the comparison landed on Monday.

**Blue Star's June-quarter room-air-conditioner margin halved, from 5.8% to 2.9%, with pre-tax profit down 23.7%.** Read alone, that looks like an industry problem, everyone squeezed by what it costs to build an air conditioner. **Then Voltas' quarter was read. Same quarter, same product, same weather, and Voltas expanded its core cooling margin from 3.7% to 5.3% on volumes up 45%.** Volumes are units sold rather than rupees of sales, so a company can grow its sales value on price while shifting fewer machines; 45% more machines is 45% more machines. One sell-side desk estimates the whole industry grew volumes 20 to 22% in that quarter. Volume share is close to zero-sum at the margin, so if the leader grew at double the industry, somebody grew a good deal below it.

**Two firms cannot both be victims of the same input costs when one of them expands its margin on 45% volume growth.** That reclassifies the problem from a sector cost squeeze to a lost-share problem, which is a different and worse thing for the loser to own. Neither share rewarded anybody this week: Blue Star spent all four observed sessions within about 1.3% of its one-year low, and Voltas, the company that won the quarter, fell 2.39% on Wednesday with no cause we could establish.

### The headline number that lied hardest

**IRCTC put its June-quarter transcript on the exchange at 13:48 on Wednesday, and the decomposition is the story.** Revenue rose 18.1% to ₹1,370 crore. Operating profit fell 2.77% and the operating margin dropped 5.1 percentage points to 28.17%.

The reason is in the mix. **Internet ticketing, which handles 89% of India's reserved rail bookings and keeps above eighty rupees in every hundred it earns, grew 0.5%.** Effectively flat. **Catering grew 33.8% and is now 53% of revenue**, and management named catering itself as the thing diluting the margin. A business that takes a fee on a rail booking carries almost no cost against it. A catering business must buy and cook and deliver food. When the cheap-to-run business stops growing and the expensive-to-run business grows a third, total revenue rises and total profit does not. The share closed Wednesday 0.49% above its one-year low and closed Thursday exactly on it, on a session when the Nifty rose 0.64%. All those figures are for the parent company alone; no set covering its subsidiaries is established anywhere in our briefs.

### The pattern underneath, in five sets of accounts

Strip out the individual stories and one shape repeats all week. **Companies that build things in India grew their sales in the June quarter and did not keep the growth.**

The mechanism differs by industry and is named in each case. In contracting it is fixed-price work meeting higher input costs. In renewables it is interest and depreciation on a heavy building phase, which is what "finance costs up 109%" means in practice.

**And one timing observation that ties the section together.** Continuous trading on the exchange ends at 15:15 and the closing auction at 15:35, so a document published after that cannot have moved that day's closing price; it becomes the next session's cause. This week a striking number of the relevant filings landed at night or in the last half hour. NGL Fine-Chem's presentation at 15:02. Zaggle's income-tax appeal dismissal at 22:09 on Saturday 15 August, Independence Day, roughly 65 hours before anyone could react, after which it opened locked at its 20% lower limit. Blue Star's divestment agreement at 21:01 on the Friday of a three-day break. EFC (I) filing four separate documents on Tuesday between 89 and 102 minutes after the continuous close. **None of that breaks any rule and we allege nothing. The pattern is simply that the disclosure clock and the trading clock are not the same clock.**

**A note on scale before anything else.** Nothing in that table is a large weekly sector move. The widest is 2.63% and seven of the eleven finished inside one percentage point either way. **This week's real dispersion was between individual companies, not between sectors**, and Section 7 is where it lives.

**Metal and realty tie for the best week, and they got there in opposite ways.** Metal made its week on Monday, and Monday is fully explained: copper rose 1.44% and aluminium 2.97% on the London exchange and the Indian metals complex followed, as set out in Section 2. It then did nothing for three days, and by Thursday the base metals had actually turned, copper down 0.71% and aluminium down 0.10%, with the Indian industrial metals declining to join Thursday's rally. Then it added 0.86% on Friday, nearly half the week's gain, and **cause not established in our briefs.** Realty made its week on Monday and Thursday, and neither session carries a property-specific reason. Monday it was the best sector on a day money was moving into domestic cyclical businesses generally. Tuesday it gave the gain back and there the detail is good: two companies did all the damage, Macrotech Developers down 3.42% and Oberoi Realty down 3.78%, with no filing and no exchange query behind either, and the obvious candidate, sensitivity to interest rates, could not be tested because our bond curve was dark.

**Media rose 1.42% and it was one session.** It rose 2.13% on Thursday, which is more than the whole week's gain, and Zee Entertainment rose 5.68%. That corroborates two outside reports describing Thursday as driven by media and capital-market buying, which our Thursday brief could not size at the time. **Why anybody was buying media companies is still not established in our briefs.**

**Nifty Auto did nothing at all, at down 0.29%**, and it is worth one line because our own data feed insists otherwise. The feed reports Auto as the week's best sector at plus 7.94%. That gain was real and it was already in place before this week started: the same field read plus 8.93% on 13 August and plus 8.25% on 14 August. It belongs to the previous week, not this one. The one named auto move inside this week is Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles down 2.24% on Tuesday, on a day its own sector rose, and its **cause is not established in our briefs.**

**Nifty Energy fell 0.78% because oil rose, which runs against most people's intuition and is the cleanest chain of the week.** Nifty Energy holds refiners and gas distributors. They buy crude, they do not sell it. India imports roughly 85% of its oil by sea. So a rising oil price is a debit to that index, not a credit, and on Wednesday, the fourth consecutive up-session in crude, energy was among the two worst sectors at about down 1.2%, the one sector figure we independently corroborated against outside sources that day. Deep Industries fell 7.05%. The premium is being paid for oil that has to travel by sea specifically: the gap between Brent and the American WTI benchmark widened to about US$7.16, which is the signature of a supply disruption rather than of booming demand. The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively shut since 28 February, eight attacks on ships were reported in August, the 60-day American-Iranian understanding expired on 17 August with no deal and no extension, and on 19 August the United Arab Emirates suspended all trade and banking with Iran, its second-largest commercial partner.

**The state-owned banks index fell 1.36% and the entire week happened on Tuesday**, when it lost 1.01% against 0.41% for the private-lender-heavy Nifty Bank. That 60 basis-point gap would ordinarily be a bond-yield story and we could not test it, because our own government bond curve was dark all week (Section 6). What our private note supplies is the missing leg: the Reserve Bank shut its subsidised dollar-swap window six weeks early that Monday, and government bond yields repriced immediately, the five-year up about 9 basis points to 6.44%. Those yields are press-sourced rather than ours. Higher government bond yields reduce the value of the large government-bond holdings state-owned banks carry, which is exactly the kind of day on which they lag private lenders. Note what did **not** move them: the Reserve Bank's hawkish minutes on Wednesday evening moved them 0.01% down that session and 0.01% down the next.

**Fast-moving consumer goods fell 2.27%, and this is the best sector-level explanation of the week.** That means soap, biscuits, tea, packaged food, the things households buy weekly. **Wholesale prices are rising at nearly twice the pace of shop prices.** July's wholesale inflation was 9.78% and was widely reported as having eased; it eased only because fuel and power fell from 27.41% to 20.05%, which is an arithmetic effect of comparing against an expensive month a year earlier. Underneath that, **what factories charge at their own gate accelerated by 0.81 of a percentage point to 8.29%, and raw materials rose 1.52 points to 8.52%, while what households actually paid in the shops rose 4.45%.** That gap of roughly 3.8 percentage points has to be absorbed by somebody, and it is absorbed by whoever sits in the middle and cannot raise prices, which is packaged goods, chemicals, cement and building products. Two further inputs pushed the same way inside the week: crude up five sessions running, which is an input to packaging, transport and detergents alike, and Russian wheat exports for August cut to 1.8 million tonnes, roughly 60% below a year earlier and the lowest August since 2010. The honest limit is that no brief attributes any single consumer-goods session to any of this. The mechanism is sourced, dated and sector-specific; the day-by-day attribution is not. Nifty Consumption, down 1.02%, is the same squeeze and has no separate cause established.

**Nifty IT was the worst sector at down 2.63%, and most of that fall has no established cause.** The arithmetic is worth doing. The two sessions that produced 3.65 percentage points of damage, Monday and Tuesday, carry no established cause at all. The mid-week recovery of about 1.5 points is explained by an American bond operation that has nothing to do with Indian software demand. Friday is unwritten. **The week's worst sector is mostly unexplained, and that is the finding rather than a gap in it.** One caution on the other side: almost every outlet called Thursday a technology-led rally, and Nifty IT rose 0.79% against an index up 0.64%. Fifteen basis points of difference is not leadership.
