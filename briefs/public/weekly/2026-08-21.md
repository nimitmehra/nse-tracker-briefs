## 1. The week in plain English

The Nifty 50 finished the week at 24,252.00, down 0.47%. The shape of the week was three falls, one rise and one day that did almost nothing. Monday fell 0.32%, Tuesday fell 0.55%, Wednesday fell 0.32% and made it seven losing sessions in a row, Thursday rose 0.64% and ended the run, and Friday added 0.08%, and on the Sensex the whole session came to a gain of three points.

That headline number hides the more useful fact. **The fall was a big-company fall, not a market fall.** Three of the seven headline indices we track went up this week. The Nifty Smallcap 250, which holds 250 smaller companies, rose 0.74%. The Nifty Midcap 50 rose 0.33%. Bank Nifty, the twelve large banks, rose 0.47%. The four that fell are the four most crowded with giants: the Nifty 500, the broadest of the seven and the closest thing here to "the market", down 0.27%; the Nifty 50 down 0.47%; the Nifty 100 down 0.50%; and the Sensex, thirty of the largest companies on the Bombay exchange and the most concentrated of the lot, worst of all at down 0.60%. **The order is the point: the more an index is weighted towards the very biggest companies, the worse its week was.**

Four things mattered this week, and each can be said in one sentence.

**Oil went up every single session, and India buys nearly all the oil it burns.** Brent crude, the benchmark price most of the world's oil trades against, rose from US$89.01 on Monday to US$94.19 by Friday, which is 5.8% across those five closes. Our data feed puts the week's rise at 6.41% because it measures from a different starting point; both describe the same move and we are showing you the one you can check yourself. It had risen for five straight sessions by Thursday and did not stop. For an economy that imports the overwhelming majority of its crude, that is a cost landing on everything at once: on the fuel bill, on transport, on the price of goods in the shops, and on the rupees that leave the country to pay for the barrels. It is the single thread that runs through the whole week.

**Thursday's rise was made in Washington, not in India.** On Wednesday evening American time, the United States Treasury said it would at least double its buybacks of long-dated government bonds, from US$2 billion to at least US$4 billion an operation, running from 9 September to 4 November. A buyback here simply means the government buying its own older bonds back off the market. American long-term interest rates fell on the news, the American 30-year yield by 9 basis points, a basis point being one hundredth of a percentage point. Lower long-term interest rates make the future profits of companies worth more today, so share prices rise nearly everywhere when they fall. Asia opened up on that alone.

**India got the smallest slice of that rally in Asia, and crude is the reason.** Korea's Kospi rose somewhere between 5.5% and 6% that day, Japan's Nikkei between 1.16% and 1.3%, Hong Kong's Hang Seng around 1.1%. India managed 0.64%, and oil and gas was the only sector to close red.

**India's large software exporters fell hard for two days and we cannot tell you why.** The Nifty IT index, which holds the big software-services companies that earn in dollars, fell 1.75% on Monday and 1.93% on Tuesday, and was by a distance the worst sector of the week at down 2.63%. Two explanations were circulating and we checked both. A stronger rupee, which would shrink their dollar earnings when converted, is the wrong way round: the rupee actually weakened. A Jefferies downgrade of Infosys turned out to be dated February 2026, six months old. On Tuesday we searched again and found no dated reason at all, and we did not invent one. Then on Wednesday the same index went from the week's worst sector to the day's best, and we cannot explain that either. That is three unexplained moves in one sector in three sessions.

Two more facts belong up here before anything else.

**Indian institutions bought heavily on every session we can measure, and the market still went down.** Domestic institutional investors, which is mostly Indian mutual funds, insurers and pension funds, and therefore mostly ordinary people's monthly savings, put in roughly ₹11,650 crore across the three sessions where our own briefs carry the figure. Foreign institutions, meaning overseas funds, were close to flat over the same three sessions, net sellers of about ₹476 crore, which on a market this size is nothing at all. Buying that large failing to lift the index, while the smaller-company indices rose, points at the largest companies as the source of the drag. Section 3 has the day-by-day figures and the two sessions we could not verify.

**And we have no written record of Friday.** We publish a daily brief on the market; this week we published for Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday, and for Friday 21 August there is no brief of any kind. The price data for Friday is complete, so we know exactly what happened. We do not know why. That matters more than it sounds, because Friday held the week's biggest single stock move, the only meaningful jump in the market's fear gauge, and a session where the index rose while more companies fell than rose. Where we cannot source a cause below, the brief says so in plain words rather than reaching for a plausible one. Section 9 sets out the whole gap.

The explanation running on the wires was that Indian shares fell because crude is high and American long-term bond yields are rising. Both are true as descriptions of a level and neither is true as a description of a move. **Brent changed by 0.01% that day.** Crude was indeed sitting about 9% above where it was on 7 August and long American yields really were at their highest since 2007, but a level that has not changed cannot move a market on a Tuesday. Nor was the fall imported: Asia split in two, with Japan, Korea and Taiwan down between 1.1% and 1.8% while Shanghai rose 1.61% and Hong Kong 1.41%, which looks nothing like a regional flight out of risk. The fall was made at home, and we could not say by whom or why.

**What Tuesday's index hides is that two government notifications organised the session and both cohorts rose against a falling market.** The electronics ministry cleared ₹7,877 crore of component-manufacturing projects on letters dated 17 August, and the defence ministry notified a sixth list of items that must be sourced from Indian suppliers, 405 of them, on 18 August. Paras Defence closed 10% higher at a one-year high and Jyoti CNC Automation rose 8.30%. **The arithmetic nobody else put next to that is worth carrying.** Those 405 items carry an estimated ₹3,070 crore of business potential, spread across the entire defence public-sector supply chain over several years, and nothing has been awarded to anybody. Paras Defence alone is worth roughly four times that whole figure. The direction of the news is real and the size of it is much smaller than the reaction.

**Wednesday 19 August fell only 0.32% and was by a distance the worst day of the week for the ordinary company.** Across our screened list of roughly 700 companies above a ₹1,000 crore size floor, 225 rose and 477 fell, and the middle company of the day was down 0.53%. On Monday the middle company was down 0.08%. So on the smallest of the three index falls, the market underneath fell about six times harder than it had on Monday. This is the clearest example this week of an index understating what actually happened.

Wednesday came in three parts and we are not going to weld them into one story, because they are not one story. First, Nifty IT went from the week's worst sector to the day's best, closing up 0.73%, and **we do not know why**. Two explanations were on offer and both fail: the rupee moved 0.05%, which is nowhere near enough to reprice a whole sector, and "buying at lower prices" describes a bounce rather than causing one. Second, and this part is explained, crude rose for a fourth straight session to US$91.74 and Indian energy shares fell because of it, which Section 5 sets out in full. Third, India's defence shares fell roughly 1.5% to 1.6% and a dedicated search for a reason returned only articles dated 2024 and February 2026. Defence shares falling that hard days after a Gulf tanker attack is an open question, and it stays open.

The smallest companies were Wednesday's casualty, and it reversed Tuesday exactly. The Smallcap 250 was Tuesday's only green headline index and Wednesday's worst faller at down 0.63%, while technology went from worst to best in the same twenty-four hours.

**Thursday 20 August rose 0.64% and ended the seven-session losing run, on the American buyback announcement described in Section 1.** It was the week's only genuinely broad day and it was broad by a wide margin: 411 companies rose against 288 that fell on our screened list, the middle company rose 0.27%, the only positive median of the week, and across the whole exchange advances beat declines about three and a half to one, 2,148 to 600, on a live count taken during the session rather than at the close. Fifty-seven names in our roughly 700-company universe rose more than 3% and exactly one fell more than 5%.

Three popular explanations for Thursday were checked and killed. **Foreign buying**, written up by several desks, has no published figure behind it, because on Thursday evening the only flow data in existence was Wednesday's; Section 3 explains why that is true every single day. **The rupee** firmed 0.13% on a day the dollar index, which measures the dollar against a basket of other major currencies, itself fell 0.14%. Those are the same size, so the rupee contributed nothing of its own; it simply stood still while the dollar moved. **Derivatives expiry** cannot explain that day's 4.99% collapse in the fear gauge, because weekly settlement on this exchange has been held on Tuesdays since September 2025.

There is one caveat on Thursday that almost nobody carried, and it is the honest one. Those Treasury buybacks are repurchases of older, thinly traded bonds meant to relieve congestion at the long end of the market. They are not the Federal Reserve, they are not new money, and they are not stimulus. **The enlarged operations do not begin until 9 September.** India rose on an announcement whose first practical effect is three weeks away. One report also has the American 30-year yield's fall already unwinding later that day, after India had closed, and that is single-sourced, so we flag it as the thing to watch rather than as a fact.

**Friday 21 August rose 0.08% and we have no brief covering it.** The Sensex moved 3.11 points in a full session, which is about as close to nothing as an index gets. Banks were the strongest of the seven indices for a second day running, up 0.46%. Underneath, 320 companies rose and 372 fell of the 703 we priced, and the middle company was down 0.08%, so the index closed higher while the typical company closed lower. A small number of larger members carried it. Which members, and why, is **not established in our briefs**, and the same sentence applies to every line of Friday's data, including the two sector figures that did come through, Nifty IT down 0.46% and Nifty Pharma down 0.21%.

Three things were scheduled into that session and we cannot tell you whether any of them was priced: the market regulator's annual derivatives study, published at about 21:00 on Thursday and therefore tradeable for the first time on Friday (Section 6), and the Reserve Bank's weekly statistics and a ₹28,000 crore bond auction in which banks agree in advance to buy whatever the government cannot sell, both after the close. **No brief records the outcome of any of the three.**

## 3. Breadth and flows: what the index was not telling you

**Breadth is a count, not an average.** It asks how many separate companies went up and how many went down, counting each one once. The index tells you what the biggest companies did, weighted by how big they are. When the two disagree, the disagreement is usually the more useful fact.

Here is the week, day by day, across our screened list of companies above a ₹1,000 crore size floor. The **middle company** is exactly that: half the list did better, half did worse. It is a fairer summary than an average, because one share up 18% cannot drag it.

**Wednesday is the one to notice.** The Nifty fell less on Wednesday than on Tuesday, and underneath it 477 companies fell against 225 that rose.

**Over the full week, 313 companies rose and 385 fell out of the 698 we priced.** That is 45 out of every 100 finishing the week higher. Put that next to Section 1: **the Nifty Smallcap 250 had the best week of the seven indices, and most individual companies still went down.** Both are true, and they are true together because an index is weighted by company size, so a minority of larger members inside it can lift it while the rest drift lower. Anyone telling you small companies had a good week owes you that second half of the sentence.

### Where the money came from

Every trading day the exchanges publish two provisional net figures. **Foreign institutional investors** are overseas funds buying and selling Indian shares. **Domestic institutional investors** are Indian mutual funds, insurers and pension funds doing the same. "Net" is purchases minus sales, so a positive number is money coming in. These two pools are the largest identifiable buyers and sellers in the market, and they usually pull against each other.

**There is a hard limit on how fresh these numbers can ever be, and it explains a mistake several desks made this week.** The exchange publishes the figures at around 19:00 India time. Our own daily data run happens at about 17:00. So every evening brief carries the *previous* session's flows, never that day's. That is a publishing schedule, not a failure we can fix by trying harder, and it means **a flow number can never explain the session it is published after**. When several desks attributed Thursday's rally to foreign buying, the only flow figure in existence on Thursday evening was Wednesday's. The claim had nothing behind it.

**Domestic institutions bought on every session for which a figure exists, and they bought heavily**, putting in ₹11,654.49 crore net across Monday to Wednesday alone. **Foreign institutions were close to flat**, net sellers of ₹475.58 crore across the same three sessions, and their one large day was Monday's sale of ₹2,535.10 crore, by far the biggest single number of the week in either direction.

Set those two side by side. Indian savings institutions bought about eleven and a half thousand crore in three days, foreign funds sold about five hundred crore, and the index still fell 0.47% over the week. Buying that large absorbing selling that small without lifting the market is what it looks like when the pressure sits in a handful of the very largest companies, which is exactly what Section 2's index table shows.

Two limits. A Thursday figure exists in our own data feed, but it was single-sourced and unconfirmed on the night and no brief verified it, so we are not printing it. For Friday there is no figure at all and there cannot be one, because it would only have been published the following Monday. The three-session sums above are our own arithmetic on daily figures our briefs verified, which is why they are labelled as three sessions rather than as a week.

### The fear gauge

India VIX is a single number worked out from the prices of Nifty options. It estimates how much movement the options market expects in the index over the next thirty days. It is usually called the fear gauge because it rises when traders pay up for protection. **A low reading does not mean the market will be calm. It means nobody is currently paying much for insurance against it not being calm.**

It read 11.325 on Monday, 11.39 on Tuesday and 11.32 on Wednesday, which is to say it did essentially nothing across three consecutive falling sessions, sitting at the low end of its range and about 42% below where it stood three months earlier on our own feed's history. How low is a question we take up two paragraphs down, because our feed and two outside vendors do not agree on where the floor is. **That is orderly selling rather than stress, and it also means nothing at all was priced for a break.** Then on Thursday, the one day the market rose, it collapsed 4.99% to 10.755. Our own market-data feed puts Friday at 11.195, up 4.04%, and no brief covers that session, so the rebound is on our record without an explanation attached to it.

One correction to our own work belongs here. Our internal note for Thursday called 10.755 a fresh 52-week low. Our public brief for the same session refused to publish that, because two independent data vendors put the one-year range at 8.72 to 28.91 against our own feed's 10.755 to 27.89, and both of our bounds sitting inside theirs points to a shortened lookback window rather than a genuine new low. **The fall is real. The record claim is not, and we are not making it.**

## 4. Earnings: the week the paperwork moved the prices

**This was not a reporting week, and saying so is the first honest thing about it.** The June-quarter filing deadline had passed. Our exchange feed lists 43 companies with an earnings-related filing this week, and when we searched every one of them across all six of our briefs we found a single mention, and that mention is about a company joining an index rather than about any result. The 43 are overwhelmingly compliance paperwork: newspaper-publication copies of results already filed, transcripts of calls already held on 10 to 12 August, press releases, delay notices and clarifications.

So what moved prices between Monday and Thursday was not new numbers. **It was the market finishing its reading of numbers it already had**, in the call transcript, the management commentary, the presentation footnote, the segment breakdown. Several of the week's largest single-session moves came from a document that contained no new figure at all.

Three words first, because the rest of this section leans on them. **Revenue** is what a company sold. **Operating profit** is what is left after the costs of running the business, before interest and tax, and the **operating margin** is that profit as a percentage of revenue, so it tells you how many rupees out of every hundred of sales the company actually keeps. **Net profit** is what survives at the very end, after interest, tax and everything else.

### What was bought, and deserved to be

**Ipca Laboratories rose 8.67% on Monday to an all-time high, on 17.8 times its normal trading volume.** The June quarter ran revenue up 21%, operating profit up 61% and net profit up 72% against the same quarter a year earlier, with branded medicines up 16%, exported drug ingredients up 33% and domestic drug ingredients up 20%. When profit grows nearly three times as fast as sales, the company is keeping more of every rupee it sells rather than simply selling more of them. That is the cleanest kind of good quarter and the market paid for it the same day.

**Tube Investments of India rose 7.94% on Tuesday, and the reason it rose on Tuesday and not earlier is the call.** Its results were approved on 14 August, the earnings call was held on Monday morning, the recording went up, and the share repriced on Tuesday. Revenue rose 17% to ₹6,215 crore, with engineering up 21%, mobility up 26% and metal-formed products up 12%. **We are not printing a profit figure for it**, because two public versions exist, one showing profit down 3% and another down 43%, and we could not establish which was comparing what. The shape we will stand behind is that growth was double-digit in every segment rather than in one lucky division.

One genuine tailwind runs under the whole week. **Where companies disclosed their order books, the order books were healthy.** An order book is work a company has won and not yet performed, so it is future revenue rather than current revenue. Genus Power carries ₹24,020 crore, roughly four and a half years of its revenue; Asian Energy Services ₹1,754 crore with guidance unchanged; Engineers India has taken ₹2,750 crore of new work this year against an ₹8,000 crore annual target, which is on pace. **Demand was not this quarter's problem.**

### The three documents that repriced a company without changing a number

**NGL Fine-Chem lost 15.80% on Monday in the last twenty-eight minutes of trading, on sixteen times its normal volume, because the company explained its own margin.** The stock opened at a one-year high. At 15:02, twenty-eight minutes before the close, it filed an investor presentation. Inside it, management disclosed that the quarter's 6.2 percentage point margin improvement was partly inventory gains, meaning stock bought cheaply and sold after input prices rose, which is real profit but a one-time windfall the business cannot repeat, and that a ₹7.0 crore currency swing worth about 29% of pre-tax profit "may not recur". **Nothing about the reported numbers changed.** The company told the market which parts of a good-looking margin were not going to happen again, and the market removed them.

**Ahluwalia Contracts was the week's largest earnings casualty and it took two sessions to finish**, down 5.19% on Monday and 10.85% on Tuesday, down 16.86% over the five sessions. On Tuesday it was the only faller in our entire mover set. June-quarter sales rose 12.03% to ₹1,125.81 crore and consolidated net profit fell 79.71% to ₹10.39 crore, from ₹51.21 crore. Its operating margin fell from about ₹5.10 in every hundred rupees of sales to about 90 paise. The sequence explains the timing exactly: the result was filed after the close on Friday 14 August, the recording of the management call went up after the close on Monday, and the market delivered its verdict on Tuesday. **The call made it worse, not better.** And revenue up 12% with profit down 80% is not a demand problem. It is a cost problem sitting below the revenue line, in a construction business where fixed-price contracts, meaning the customer's payment is agreed in advance so every rupee of unexpected cost comes out of the builder's own margin, decide whether there is anything left.

**Engineers India fell 3.22% on Wednesday on a headline profit rise of 141%**, and this is the cleanest good-number-bad-reception case of the week. Net profit rose 141% to ₹157.94 crore while **revenue fell 5.8%**, and ₹49.88 crore of the ₹92.54 crore profit increase, 54% of it, came from joint ventures and associates, businesses the company part owns but does not run, which are added into the accounts well below the line where its own consultancy work is recorded. So more than half the profit rise did not come from the business the company operates. The transcript went out at 16:11 on Tuesday, after the close, and Wednesday was the first session in which anybody could trade against it.

### One quarter, one product, two companies, opposite answers

This is the week's clearest competitive story and neither company reported in it. Both had reported earlier, and the comparison landed on Monday.

**Blue Star's June-quarter room-air-conditioner margin halved, from 5.8% to 2.9%, with pre-tax profit down 23.7%.** Read alone, that looks like an industry problem, everyone squeezed by what it costs to build an air conditioner. **Then Voltas' quarter was read. Same quarter, same product, same weather, and Voltas expanded its core cooling margin from 3.7% to 5.3% on volumes up 45%.** Volumes are units sold rather than rupees of sales, so a company can grow its sales value on price while shifting fewer machines; 45% more machines is 45% more machines. One sell-side desk estimates the whole industry grew volumes 20 to 22% in that quarter. Volume share is close to zero-sum at the margin, so if the leader grew at double the industry, somebody grew a good deal below it.

**Two firms cannot both be victims of the same input costs when one of them expands its margin on 45% volume growth.** That reclassifies the problem from a sector cost squeeze to a lost-share problem, which is a different and worse thing for the loser to own. Neither share rewarded anybody this week: Blue Star spent every observed session near its one-year low, and Voltas, the company that won the quarter, fell 2.39% on Wednesday with no cause we could establish.

### The headline number that lied hardest

**IRCTC put its June-quarter transcript on the exchange at 13:48 on Wednesday, and the decomposition is the story.** Revenue rose 18.1% to ₹1,370 crore. Operating profit fell 2.77% and the operating margin dropped 5.1 percentage points to 28.17%.

The reason is in the mix. **Internet ticketing, which handles 89% of India's reserved rail bookings and keeps above eighty rupees in every hundred it earns, grew 0.5%.** Effectively flat. **Catering grew 33.8% and is now 53% of revenue**, and management named catering itself as the thing diluting the margin. A business that takes a fee on a rail booking carries almost no cost against it. A catering business must buy and cook and deliver food. When the cheap-to-run business stops growing and the expensive-to-run business grows a third, total revenue rises and total profit does not. The share closed Wednesday 0.49% above its one-year low and closed Thursday exactly on it, on a session when the Nifty rose 0.64%. All those figures are for the parent company alone; no set covering its subsidiaries is established anywhere in our briefs.

### The pattern underneath

Strip out the individual stories and one shape repeats all week. **Companies that build things in India grew their sales in the June quarter and did not keep the growth.** Five separate sets of accounts say it. Ahluwalia and Engineers India are set out above. K.P. Energy grew revenue 136% and profit 4%. KPI Green Energy grew its operating profit 19% and still lost 15% of its net profit, because the interest it pays on its borrowings rose 109%. The fifth is the company Section 7 declines to name, and we are not naming it here either: our own two briefs describe that symbol as two different businesses in two different industries and we could not settle which is right.

The mechanism differs by industry. In contracting it is fixed-price work meeting higher input costs. In renewables it is interest and depreciation, the accounting charge that spreads an asset's cost across its useful life, arriving together during a heavy building phase.

**And one timing observation ties the section together.** Continuous trading ends at 15:15 and the closing auction at 15:35, so a document published after that cannot have moved that day's closing price; it becomes the next session's cause. A striking number of this week's relevant filings landed at night or in the last half hour: NGL Fine-Chem's presentation at 15:02; Zaggle's income-tax appeal dismissal at 22:09 on Saturday 15 August, Independence Day, roughly 65 hours before anyone could react, after which it opened locked at its 20% lower limit; Blue Star's divestment agreement at 21:01 on the Friday of a three-day break; EFC (I) filing four documents on Tuesday between 89 and 102 minutes after the continuous close. **None of that breaks any rule and we allege nothing. The pattern is simply that the disclosure clock and the trading clock are not the same clock.**

## 6. Three things worth understanding properly

### The rupee's main support was switched off six weeks early, and we published the wrong closing date

Indian banks can offer deposits to non-resident Indians held in foreign currency. The bank takes in dollars and eventually has to give dollars back, so to protect itself it does a **swap** with the Reserve Bank: it hands over the dollars now for rupees, with an agreement to reverse the exchange at a set rate on a set future date. That agreement has a price, and the Reserve Bank had been offering it at a discount. The subsidy made the whole deposit product cheaper for banks to run, so banks pushed it, and dollars came into the country. **It was, in effect, a dated scheme to attract foreign currency, and every dollar it brought in supported the rupee.**

**On 17 August the Reserve Bank closed that window roughly six weeks early. Money-raising under the scheme now ends 31 August rather than 30 September, with the swaps themselves executable to 11 September.** Cumulative inflows had reached US$56.85 billion as of 13 August, of which US$52.3 billion came through those deposits.

**A correction to our own work, and it matters because it changes a date the reader is planning around.** Our public daily brief of 18 August told readers the window shuts on 30 September. That was the scheme's original end date and it had already been superseded when we published it. **The correct date is 31 August.** We are stating that plainly rather than quietly using the right number.

The stated reason for closing early is the opposite of what a closure usually signals: inflows topped US$50 billion, which was better than expected, and policymakers were wary of the effect on domestic cash conditions and of the cost. **That is a tap being closed because it worked, not because it failed.**

The effect showed up inside the same week. The rupee weakened from 95.43 to 95.592 on Monday **on a day the dollar index itself fell 0.23%**, which is the India-specific part: the dollar was softening against everything else and India took none of it. It closed the week 0.30% weaker at 95.685. Government bond yields repriced within a day, as Section 5 sets out, because a subsidised source of foreign funding disappearing makes domestic funding relatively more valuable.

**The test we said we would run went unobserved, and that is the sharpest single cost of not having a Friday brief.** We wrote on Tuesday that the release to read was the Reserve Bank's weekly reserves statement, due Friday at about 17:00, because a large fall in reserves against a shut deposit window would mean the rupee is now being defended out of the reserves themselves. Foreign exchange reserves are the central bank's own stock of foreign currency, and selling from that stock to buy rupees is the other way a country holds its exchange rate up. **No brief records what that release said.** The level, about US$707 billion, is the number we would stand behind; Section 9 explains why we are not publishing a change figure.

### Two Reserve Bank drafts are being reported as one, and neither of them is law

This touches more Indian households than anything else in the week, because it is about what a lender may charge you and how often it may change what it charges. It is also the story most at risk of being told wrongly.

**Both of these are drafts. Neither is in force. Neither obliges anybody.** In India a financial regulator publishes a draft, invites written comments by a stated date, and only then decides whether to issue a final rule, change it, or drop it altogether. A comment window is not a countdown to enactment. It is the point at which a proposal can still change shape. One more caution travels with everything below: our source is press summaries of these documents, not the documents themselves, so read it as reported.

**The first draft, published earlier in August, would as reported stop non-bank lenders from offering revolving credit. Comments close 28 August.** A non-bank lender is a finance company that lends without holding a banking licence, so it cannot take deposits from the public and funds itself in the markets instead. Revolving credit is an arrangement where a borrower has a standing limit, draws from it, repays, and can draw again without a fresh application, which is how a credit card works, as opposed to a term loan, which is a fixed sum repaid on a fixed schedule and then finished. Three specific things about it remain open: whether the restriction survives at all, whether the exemption for credit cards widens, and whether a transition period appears. Any of the three changes the outcome materially. That draft has already taken roughly ₹33,000 crore off the market value of one lender, which is a proposal repricing a company.

**The second draft, published 12 August, would as reported change how every regulated lender in India sets its loan rates. Comments close 11 September.** Two contents are named. A maximum three-month reset on floating-rate loans from 1 April 2027: a floating-rate loan, which is how most Indian home loans work, has an interest rate that tracks a published reference rate, and a reset is the moment your lender recalculates your rate against it. Slow resets mean a borrower keeps paying the old higher rate for months after the reference rate has fallen. Capping the gap at three months means a cut reaches you faster, and it also means an increase does. And a ceiling on the all-in annual rate charged on small loans, where "all-in" is the phrase doing the work: not the headline interest rate but the total annual cost including fees and charges, which is where the real price of a small loan usually hides.

**One sentence separates them. One draft restricts a product; the other touches every loan book in the country.** Their comment windows close two weeks apart. A reader told "the Reserve Bank is cracking down on lending and comments close this month" has been handed a merged thing that does not exist, and will be wrong about both the timing and the reach.

### The regulator counted who makes money trading derivatives, and the answer got better for the wrong reason

The market regulator published its annual derivatives study at about 21:00 on Thursday, after the close, so Friday was the first session in which anybody could trade on it.

In the year to March 2026, **87.7% of individual traders lost money**, ₹91,685 crore net against about ₹1.12 lakh crore the year before. On the measure the release leads with, **the number of unique active traders fell 18% to 87.5 lakh, the first contraction in a decade.** A second, narrower count inside the same document counts differently and we are not using it.

Put those two together and the improvement disappears. **Aggregate losses fell because fewer people were trading, not because more of them started winning.** Roughly seven out of eight individual participants still lost. Whether Friday priced any of this is not established in our briefs.
