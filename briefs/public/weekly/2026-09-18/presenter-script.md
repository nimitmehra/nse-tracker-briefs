# Presenter script: week ending Friday, 18 September 2026

*Read to camera. Weekend recording, Saturday 19 September. Source: `briefs/public/weekly/2026-09-18.md` and nothing else. Next trading day is Monday 21 September.*

## 1. Hook

Three costs turned against India over a long weekend, and the market paid for all three in one afternoon.

Oil went up, because a drone strike shut Saudi Arabia's main export pipeline. The interest rate on ten-year American government bonds touched five per cent for the first time since 2007. And our own central bank said it would sell a trillion rupees of bonds to pull cash out of the banking system. Tuesday fell to a five-month low.

Then the three things the market feared most all happened, and nothing broke. By Friday the market had clawed back nearly all of Tuesday's fall.

## 2. Before we begin

Before we begin: this is general market commentary, not investment advice. I am not a SEBI-registered Research Analyst.

## 3. The week in one minute

The Nifty 50 closed the week at 23,346, down about a fifth of a per cent. The Sensex did worse, down about two thirds of a per cent, which the press counted as its sixth losing week in a row. On our own record the Nifty has fallen five weeks running. Two different indices, two different counts.

One honesty note for everything that follows. This was a four-session week. Monday was Ganesh Chaturthi and the exchange was closed. We observed Tuesday, Wednesday and Friday. Thursday was a real trading day, the busiest of the week, and our daily process did not run. So everything I say about Thursday is second-hand, taken from Friday's account or worked backwards from Friday's numbers.

## 4. How each day moved

Tuesday was the week. The Nifty opened higher on a bid for IT shares, gave it all back through the day and closed at its low, down 1.19 per cent. Of the 712 shares we track, 83 rose and 629 fell. That is a crowd leaving.

The cause was the long weekend. While the exchange was shut, oil rose to a four-month high and the American ten-year yield touched five per cent. And the Reserve Bank's bond-sale plan pushed our own ten-year yield to about 7.1 per cent. Tuesday was the first session anyone could trade any of that.

One sector rose. IT closed up about two per cent, on a weekend essay by the head of Anthropic, itself one of the AI laboratories, urging those laboratories to slow down, which some read as buying Indian software firms time. Every other sector fell, and the order tells you the cause. Realty fell hardest, over four per cent, then consumer durables, public-sector banks and autos, the sectors most exposed to dearer money and dearer oil. Metal fell too, but on a fall in copper prices, not Indian rates.

Why the fall came in the last hour rather than at the open, we could not establish, and no Indian outlet found a cause either.

Wednesday rose 0.43 per cent, and most shares did not rise with it. It was a big-company, financials-led day. Bond yields eased a little ahead of Thursday's sale, so the sectors hit on Tuesday rose first: public-sector banks, realty and life insurers.

Thursday, the day we did not see. On Friday's account the Nifty rose about a fifth of a per cent and the wires said little changed. The Federal Reserve, America's central bank, raised its rate by a quarter point, the first rise since 2023. The Bank of Japan, Japan's central bank, raised too. And the Reserve Bank sold its first 50,000 crore rupees of bonds into more than 66,000 crore of bids, with the yield essentially flat. Three feared events. No accident.

Friday. The Nifty rose a third of a per cent and the Sensex fell slightly, and the gap is one stock. TCS, Tata Consultancy Services, the group's software-services company, fell nearly four per cent after the board and the majority owner of Tata Sons fell out in public on Thursday evening, a story I will come to. TCS is heavier in the thirty-share Sensex than in the fifty-share Nifty. Underneath, it was the best day of the week for smaller companies: 364 of the 500 shares in the Nifty 500 rose.

The reason was mechanical, not news. A global index publisher, FTSE, put its September membership changes into effect at Friday's close. So every fund that copies those indices had to buy the added companies at the closing price. That buyer is gone on Monday.

## 5. Earnings: nobody reported, and that is the calendar

No company of any size reported quarterly results this week. The quarter now running ends on 30 September, and nobody can report it before it ends.

What fills the gap is annual general meetings. Engineers India, the state-owned engineering consultancy, restated a record year on Friday, revenue up 27 per cent and profit up 37. The story was what was not said. In August the company promised 8,000 crore rupees of new orders this year and a sixteen per cent margin, meaning sixteen rupees of every hundred of sales kept as profit after costs. The speech repeated neither and withdrew neither. And institutional shareholders voted sixty per cent against re-appointing one director. It passed anyway, because the government holds 51 per cent and voted for.

## 6. Three things worth understanding properly

**First, why a record foreign-reserves number and a five-month low in property shares are the same story.** Over the summer Indian banks raised about 136 billion dollars, mostly deposits from Indians abroad, and swapped those dollars with the Reserve Bank for rupees. So India's reserves jumped a record 45 billion dollars in one week. That looks like strength. It is a loan, because the Reserve Bank has to hand the dollars back later.

The rupee side of that swap is a cash flood, roughly ten trillion rupees of surplus in the banking system. That pushed the overnight rate between banks below the Reserve Bank's 5.25 per cent policy rate. That is looser money than the policy says, with inflation rising ten months running. So the Reserve Bank is taking the cash back by selling a trillion rupees of bonds, in three sales.

More bonds for sale means bond prices fall, and when a bond's price falls its yield rises. That ten-year yield is the risk-free rupee rate, what you earn lending to the government, and every other investment is compared against it. When it rises, a property developer's future sales are worth less today and a lender's cost of money goes up. That is why realty fell four per cent on Tuesday and rose on Wednesday and Friday as yields eased. The policy rate has not moved. Nobody at the Reserve Bank has said the word hike, and I am not saying it for them.

**Second, Tata Sons, in two acts.** Tata Sons is the unlisted company at the top of the Tata group. Act one: in a letter dated 11 September, the Reserve Bank told it to proceed to a stock-market listing. Tata Sons had asked to give up its registration as a core investment company, the regulator's label for a company that exists to hold group shares. The Reserve Bank said no. On Tuesday, Tata Chemicals rose twenty per cent and locked at its upper price limit, the exchange's daily cap on how far a share may rise. Why? It owns two and a half per cent of Tata Sons, and at the private valuations quoted in the press, that stake alone is worth more than all of Tata Chemicals. A listing would make it visible and sellable.

Act two: on Thursday evening the Tata Sons board re-appointed N. Chandrasekaran as executive chairman for five years, four votes to one. The same evening Tata Trusts, which owns about two thirds of Tata Sons, called the vote a legal nullity, meaning the majority owner says the board's vote does not count. That is an assertion. No court or regulator has ruled. A wire report that the board also approved a listing came from one anonymous source and was denied the same day, so it is a claim, not a fact. On Friday, Tata Chemicals fell eleven per cent and TCS fell nearly four.

**Third, UPI gets its first merchant fee.** From 15 October, when you pay a shop more than 2,000 rupees by UPI, the shop's bank will charge the shop 0.4 per cent, capped at 300 rupees. Paying a friend, or paying a shop 2,000 or less, changes nothing. Payments above 2,000 were only four per cent of transactions last year but two thirds of the money moved.

## 7. The big movers, risers first

Twenty-two companies worth more than 1,000 crore rupees moved more than six per cent on the week. Six have a documented cause, four a partial one, and for twelve we could not establish why.

Risers. eMudhra, a digital-signature company, rose 24 per cent after being appointed to issue and renew the corporate identity codes that regulators require of large borrowers. Yatharth Hospital rose 18 per cent after a Thursday filing that the private-equity firm Advent will put in 3,150 crore rupees of new money. Most of that rise came on Thursday, the day we did not see. Avalon Technologies rose 14 per cent, almost all of it Friday, because it was added to a FTSE index and index funds bought it at the close. HMA Agro, a meat exporter, Birla Cable, a telecom-cables maker, and TEGA rose 12 to 19 per cent. For HMA Agro and TEGA we looked and found no cause. Birla Cable we have not examined.

Fallers. Solar Industries, the largest company on either list, fell 17 per cent. It agreed to buy South Africa's Omnia, a mining-explosives and farm-chemicals business, for about 13,000 crore rupees in cash, and the filing said nothing about how that would be paid for. That silence is what was sold. On Thursday's call the company said all of it will be borrowed, and the stock kept falling. Chennai Petroleum fell ten per cent in a week oil hit a four-month high, which was supposed to help a refiner, and we cannot explain it.

## 8. What to watch next week

Five full sessions, Monday 21 to Friday 25 September. Monday: the NSE's own share offer closes to bidders. The number to read is the retail line, how many times over ordinary investors have bid for their share of the offer, which stood under one after two days. Also the Reserve Bank's second bond sale; read the cut-off yield, the highest yield it agreed to sell at, not "fully subscribed". And with the FTSE buyer gone, Friday's small-company winners face their honest test. Tuesday: Nifty weekly options expire on the NSE's Tuesday calendar. Friday: the Reserve Bank's weekly reserves figure. Reserves fell about five billion dollars in the week to 11 September, and the Bank did not say how much of that was dollars sold to support the rupee. A second fall of that size, with the dollar sales spelt out, would be the first sign that the swap money can no longer hide the Reserve Bank spending dollars to defend the rupee. A rise would tell us nothing either way.

Beyond that: the third bond sale on 28 September, the Reserve Bank's rate decision on 7 October, and Infosys's results board meeting on 22 and 23 October, the first big-company results of the new season.

Our last watch list is two weeks old, because no weekly was written for the week ending 11 September. Of fourteen items, eight held, three held with the date moved, three could not be graded.
