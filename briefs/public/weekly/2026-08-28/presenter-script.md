# Presenter script: week ending Friday, 28 August 2026

*Read to camera. Recorded Saturday 29 August. Source: `briefs/public/weekly/2026-08-28.md` and nothing else.*

---

## 1. Hook

The headline barely moved this week. The market underneath it moved a great deal.

The Nifty 500, the broadest index we track and the one that comes nearest to describing the whole listed market, finished the week down one hundredth of one per cent. After five full sessions of trading, that is dead flat.

Now the number that actually describes the week. Of roughly seven hundred and four shares we can price, three hundred and sixteen rose and three hundred and eighty-eight fell. So the typical Indian share lost money in a week the headline called flat.

And it gets worse day by day. The index and the typical share agreed with each other on exactly one session out of five. That was Thursday. On the other four the headline either pointed the wrong way or badly understated what happened.

Anyone who read this week off the index read almost none of it.

## 2. Before we begin

Before we begin: this is general market commentary, not investment advice. I am not a SEBI-registered Research Analyst.

## 3. The week in one minute

The Nifty 50 closed at 24,175, down about a third of a per cent. It was not a quiet week, it was a round trip. Tuesday's close was the week's high, Thursday's was the low, and Friday gave back a little under half of the fall.

Three separate forces pushed in three directions and roughly cancelled each other out, and none of them had anything to do with the other two.

The first was technology, moving on an American clock. Nifty IT, the index of the country's software services companies, finished the week up about two and a half per cent, and I will come back to why the timing of that matters more than the number.

The second was oil. Brent crude, the global benchmark grade India prices most of its imports against, fell on every single session, from about ninety-four dollars to about eighty-eight. That is roughly six per cent, and I would treat that as approximate rather than exact, for a reason I will get to at the end. The cause was diplomacy rather than demand. Iran and Oman resumed talks over the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow sea passage most of India's crude arrives through, discussing a temporary navigational corridor and agreeing to work on clearing mines. Cheaper oil is a tailwind for a country that imports about eighty-five per cent of what it refines.

The third force is the one nobody named. The domestic-demand complex sold off quietly all week. On our own recomputed figures, and I want to be clear those are ours and not the exchange's published weekly table, Nifty FMCG lost about one and a half per cent, Consumption about one, and Auto about one. Those are the three largest weekly sector falls in the market and no headline anywhere named any of them.

## 4. How the five days moved

Monday. The index lost a seventh of a per cent while four hundred shares fell and three hundred and two rose. The day's named cause was geopolitical and it pointed the wrong way. The United States Treasury Secretary announced what he called the single greatest financial offensive ever mounted against Iran, and the part that reaches India is that Washington says it will sanction any country that keeps trading with Iran. And yet Brent fell nearly two per cent on the day. A threat to oil supply that leaves oil cheaper has not been priced as a threat to oil supply. The announcement itself landed between about half past five and seven in the evening India time, after our market had shut. So Monday traded the anticipation, which had been pre-announced on Sunday. It did not trade the contents.

Tuesday. The index rose about half a per cent to the week's high while three hundred and eighty-six shares fell. This is the cleanest explanation of the week and it has nothing to do with mood. Tuesday was the National Stock Exchange's monthly derivatives expiry, the day the month's futures and options contracts settle and positions in them have to be closed or rolled forward. Settlement demand concentrates in a narrow band of very large, heavily traded companies, which is exactly the band an index weights most. Expiry propped up the index through a handful of giants while the rest of the market did not participate.

Wednesday. The exact reverse, one day later. The index fell about half a per cent while three hundred and eighty-three shares rose. The dated cause at the top was real and narrow. The United States paused visa appointments at its embassies and consulates worldwide while it re-screens and retrains consular officers, with no announced date for normal service to resume. That is why Nifty IT fell about one and a half per cent. But the index badly overstates it. Across the IT index's thirty-four members the middle share fell only four tenths of a per cent, twelve of them actually rose, and Cyient gained seven per cent. Five very large companies were effectively the whole move. One caution that travels with this story: the six-figure H-1B visa fee circulating alongside it is a proposal, not a rule in force.

Thursday. The one session where the index told the truth. It fell about half a per cent, four hundred and forty-nine shares fell against two hundred and fifty-three, and the middle share fell about the same as the index. It was the week's worst day for the typical shareholder and the only day the headline matched the experience. Most of the fall was one company. HDFC Bank fell 2.23 per cent, and on roughly a thirteen per cent weight in the index that single share takes about three fifths of the whole decline. Nobody should conclude from that that banks sold off. Kotak Mahindra rose 1.8 per cent that day, ICICI Bank rose nearly one, and the private banks as a group beat the market. What is wearing HDFC Bank down is its own arithmetic. Its net interest margin, which is the gap between what a bank earns on its loans and what it pays on its deposits, is at a record low of 3.26 per cent, and its cheapest deposits have fallen from thirty-eight per cent of the book two years ago to thirty-two.

Friday. A green headline on a flat market. The index rose about a third of a per cent and three hundred and sixty-one shares fell against three hundred and forty-three. It was one sector. Nifty IT rose three and a half per cent with all nine of its priced members higher, and on that sector's weight it accounts for effectively the entire index gain. Strip the technology names out of the Nifty 50 and the rest of it was eighteen up, eighteen down, one flat.

One thing you are owed about this week's record. Two of the five sessions never got a public write-up. There is no public brief for Tuesday and none for Wednesday, so anyone following only our public feed went from Monday evening to Thursday morning with nothing. The prices for all five sessions exist and they are used throughout. What was missing was our writing, not the observation.

## 5. The earnings week that was not one

Six Indian companies actually reported results this week, and all six filed on Tuesday and Wednesday. Not one of them is a company we can price. And not one price move in any of this week's seven briefs is attributed to a result.

Our own scan said fifteen companies. Reading the rows one by one, the file on this desk named for financial results contained no financial results at all. Thirteen of its rows are the exchange asking a company for a clarification, and four are companies replying to one. On Friday the exchange broadcast ten of those clarification requests in a single block inside thirteen minutes, every one of them for the quarter that ended in June. Ten inside thirteen minutes is one compliance sweep by the exchange, not ten company events.

Only one of the six real results landed while the market was open. Continuous trading ends at quarter past three and Ardee Industries filed at twenty-five to three, and that was on Tuesday, the session whose pipeline died.

So we did not fail to explain a results reaction. There was no reaction to explain.

What was still moving Indian shares this week were results reported before it. Positives first. LT Foods, a rice and packaged foods company, rose 7.3 per cent on Monday on a competitor's numbers rather than its own. KRBL's June quarter showed basmati realisations, meaning the average price a tonne of rice actually fetches, holding about twenty per cent higher than a year earlier despite West Asian shipping disruption. LT Foods finished the week up more than ten per cent, though it handed back roughly half of Monday's move before the close.

Now the negatives. KPI Green Energy, a solar power developer, showed the business and the balance sheet moving in opposite directions. Operating profit rose nineteen per cent and the operating margin, the share of every hundred rupees of sales kept as profit after running costs, improved. Net profit still fell fifteen per cent, because finance costs, which is the interest paid on borrowings, more than doubled. One quarter is not a pattern. The test is whether it persists into September.

IRCTC, the railway ticketing and catering company, is a mix problem. Revenue grew eighteen per cent while operating profit actually fell, because ticketing, which earns margins above eighty per cent, grew half a per cent while catering grew thirty-four. Growing fast in the low-margin half and standing still in the high-margin half does that to a profit line even when sales rise by nearly a fifth.

And the other half of reporting is largely missing. The call, where management takes questions, is where you find out what the numbers mean. Apar Industries' June-quarter transcript was thirty-four days pending by Thursday. Varun Beverages' is twenty-eight days pending, and that has a direct consequence. The company approved a new subsidiary to enter ready-to-drink alcoholic beverages this week, and nobody has been able to ask whether its bottling agreements even permit the parent to build one.

Against that, the week's one good process finding. Several of the smallest companies on the exchange ran a better disclosure calendar than several much larger ones. Juniper Green Energy filed a press release, a presentation, a call recording and a newspaper advertisement around its result, and pre-announced the call.

## 6. Three things worth understanding properly

First. An index is a weighted average, not a vote.

The Nifty 50 is not a count of how many of its fifty companies rose. It is an average in which every company is weighted by its size, so a very large company moves the number far more than a small one, and the biggest handful can outvote everybody else. The index answers how much money moved. Breadth, the count of risers against fallers, answers how many companies moved. Different questions, and this week they gave different answers four times out of five.

Friday is the clean demonstration. Nine technology shares were heavy enough to turn the whole scoreboard green while slightly more shares fell than rose. Thursday is the same thing one level down at a single company. One bank caused over half the index's fall and most other banks went up. Someone who saw "banks drag index lower" and concluded "banks are in trouble" would have drawn precisely the wrong conclusion from a true headline.

Second. A market can only react when it is open, and the clock is not the one you assume.

Indian continuous trading ends at quarter past three, and the closing auction, the short final window in which the official closing price of every share is set, ends at twenty-five to four. Nothing published after that can have moved that day's closing price. A company filing at half past six on a Thursday evening is not Thursday's news. It is Friday's. The rule runs both ways. Something filed on a Friday night or over a weekend does not disappear, it queues, and Monday is the first session that can price it. Vishal Mega Mart re-appointed its founder as chief executive in a filing at ten past eight on a Friday night, and the share rose nearly ten per cent the following Monday.

The strongest proof of this rule in the whole week is a mistake we made and then corrected. Nvidia reported after the American close on Wednesday. Revenue of ninety-six billion dollars, up one hundred and six per cent on the year, and guidance, meaning the company's own forecast for the coming quarter, of a hundred and eight billion against about a hundred and four expected. Indian traders had all of it before Thursday's open. And on Thursday, India did nothing with it. Nifty IT fell, while Korea rose one and a half per cent, Shanghai over one, Taiwan a third. Thursday's brief read that as India sitting out the biggest company result in the world.

On Friday the picture inverted and we said plainly what had been wrong. The American session that actually voted closed at half past one in the morning India time on Friday. The Nasdaq rose one and a half per cent, Nvidia itself rose nearly nine, and technology was the only American sector to advance. India opened against that close and Nifty IT gained three and a half per cent. There was never a lag. We had measured India against a press release, when the clock Indian technology shares actually track is the American close.

So the rule to carry away is this. Before believing any sentence of the form "shares fell because of X", check whether X happened while the market was open.

Third. "Nobody established why" is a finding, and this week it has a name and a price.

Companies have to tell the exchange about anything price-sensitive, and those filings are timestamped, so a filing can be matched to a move. But not everything that moves a share is a filing. The biggest category that is not is a block deal, which is a large pre-arranged trade where one holder sells a big parcel of shares directly to a buyer, away from the ordinary order book. It shows up in the exchange's record of large negotiated trades, and it very frequently explains a share that jumps with nothing filed. That record has been missing from our feed for this entire week and beyond.

The clean proof is the one case where the answer was found by hand. Ather Energy, the electric two-wheeler maker, rose about eight per cent on Friday to a fifty-two week closing high. Hero MotoCorp lifted its fully diluted holding to about thirty-three per cent through a block deal worth one thousand seven hundred and fifty-eight crore rupees, completing on the third of September. The only reason we have that is that somebody went and searched for it by hand.

Two things worth glossing in the same breath. That was a secondary transaction. Hero bought existing shares from existing owners, so no new money entered the company at all. That is a completely different thing from a company raising funds, and the two have the same headline shape.

So the argument writes itself. On one Friday name, the missing record was recovered by hand and it explained an eight per cent move completely. On the several names where it could not be recovered, the most likely explanation sits in a file nobody could open. "We do not know why" is a statement about a specific missing instrument, not about markets being unknowable.

And there is a size test you can apply with no data at all. Aegis Logistics fell more than five per cent on a day whose only filing was a tax penalty of about eleven lakh rupees, at a company worth forty-seven thousand crore. If the number in the explanation is thousands of times smaller than the company, it is not the explanation.
