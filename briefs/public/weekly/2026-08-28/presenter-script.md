# Presenter script: week ending Friday, 28 August 2026

*Read to camera. Recorded Saturday 29 August. Source: `briefs/public/weekly/2026-08-28.md` and nothing else.*

---

## 1. Hook

The headline barely moved this week. The market underneath it moved a great deal.

The Nifty 500, the broadest index we track, finished the week down one hundredth of one per cent. After five full sessions, that is dead flat.

Now the number that actually describes the week. Of roughly seven hundred shares we can price, three hundred and sixteen rose and three hundred and eighty-eight fell. The typical Indian share lost money in a week the headline called flat.

And the index and the typical share agreed with each other on exactly one session out of five.

Anyone who read this week off the index read almost none of it.

## 3. The week in one minute

The Nifty 50 closed at 24,175, down about a third of a per cent. It was a round trip rather than a quiet week. Tuesday was the high and Thursday the low.

Three forces pushed in three directions and roughly cancelled out, and none of them had anything to do with the other two.

Technology moved on an American clock, and Nifty IT, the index of the country's software services companies, finished the week up about two and a half per cent.

Oil fell on every single session. Brent crude, the benchmark grade India prices its imports against, went from about ninety-four dollars to about eighty-eight, roughly six per cent, and treat that as approximate for a reason I will come to at the end. The cause was diplomacy rather than demand. Iran and Oman resumed talks over the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow sea passage most of India's crude arrives through. Cheaper oil helps a country that imports about eighty-five per cent of what it refines.

And the third force, which nobody named. Companies that sell to Indian households were sold all week. On our own recomputed figures, and those are ours rather than the exchange's published table, food and household goods fell about one and a half per cent, and consumption and autos about one each. Those were the three largest sector falls of the week.

## 4. How the five days moved

Monday. The index lost a seventh of a per cent while four hundred shares fell, and the day's named cause pointed the wrong way. The United States Treasury Secretary announced what he called the greatest financial offensive ever mounted against Iran. And Brent fell nearly two per cent that day. A threat to oil supply that leaves oil cheaper has not been priced as a threat to oil supply. The announcement itself landed in the evening, after our market had shut. Monday traded the anticipation, not the contents.

Tuesday. The index rose to the week's high while three hundred and eighty-six shares fell. It was the monthly derivatives expiry, the day the month's futures and options contracts settle and positions in them have to be closed or rolled forward. That demand concentrates in a handful of very large companies, exactly the band an index weights most. Expiry propped up the index while most of the market sat out.

Wednesday. The exact reverse, one day later. The index fell half a per cent while three hundred and eighty-three shares rose. The United States paused visa appointments at its embassies worldwide, and that is why Nifty IT fell about one and a half per cent. But the index overstates it badly. The middle share in that index fell only four tenths of a per cent and twelve of its thirty-four members actually rose. Five very large companies were the whole move. One caution: the six-figure H-1B visa fee circulating alongside that story is a proposal, not a rule in force.

Thursday. The one session where the index told the truth. Four hundred and forty-nine shares fell and the middle share fell about as much as the index. Most of that was one company. HDFC Bank fell 2.2 per cent, and on roughly a thirteen per cent weight that single share is over half the index's decline. Do not conclude banks sold off. The other large private banks rose. What is wearing HDFC Bank down is its own arithmetic. Its net interest margin, the gap between what a bank earns on its loans and pays on its deposits, is at a record low of 3.26 per cent.

Friday. A green headline on a flat market. The index rose a third of a per cent while three hundred and sixty-one shares fell. One sector did it. Nifty IT rose three and a half per cent with all nine of its priced members higher, and that is effectively the entire index gain. Strip technology out of the Nifty 50 and the rest was eighteen up, eighteen down, one flat.

One thing you are owed about our own record. Two of this week's sessions never got a public write-up. There is no public brief for Tuesday and none for Wednesday, so anyone following only our public feed went from Monday evening to Thursday morning with nothing. The prices exist and are used throughout. What was missing was our writing, not the observation.

## 5. The earnings week that was not one

Six Indian companies actually reported results this week, all on Tuesday and Wednesday, and not one of them is a company we can price. Not one price move all week is attributed to a result.

Our own scan said fifteen. Reading the rows, the file on this desk named for financial results contained no financial results at all. Thirteen are the exchange asking a company for a clarification and four are companies replying to one. Ten of those requests were broadcast in a single block inside thirteen minutes on Friday. That is one compliance sweep, not ten company events. So we did not fail to explain a results reaction this week. There was no reaction to explain.

What was still moving shares were results reported earlier. LT Foods, a rice and packaged foods company, rose seven per cent on Monday on a competitor's numbers rather than its own, after KRBL's June quarter showed basmati realisations, meaning the average price a tonne of rice actually fetches, about twenty per cent higher than a year earlier. On the other side, IRCTC, the railway ticketing and catering company, grew revenue eighteen per cent and its operating profit still fell, because ticketing, which earns margins above eighty per cent, grew half a per cent while catering grew thirty-four.

## 6. Three things worth understanding properly

First. An index is a weighted average, not a vote.

The Nifty 50 is not a count of how many of its fifty companies rose. Every member is weighted by its size, so the biggest handful can outvote everybody else. The index answers how much money moved. Breadth, the count of risers against fallers, answers how many companies moved. Different questions, and this week they gave different answers four times out of five. On Friday, nine technology shares turned the whole scoreboard green while more shares fell than rose. On Thursday, one bank caused over half the index's fall while most other banks went up. Anyone who saw "banks drag index lower" and concluded "banks are in trouble" drew precisely the wrong conclusion from a true headline.

Second. A market can only react when it is open, and the clock is not the one you assume.

Indian continuous trading ends at quarter past three, and the closing auction, the short final window where the official closing price of every share is set, ends at twenty-five to four. Nothing published after that can have moved that day's close. A company filing on Thursday evening is not Thursday's news. It is Friday's.

The proof is a mistake we made and then corrected. Nvidia reported after the American close on Wednesday, with revenue up more than a hundred per cent on the year and its own forecast for the coming quarter ahead of expectations. Indian traders had all of it before Thursday's open, and on Thursday India did nothing with it. Nifty IT fell. Thursday's brief read that as India sitting out the biggest company result in the world.

On Friday the picture inverted and we said plainly what had been wrong. The American session that actually voted closed at half past one in the morning India time on Friday. The Nasdaq rose one and a half per cent and Nvidia itself rose nearly nine. India opened against that close and Nifty IT gained three and a half. There was never a lag. We had measured India against a press release, when the clock those shares track is the American close.

So before believing any sentence of the form "shares fell because of X", check whether X happened while the market was open.

Third. "Nobody established why" is a finding.

Companies have to tell the exchange about anything price-sensitive, and those filings are timestamped, so a filing can be matched to a move. But not everything that moves a share is a filing. The largest thing that is not is a block deal, which is a big pre-arranged trade where one holder sells a large parcel of shares directly to a buyer, away from the ordinary order book. It very often explains a share that jumps with nothing filed. The exchange's record of those trades has been missing from our feed all week and beyond.

The proof is the one case we found by hand. Ather Energy, the electric two-wheeler maker, rose about eight per cent on Friday to a fifty-two week closing high, and Hero MotoCorp lifted its fully diluted holding to about thirty-three per cent through a block deal worth one thousand seven hundred and fifty-eight crore rupees, completing on the third of September. Note what that is. Hero bought existing shares from an existing holder, so no new money entered the company. That is a different thing from a company raising funds, and the two have the same headline shape.

On the names where that record could not be recovered, the most likely explanation sits in a file nobody could open. "We do not know why" is a statement about a missing instrument, not about markets being unknowable.
