# Presenter script: week ending Friday, 28 August 2026

*Read to camera. Recorded Saturday 29 August. Source: `briefs/public/weekly/2026-08-28.md` and nothing else.*

## 1. Hook

The headline barely moved this week. The market underneath it moved a great deal.

The Nifty 500, the broadest index we track and the one that comes nearest to describing the whole listed market, finished the week down one hundredth of one per cent. After five full sessions of trading, that is dead flat.

Now the number that actually describes the week. Of roughly seven hundred and four shares we can price, three hundred and sixteen rose and three hundred and eighty-eight fell. So the typical Indian share lost money in a week the headline called flat.

And it gets worse day by day. The index and the typical share agreed with each other on exactly one session out of five. That was Thursday. On the other four the headline either pointed the wrong way or badly understated what happened.

Anyone who read this week off the index read almost none of it.

## 2. Before we begin

Before we begin: this is general market commentary, not investment advice. I am not a SEBI-registered Research Analyst.

## 3. The week in one minute

The Nifty 50 closed at 24,175, down about a third of a per cent. It was not a quiet week, it was a round trip. Tuesday's close was the week's high, Thursday's was the low, and Friday gave back a little under half of the fall.

Three separate forces pushed in three directions and roughly cancelled each other out, and none of them had anything to do with the other two.

The first was technology, moving on an American clock. Nifty IT, the index of the country's software services companies, finished the week up about two and a half per cent, and I will come back to why the timing of that matters more than the number.

The second was oil. Brent crude, the global benchmark grade India prices most of its imports against, fell on every single session, from about ninety-four dollars to about eighty-eight. That is roughly six per cent, and I would treat that as approximate rather than exact, for a reason I will get to at the end. The cause was diplomacy rather than demand. Iran and Oman resumed talks over the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow sea passage most of India's crude arrives through, discussing a temporary navigational corridor and agreeing to work on clearing mines. Cheaper oil is a tailwind for a country that imports about eighty-five per cent of what it refines.

The third force is the one nobody named. The domestic-demand complex sold off quietly all week. On our own recomputed figures, and I want to be clear those are ours and not the exchange's published weekly table, Nifty FMCG lost about one and a half per cent, Consumption about one, and Auto about one. Those are the three largest weekly sector falls in the market and no headline anywhere named any of them.

## 4. How the five days moved

Monday. The index lost a seventh of a per cent while four hundred shares fell and three hundred and two rose. The day's named cause was geopolitical and it pointed the wrong way. The United States Treasury Secretary announced what he called the single greatest financial offensive ever mounted against Iran, and the part that reaches India is that Washington says it will sanction any country that keeps trading with Iran. And yet Brent fell nearly two per cent on the day. A threat to oil supply that leaves oil cheaper has not been priced as a threat to oil supply. The announcement itself landed between about half past five and seven in the evening India time, after our market had shut. So Monday traded the anticipation, which had been pre-announced on Sunday. It did not trade the contents.

Tuesday. The index rose about half a per cent to the week's high while three hundred and eighty-six shares fell. This is the cleanest explanation of the week and it has nothing to do with mood. Tuesday was the National Stock Exchange's monthly derivatives expiry, the day the month's futures and options contracts settle and positions in them have to be closed or rolled forward. Settlement demand concentrates in a narrow band of very large, heavily traded companies, which is exactly the band an index weights most. Expiry propped up the index through a handful of giants while the rest of the market did not participate.

Wednesday. The exact reverse, one day later. The index fell about half a per cent while three hundred and eighty-three shares rose. The dated cause at the top was real and narrow. The United States paused visa appointments at its embassies and consulates worldwide while it re-screens and retrains consular officers, with no announced date for normal service to resume. That is why Nifty IT fell about one and a half per cent. But the index badly overstates it. Across the IT index's thirty-four members the middle share fell only four tenths of a per cent, twelve of them actually rose, and Cyient gained seven per cent. Five very large companies were effectively the whole move. One caution that travels with this story: the six-figure H-1B visa fee circulating alongside it is a proposal, not a rule in force.

Thursday. The one session where the index told the truth. It fell about half a per cent, four hundred and forty-nine shares fell against two hundred and fifty-three, and the middle share fell about the same as the index. It was the week's worst day for the typical shareholder and the only day the headline matched the experience. Most of the fall was one company. HDFC Bank fell 2.23 per cent, and on roughly a thirteen per cent weight in the index that single share takes about three fifths of the whole decline. Nobody should conclude from that that banks sold off. Kotak Mahindra rose 1.8 per cent that day, ICICI Bank rose nearly one, and the private banks as a group beat the market. What is wearing HDFC Bank down is its own arithmetic. Its net interest margin, which is the gap between what a bank earns on its loans and what it pays on its deposits, is at a record low of 3.26 per cent, and its cheapest deposits have fallen from thirty-eight per cent of the book two years ago to thirty-two.

Friday. A green headline on a flat market. The index rose about a third of a per cent and three hundred and sixty-one shares fell against three hundred and forty-three. It was one sector. Nifty IT rose three and a half per cent with all nine of its priced members higher, and on that sector's weight it accounts for effectively the entire index gain. Strip the technology names out of the Nifty 50 and the rest of it was eighteen up, eighteen down, one flat.

One thing you are owed about this week's record. Two of the five sessions never got a public write-up. There is no public brief for Tuesday and none for Wednesday, so anyone following only our public feed went from Monday evening to Thursday morning with nothing. The prices for all five sessions exist and they are used throughout. What was missing was our writing, not the observation.

## 5. The earnings week that was not one

Six Indian companies actually reported results this week, and all six filed on Tuesday and Wednesday. Not one of them is a company we can price. And not one price move in any of this week's seven briefs is attributed to a result.

Our own scan said fifteen companies. Reading the rows one by one, the file on this desk named for financial results contained no financial results at all. Thirteen of its rows are the exchange asking a company for a clarification, and four are companies replying to one. On Friday the exchange broadcast ten of those clarification requests in a single block inside thirteen minutes, every one of them for the quarter that ended in June. Ten inside thirteen minutes is one compliance sweep by the exchange, not ten company events.

Only one of the six real results landed while the market was open. Continuous trading ends at quarter past three and Ardee Industries filed at twenty-five to three, and that was on Tuesday, the session whose pipeline died.

So we did not fail to explain a results reaction. There was no reaction to explain.

What was still moving Indian shares this week were results reported before it. Positives first. LT Foods, a rice and packaged foods company, rose 7.3 per cent on Monday on a competitor's numbers rather than its own. KRBL's June quarter showed basmati realisations, meaning the average price a tonne of rice actually fetches, holding about twenty per cent higher than a year earlier despite West Asian shipping disruption. LT Foods finished the week up more than ten per cent, though it handed back roughly half of Monday's move before the close.

Now the negatives. KPI Green Energy, a solar power developer, showed the business and the balance sheet moving in opposite directions. Operating profit rose nineteen per cent and the operating margin, the share of every hundred rupees of sales kept as profit after running costs, improved. Net profit still fell fifteen per cent, because finance costs, which is the interest paid on borrowings, more than doubled. One quarter is not a pattern. The test is whether it persists into September.

IRCTC, the railway ticketing and catering company, is a mix problem. Revenue grew eighteen per cent while operating profit actually fell, because ticketing, which earns margins above eighty per cent, grew half a per cent while catering grew thirty-four. Growing fast in the low-margin half and standing still in the high-margin half does that to a profit line even when sales rise by nearly a fifth.

And the other half of reporting is largely missing. The call, where management takes questions, is where you find out what the numbers mean. Apar Industries' June-quarter transcript was thirty-four days pending by Thursday. Varun Beverages' is twenty-eight days pending, and that has a direct consequence. The company approved a new subsidiary to enter ready-to-drink alcoholic beverages this week, and nobody has been able to ask whether its bottling agreements even permit the parent to build one.

Against that, the week's one good process finding. Several of the smallest companies on the exchange ran a better disclosure calendar than several much larger ones. Juniper Green Energy filed a press release, a presentation, a call recording and a newspaper advertisement around its result, and pre-announced the call.

## 6. Three things worth understanding properly

First. An index is a weighted average, not a vote.

The Nifty 50 is not a count of how many of its fifty companies rose. It is an average in which every company is weighted by its size, so a very large company moves the number far more than a small one, and the biggest handful can outvote everybody else. The index answers how much money moved. Breadth, the count of risers against fallers, answers how many companies moved. Different questions, and this week they gave different answers four times out of five.

Friday is the clean demonstration. Nine technology shares were heavy enough to turn the whole scoreboard green while slightly more shares fell than rose. Thursday is the same thing one level down at a single company. One bank caused over half the index's fall and most other banks went up. Someone who saw "banks drag index lower" and concluded "banks are in trouble" would have drawn precisely the wrong conclusion from a true headline.

Second. A market can only react when it is open, and the clock is not the one you assume.

Indian continuous trading ends at quarter past three, and the closing auction, the short final window in which the official closing price of every share is set, ends at twenty-five to four. Nothing published after that can have moved that day's closing price. A company filing at half past six on a Thursday evening is not Thursday's news. It is Friday's. The rule runs both ways. Something filed on a Friday night or over a weekend does not disappear, it queues, and Monday is the first session that can price it. Vishal Mega Mart re-appointed its founder as chief executive in a filing at ten past eight on a Friday night, and the share rose nearly ten per cent the following Monday.

The strongest proof of this rule in the whole week is a mistake we made and then corrected. Nvidia reported after the American close on Wednesday. Revenue of ninety-six billion dollars, up one hundred and six per cent on the year, and guidance, meaning the company's own forecast for the coming quarter, of a hundred and eight billion against about a hundred and four expected. Indian traders had all of it before Thursday's open. And on Thursday, India did nothing with it. Nifty IT fell, while Korea rose one and a half per cent, Shanghai over one, Taiwan a third. Thursday's brief read that as India sitting out the biggest company result in the world.

On Friday the picture inverted and we said plainly what had been wrong. The American session that actually voted closed at half past one in the morning India time on Friday. The Nasdaq rose one and a half per cent, Nvidia itself rose nearly nine, and technology was the only American sector to advance. India opened against that close and Nifty IT gained three and a half per cent. There was never a lag. We had measured India against a press release, when the clock Indian technology shares actually track is the American close.

So the rule to carry away is this. Before believing any sentence of the form "shares fell because of X", check whether X happened while the market was open.

Third. "Nobody established why" is a finding, and this week it has a name and a price.

Companies have to tell the exchange about anything price-sensitive, and those filings are timestamped, so a filing can be matched to a move. But not everything that moves a share is a filing. The biggest category that is not is a block deal, which is a large pre-arranged trade where one holder sells a big parcel of shares directly to a buyer, away from the ordinary order book. It shows up in the exchange's record of large negotiated trades, and it very frequently explains a share that jumps with nothing filed. That record has been missing from our feed for this entire week and beyond.

The clean proof is the one case where the answer was found by hand. Ather Energy, the electric two-wheeler maker, rose about eight per cent on Friday to a fifty-two week closing high. Hero MotoCorp lifted its fully diluted holding to about thirty-three per cent through a block deal worth one thousand seven hundred and fifty-eight crore rupees, completing on the third of September. The only reason we have that is that somebody went and searched for it by hand.

Two things worth glossing in the same breath. That was a secondary transaction. Hero bought existing shares from existing owners, so no new money entered the company at all. That is a completely different thing from a company raising funds, and the two have the same headline shape.

So the argument writes itself. On one Friday name, the missing record was recovered by hand and it explained an eight per cent move completely. On the several names where it could not be recovered, the most likely explanation sits in a file nobody could open. "We do not know why" is a statement about a specific missing instrument, not about markets being unknowable.

And there is a size test you can apply with no data at all. Aegis Logistics fell more than five per cent on a day whose only filing was a tax penalty of about eleven lakh rupees, at a company worth forty-seven thousand crore. If the number in the explanation is thousands of times smaller than the company, it is not the explanation.

## 7. The movers, risers first

One warning about the list itself before I read any of it. Our screen only surfaces companies worth at least a thousand crore that moved at least six per cent. On Thursday it produced ten gainers and zero fallers, on a session where four hundred and forty-nine shares out of seven hundred fell. That is not the market, that is our own threshold. Do not read ten green rows as a good day.

Risers where we established a cause. Cyient, an engineering and technology services company, rose twenty-four per cent, the biggest gain in the screen, spread across four sessions, with its biggest day on its Investor Day. The honest limit is that nobody here has read what was presented. NMDC Steel rose twenty-three per cent, and a counterweight belongs beside it: the share was still down about eleven per cent over three months, so this is a recovery inside a decline. Ratnamani Metals and Tubes, which makes steel pipes and tubes, rose fourteen and a half per cent on Monday on export orders won by a subsidiary worth about two thousand seven hundred crore rupees. Two qualifications sit inside that filing. The buyer is described only as international customers and is not named, and the orders will be partly made in-house and partly bought in and sold on, with the split undisclosed. Buying something in and selling it on earns a thin margin. Making it yourself does not. The order is real. The margin mix the share price is paying for has not been published.

Bombay Burmah rose thirteen per cent and the mechanism is worth getting right. The Supreme Court recalled an observation it had made in May, that four thousand six hundred and fifty-five crore rupees of lease rent remained recoverable from the company on a former tea estate. It recalled that observation on procedural grounds, because the amount had never been the subject of a notice served on the company nor determined after a hearing. It did not rule that nothing is owed. The sum is undetermined, not extinguished. And note the dates. The order was a week old and the share did nothing on it. What moved it was the company's own disclosure of it, filed just before four in the afternoon on Wednesday. The market prices the disclosure, not the order.

Risers where we could not establish a cause, and where they stay unexplained. Ram Ratna Wires rose twenty-four per cent with nothing filed on any day, and two arms of this desk disagree about which company that ticker even is, which is unresolved. Standard Engineering Technology rose twenty-two per cent across five sessions with nothing disclosed behind any of it, and it is up more than a hundred and sixty per cent over three months. Four of its five daily closes came in just under five per cent. That is the signature of a share hitting a daily price limit, which is an exchange-imposed cap on how far it can move in one session. That is our own flag and not a sourced finding, and I will not assert it. But it should not be read as a market that chose to pay twenty-two per cent more for the company. IDBI Bank rose nearly thirteen per cent and is the most searched-for blank of the week. The government and Life Insurance Corporation are selling a combined sixty per cent stake and that story is real and public, but nothing in it is dated to the day the share moved, and a decision window running out is not an event.

Now the fallers. BLS International fell twelve per cent on the week and nearly eleven of that on Monday alone. Reports say Spain's National Court has widened an investigation into an alleged visa-fraud network at a Spanish consulate to examine the company's own document-processing role. The company filed a media statement at one in the afternoon, while the market was open, categorically rejecting the allegations, saying its role is strictly administrative and that decisions to issue or refuse a visa rest with the diplomatic authorities. The limits have to travel with that. The denial is confirmed from the primary filing. The allegation is reported only. It is not established that the company is a named party, an accused, or the subject of any order. That is enough to say the reports caused the fall. It is not enough to characterise the legal exposure. And why eleven per cent rather than two: the company's entire business is holding outsourced visa-processing contracts from sovereign governments, so what is at risk is the contract, not a fine.

Then there is the category I want you to sit with. Six of the week's twenty-two biggest movers appear in none of our seven briefs at all. Paradeep Phosphates, Balrampur Chini Mills, Piccadily Agro Industries, Crompton Greaves Consumer Electricals, Sanghvi Movers and CreditAccess Grameen. That is a different failure from the blanks I just read you. For IDBI Bank we looked and found nothing. For these six we did not look. Tuesday's run died mid-execution, and nothing anywhere in this process sends anybody back to a dead session. There is no step that re-opens a day already missed. So a share that made its move on the day the pipeline stalled is not merely unexplained on Tuesday. It is unexplained permanently, because the only pass it was ever going to get was the one that failed. Five of the week's ten biggest fallers are in that list.

Two smaller things worth knowing. Eight shares fell on Friday for a reason that is not a market reason at all. When a company pays a dividend, there is a cut-off date after which a new buyer no longer receives the payment, and on the first morning past that cut-off the share is worth exactly the dividend less than the night before, because the cash has left the company. Nothing has gone wrong. The value moved from the share price into the shareholder's bank account. Whirlpool's apparent fall of 3.3 per cent was really 2.7, and Mufti, which a raw screen shows falling two and a half per cent, did not actually move at all.

And institutions voted against controlling shareholders at seven companies in nine days, and were selective about it. At Gland Pharma, about forty-five to forty-seven per cent of institutional votes went against re-appointing two directors nominated by the controlling shareholder, on ninety-two per cent turnout, and the controlling shareholder's own majority carried them anyway. What makes it a story is what those same investors approved. At Gland, the institutions casting forty-five per cent against two directors voted a hundred per cent in favour of the accounts and the dividend. That is not a protest against everything. It is a targeted refusal, aimed at the resolutions where the controlling family stands to benefit. One number needs its context: Jay Bharat Maruti drew a hundred per cent against the chairman's pay, but on turnout of six per cent. A percentage without its turnout is not information.

One competitive event with a date on it. Ola Electric launched the S1Z scooter at seventy-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine rupees during Friday's session, with a Plus version at ninety-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine and deliveries from December, roughly twenty to thirty-five per cent below the one lakh to one and a quarter lakh bracket the market expected a rival launch to occupy. I am not going to characterise that as a price war, and the reason is specific. Our sources disagree with each other about what the rival scooter will actually cost, so the bracket being undercut is not itself settled. The launch, the price and the timing are facts. What they add up to is not yet one.

## 8. What to watch

Monday the thirty-first is four things landing in a single session, and it is worth saying that in advance because it will be much harder to say afterwards. A growth number before the open. An index reshuffle transacting in the closing auction. A foreign-currency funding window shutting. And the market's first chance to price a product event held over the weekend. When Monday's number appears, nobody will be able to say cleanly which of the four caused it, and anyone who tells you they can is guessing.

The growth number is India's national accounts for April to June, against 7.8 per cent in the March quarter. Carry the forecasts as a spread, because there is no consensus to collapse them into. India Ratings is at 6.9 per cent. The Reserve Bank at seven. Bank of Baroda at seven to seven point two. A fifty-eight economist poll median near seven point one. CareEdge at seven point three. And SBI Research on its own at eight. The cluster between roughly six point nine and seven point three would be a four-quarter low. The eight per cent outlier would be an acceleration. Those are not the same story and no published median reconciles them. There is also a trap inside the release itself: the same publication switches the industrial-production and price indices to a revised base year, so a number that looks like a jump or a drop against last quarter may partly be the new yardstick.

The MSCI index review transacts at Monday's close, with the new composition applying from the first of September. Funds whose whole job is to hold exactly what the index holds do not get an opinion. When the index changes, they must buy the joiners and sell the leavers, in the closing auction, because that is the price the index will use. That is mechanical buying and selling by people with no view, which is why it can move a share on a day when nothing about the company changed. We are printing no single figure for how much money that moves, and the reason is instructive. Four estimates are circulating and they measure different things. They are not a range of the same quantity. They are different quantities that got compared.

The Reserve Bank's concessional foreign-currency deposit window shuts on Monday, and it is three dates rather than one. These are dollar accounts Indians living abroad hold with Indian banks, and the central bank ran a temporary scheme letting banks swap those dollars into rupees on favourable terms. New deposits stop on the thirty-first of August. Swaps against already-eligible deposits run to the eleventh of September. The related borrowing scheme runs to the end of December. The scheme does not end on Monday. One of its three legs does. It matters because reserves have risen four weeks running, the latest by over twelve billion dollars, and that build is largely why reserves rose about thirty-four billion in three weeks while the rupee sat near three-week lows. That is swap-funded, not strength.

And Monday has to absorb one imported input. The American central bank's Jackson Hole keynote landed about half past seven on Friday evening India time, after our market had closed, so it explains nothing about this week. It is Monday's input, not last week's. Kevin Warsh, in his first keynote as Chair, said inflation is running above the two per cent target and the Fed's predominant focus should be on prices, that summer readings came in better than expected but do not tell him the underlying trend has meaningfully improved, and he declined to commit to any forward guidance. Markets here have been positioned for American rates to come down. A Fed chair saying he may have to put them up is the opposite setting, and higher American rates make American assets more attractive relative to Indian ones. We are printing no probability figure for a September rate rise, because the number in circulation could not be checked at source.

The rest of the calendar, quickly. Tuesday brings monthly vehicle sales, which is the first read on whether the new lower price points are shifting anything. Thursday the third of September, the Hero purchase of Ather shares completes, and again, that is a purchase from an existing shareholder, so no new capital enters the company. Friday the fourth, the Reserve Bank's weekly statistical release, and whether a fifth consecutive reserve build arrives. And the comment window on the Reserve Bank's draft rules for non-bank lenders closed on Friday the twenty-eighth. Say the word draft. A closed comment window is the end of consultation, not the start of obligation. Nothing was enacted and nothing binds anybody, and that is exactly the moment people start assuming a rule exists.

There is one event I deliberately cannot report. Ather Energy's Community Day was scheduled for Saturday the twenty-ninth, after every brief in this week was written, with a new scooter expected. No dated report of the outcome exists in anything we can reach. Every retrievable source is pre-event copy written in the future tense, and the manufacturer's own model page says the scooter has not been launched and no official price has been announced. If you search for it, a summary will tell you confidently that it launched, at a price. That is pre-event copy rewritten in the past tense. We do not know the price, the specification or the reception, and Monday is the first Indian session that can price any of it. And to be clear about the week just gone: Friday's move in Ather was the block deal, not the scooter.

One last observation, and it is not a forecast. India VIX, which measures what traders pay to insure against a sharp index move over the next month, closed the week about one per cent above its own fifty-two week low. So the market is charging close to nothing to insure against a surprise, going into a weekend with four of them in it.

## 9. Where we got it wrong

This is the section I would most like to skip, and it is the reason to watch.

Last week's brief published ten things to watch, and promised that a watch list published in a weekly gets graded in the next weekly. This is the first grade under that rule, so the rule is on trial as much as the list is.

Four items fired and were covered properly. The monthly settlement showed up exactly as described in advance. The Jio Financial shareholder meeting fired and the reason for watching held. The Jackson Hole slot was pinned in advance and correctly dated. And the comment deadline on the draft lending rules fired and turned up something better than the item had anticipated.

Now the miss, and it is the one that matters. Last week's brief flagged three companies to watch on Monday: Lloyds Luxuries, Manipal Hospitals and Winsome Interiors. They had filed results on a Friday we published nothing on, so Monday was the first session that could price them. None of the three appears anywhere in any of this week's seven briefs. A full-text search returns zero hits for each name.

And here is the part that convicts us. The brief that flagged them added a sentence saying it was flagging them precisely because they were the same shape as a failure it had just described. So we identified a specific failure mode, which is pointing readers at named reactions and then never reporting them. We named it. We wrote a new item in exactly that shape. We warned in writing that it was the same shape. And then we reproduced it exactly. Naming a failure was not a control against it. The check that would have caught it is three ticker lookups in a file this desk builds every evening anyway.

Two further items were quietly dropped and never graded at all. A reported American trade delegation, carried with the correct warning that it was single-sourced and unconfirmed, appears in no brief after the day it was raised. Nobody went back. The failure is not flagging something unverified. It is flagging something unverified and never returning to it.

There is also an error still in print. Last week's brief said a thirty-six thousand crore figure circulating against the Jio Financial vote traces to a vote at a different company. That is wrong. It traces to a postal ballot at a subsidiary in the same group, on a different transaction in a different year. So this is a correction of a correction, and the wrong version is the one that is published. The circulating figure is still wrong and the number to hold is still the seven thousand two hundred crore confirmed from the meeting notice itself. But our stated reason for rejecting it was inaccurate. Added specificity is not added evidence.

We corrected ourselves nine times this week across four briefs. More often than we were corrected, which is the right direction, and we corrected one thing three times in three days, which is not. The one to lead with is the clock, and I have already told you that story. Thursday's brief led on India having sat out the biggest company result in the world. Friday's brief corrected it in its own lead paragraph rather than in a footnote, and did not disown the original reporting. The reporting was right and the framing was wrong. We had measured against a press release when the clock those shares track is the American close. That is the model for how a correction should be handled.

And one correction was itself withdrawn the next day, which should read as low confidence rather than as a fix. Anyone following our commodity numbers this week was given three different reasons in three days for the same problem. Each step was an improvement and each was labelled, and the net effect is that our confidence in our own commodity data this week should read as low. The weekly direction and the two endpoints for crude are solid. That is why I asked you to treat the roughly six per cent figure as approximate.

Finally, the instruments. Our weekly sector table was wrong for nine of its eleven sectors, four of them with the sign reversed, and it is the third consecutive week that same block has failed the same way. That is why every sector figure in this episode is our own recomputation from closing prices rather than the published table. The record of large negotiated trades was unavailable on every single session. And nine of the eleven exchange sector indices were unreadable on our feed on most days, so everything except technology and pharmaceuticals is a reconstruction from individual shares.
