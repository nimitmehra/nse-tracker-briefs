# Presenter script: week ending Friday, 28 August 2026

*Read to camera. Recorded Saturday 29 August. Source: `briefs/public/weekly/2026-08-28.md` and nothing else.*

---

## 1. Hook

The headline barely moved this week. The market underneath it moved a great deal.

The Nifty 500, the broadest index we track, finished the week down one hundredth of one per cent. After five full sessions, that is dead flat. But of roughly seven hundred shares we can price, three hundred and sixteen rose and three hundred and eighty-eight fell. The typical Indian share lost money in a week the headline called flat, and the index and the typical share agreed on exactly one session out of five.

Anyone who read this week off the index read almost none of it.

## 2. Before we begin

Before we begin: this is general market commentary, not investment advice. I am not a SEBI-registered Research Analyst.

## 3. The week in one minute

The Nifty 50 closed at 24,175, down about a third of a per cent, on a round trip.

Three forces pushed in three directions and cancelled out. Technology moved on an American clock. Oil fell on every session, with Brent crude down from about ninety-four dollars to about eighty-eight after Iran and Oman resumed talks over the Strait of Hormuz. Call that roughly six per cent, and treat it as approximate, for a reason I will give at the end.

And the third force, which nobody named. Companies that sell to Indian households were sold all week. On our own recomputed figures, food and household goods fell about one and a half per cent, and consumption and autos about one each, the three largest sector falls of the week.

## 4. How the five days moved

Monday: down a seventh of a per cent, four hundred shares falling, and the named cause pointed the wrong way. Washington announced what it called the greatest financial offensive ever mounted against Iran, Brent fell nearly two per cent — and that announcement landed after our market had shut.

Tuesday and Wednesday inverted each other. Tuesday made the week's high while three hundred and eighty-six shares fell — monthly derivatives expiry, whose demand lands on the very large companies an index weights most. Wednesday the index fell half a per cent while three hundred and eighty-three shares rose: the United States paused visa appointments worldwide, so Nifty IT fell about one and a half per cent, though the middle share in it fell only four tenths.

Thursday, the one session where the index told the truth. Four hundred and forty-nine shares fell, and most of it was one company: HDFC Bank down 2.2 per cent, over half the fall on a thirteen per cent weight. The other large private banks rose, so this was not banks selling off.

Friday, a green headline on a flat market: up a third of a per cent, three hundred and sixty-one shares down, and Nifty IT up three and a half was the whole gain.

And two of those sessions never got a public write-up — none for Tuesday, none for Wednesday. Our feed went from Monday evening to Thursday morning with nothing.

## 5. The earnings week that was not one

Six Indian companies actually reported results this week, not one of them a company we can price, and not one price move all week is attributed to a result.

Our own scan said fifteen, and the file named for financial results contained none. Thirteen of its rows are the exchange asking companies for clarifications, ten of them broadcast in one block inside thirteen minutes on Friday. That is one compliance sweep, not ten company events. So there was no results reaction to explain.

## 6. Two things worth understanding properly

First: a market can only react when it is open, and the clock is not the one you assume. Trading ends at quarter past three; the closing auction that sets the official close ends at twenty-five to four. Nothing published after that moved that day's close.

The proof is a mistake we made and corrected. Nvidia reported after the American close on Wednesday. India had it before Thursday's open and did nothing with it, and Thursday's brief read that as India sitting out the biggest company result in the world. Friday inverted it: the session that actually voted closed at half past one on Friday morning India time, Nvidia up nearly nine per cent, India opened against that close, and Nifty IT gained three and a half. There was never a lag. We had measured India against a press release, when the clock those shares track is the American close.

Second: "nobody established why" is a finding. Exchange filings are timestamped, so a filing can be matched to a move — but not everything that moves a share is a filing. The biggest exception is a block deal: a large pre-arranged trade, one holder selling a big parcel straight to a buyer, away from the ordinary order book. The exchange's record of those was missing from our feed all week.

The one case we recovered by hand proves the point. Ather Energy, the electric two-wheeler maker, rose about eight per cent on Friday to a fifty-two week closing high, on a block deal: Hero MotoCorp lifted its fully diluted holding to about thirty-three per cent, buying one thousand seven hundred and fifty-eight crore rupees of stock from an existing holder. No new money entered the company. Where that record could not be recovered, the likeliest explanation sits in a file nobody could open.

## 7. The movers, and the ones we never looked at

Where we established a cause, Cyient, an engineering services company, rose twenty-four per cent, its biggest day on its Investor Day. Where we could not, IDBI Bank rose nearly thirteen per cent with nothing dated to the day it moved. The largest faller was BLS International, down twelve per cent on reports that Spain's National Court has widened a visa-fraud investigation to its document-processing role. The company rejected that in a filing while the market was open: the denial is confirmed, the allegation reported only.

And six of the week's twenty-two biggest movers appear in none of our seven briefs. That is a different failure. For IDBI we looked and found nothing. For these six we did not look, because Tuesday's run died mid-execution and nothing here sends anybody back to a dead session.

One competitive event with a date on it. Ola Electric launched the S1Z scooter at seventy-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine rupees during Friday's session, below the bracket the market expected a rival launch to occupy. Our sources disagree about what the rival scooter will cost. The launch, the price and the timing are facts. What they add up to is not yet one.

## 8. What to watch

Monday the thirty-first is four things landing in one session, and it is worth saying so in advance because it will be much harder to say afterwards. A growth number before the open. An index reshuffle transacting in the closing auction, where funds that must hold exactly what an index holds buy the joiners and sell the leavers, which is mechanical buying by people with no view. A foreign-currency deposit window shutting. And the first chance to price a product event held over the weekend. Nobody will be able to say which of the four moved the market.

The growth number is India's national accounts for April to June, against 7.8 per cent in the March quarter. Carry the forecasts as a spread, because there is no consensus to collapse them into. They run from 6.9 per cent at one house to eight at another, most clustered between 6.9 and 7.3. That cluster would be a four-quarter low, the eight an acceleration, and no published median reconciles them.

One imported input for Monday. The American central bank's Jackson Hole keynote landed on Friday evening India time, after our market had closed, so it explains nothing about last week. And we print no probability figure for an American rate rise, because the number in circulation could not be checked at source.

The comment window on the Reserve Bank's draft rules for non-bank lenders closed on Friday. Say the word draft. A closed comment window is the end of consultation, not the start of obligation. Nothing was enacted.

And one event I deliberately cannot report. Ather Energy's Community Day was scheduled for Saturday the twenty-ninth, after every brief this week was written, with a new scooter expected. No dated report of the outcome exists in anything we can reach, and the maker's own page says the scooter has not been launched and no price announced. Any search summary telling you confidently that it launched at a price is pre-event copy rewritten in the past tense. And Friday's move in Ather was the block deal, not the scooter.

## 9. Where we got it wrong

This is the section I would most like to skip, and it is the reason to watch.

Last week's brief published ten things to watch and promised that the list gets graded in the next weekly. This is the first grade. Four items fired and were covered.

Now the miss. Last week's brief flagged three companies to watch on Monday: Lloyds Luxuries, Manipal Hospitals and Winsome Interiors. A full-text search of this week's seven briefs returns zero hits for each name. And here is the part that convicts us. The brief that flagged them said it was flagging them precisely because they were the same shape as a failure it had just described. So we identified the failure mode, which is pointing readers at named reactions and then never reporting them. We named it. We wrote a new item in exactly that shape. We warned in writing that it was the same shape. And then we reproduced it exactly. Naming a failure was not a control against it. The check that would have caught it is three lookups in a file this desk builds every evening anyway.

There is also an error still in print. Last week we said a thirty-six thousand crore figure circulating against the Jio Financial vote traces to a vote at a different company. That is wrong. It traces to a ballot at a subsidiary in the same group. So this is a correction of a correction, and the wrong version is the published one.

We corrected ourselves nine times this week across four briefs. The one to lead with is the clock, and you have already had that story: the reporting was right and the framing was wrong. And one of the nine was itself withdrawn the next day, which should read as low confidence rather than as a fix. That was our commodity data, and it is why I asked you to treat the six per cent crude figure as approximate.

And two instruments. Our weekly sector table was wrong for nine of its eleven sectors, four with the sign reversed, for the third week running. That is why every sector figure here is our own recomputation, not the exchange's table. And the record of large negotiated trades was unavailable on every session.
