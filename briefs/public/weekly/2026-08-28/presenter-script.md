## 1. Hook

The headline barely moved this week. The market underneath it moved a great deal.

The Nifty 500, the broadest index we track, finished the week down one hundredth of one per cent. After five full sessions, that is dead flat.

But of roughly seven hundred shares we can price, three hundred and sixteen rose and three hundred and eighty-eight fell. The typical Indian share lost money in a week the headline called flat. And the index and the typical share agreed with each other on exactly one session out of five.

Anyone who read this week off the index read almost none of it.

## 3. The week in one minute

The Nifty 50 closed at 24,175, down about a third of a per cent, on a round trip. Tuesday was the high and Thursday the low.

Three forces pushed in three directions and cancelled out. Technology moved on an American clock, and I will come back to that. Oil fell on every single session, with Brent crude down from about ninety-four dollars to about eighty-eight after Iran and Oman resumed talks over the Strait of Hormuz. Call that roughly six per cent, and treat it as approximate, for a reason I will give you at the end.

And the third force, which nobody named. Companies that sell to Indian households were sold all week. On our own recomputed figures, food and household goods fell about one and a half per cent, and consumption and autos about one each. Those were the three largest sector falls of the week.

## 4. How the five days moved

Monday. The index lost a seventh of a per cent while four hundred shares fell, and the named cause pointed the wrong way. The American Treasury Secretary announced what he called the greatest financial offensive ever mounted against Iran, and Brent fell nearly two per cent that day. That announcement landed after our market had shut, so Monday traded the anticipation, not the contents.

Tuesday. The index rose to the week's high while three hundred and eighty-six shares fell. It was the monthly derivatives expiry, when the month's futures and options contracts settle. That demand lands on a handful of very large companies, exactly the band an index weights most.

Wednesday. The exact reverse a day later. The index fell half a per cent while three hundred and eighty-three shares rose. The United States paused visa appointments at its embassies worldwide, which is why Nifty IT fell about one and a half per cent, though the index overstates that badly. The middle share in it fell only four tenths of a per cent.

Thursday. The one session where the index told the truth. Four hundred and forty-nine shares fell, and the middle share fell about as much as the index. Most of that was one company. HDFC Bank fell 2.2 per cent, and on a thirteen per cent weight that single share is over half the index's decline. The other large private banks rose, so this was not banks selling off. It is HDFC Bank's own problem, a net interest margin, the gap between what a bank earns on loans and pays on deposits, now at a record low.

Friday. A green headline on a flat market. The index rose a third of a per cent while three hundred and sixty-one shares fell. Nifty IT rose three and a half per cent with all nine of its priced members higher, effectively the whole gain.

And one thing you are owed about our own record. Two of this week's sessions never got a public write-up. There is no public brief for Tuesday and none for Wednesday, so anyone following our public feed went from Monday evening to Thursday morning with nothing.

## 5. The earnings week that was not one

Six Indian companies actually reported results this week, and not one is a company we can price. Not one price move all week is attributed to a result.

Our own scan said fifteen. The file on this desk named for financial results contained no financial results at all. Thirteen rows are the exchange asking a company for a clarification, and ten of those went out in one block inside thirteen minutes on Friday. That is one compliance sweep, not ten company events. So we did not fail to explain a results reaction. There was no reaction to explain.

What did move shares were results reported earlier. LT Foods, a rice and packaged foods company, rose seven per cent on Monday on a competitor's numbers, after KRBL's June quarter showed the price a tonne of basmati holding about twenty per cent higher than a year before.

## 6. Three things worth understanding properly

First. An index is a weighted average, not a vote. Every member is weighted by its size, so the biggest handful can outvote everybody else. The index answers how much money moved. Breadth, the count of risers against fallers, answers how many companies moved. Anyone who saw "banks drag index lower" on Thursday and concluded that banks were in trouble drew the wrong conclusion from a true headline.

Second. A market can only react when it is open, and the clock is not the one you assume. Indian trading ends at quarter past three, and the closing auction, where the official closing price is set, ends at twenty-five to four. Nothing published after that can have moved that day's close.

The proof is a mistake we made and then corrected. Nvidia reported after the American close on Wednesday. Indian traders had all of it before Thursday's open and India did nothing with it, and Thursday's brief read that as India sitting out the biggest company result in the world. Then on Friday the picture inverted. The American session that actually voted closed at half past one on Friday morning India time, with Nvidia up nearly nine per cent. India opened against that close and Nifty IT gained three and a half. There was never a lag. We had measured India against a press release, when the clock those shares track is the American close.

So before believing any sentence of the form "shares fell because of X", check whether X happened while the market was open.

Third. "Nobody established why" is a finding. Filings to the exchange are timestamped, so a filing can be matched to a move. But not everything that moves a share is a filing. The biggest thing that is not is a block deal, a large pre-arranged trade where one holder sells a big parcel of shares directly to a buyer, away from the ordinary order book. That record has been missing from our feed all week.

The one case we recovered by hand proves the point. Ather Energy, the electric two-wheeler maker, rose about eight per cent on Friday to a fifty-two week closing high, and Hero MotoCorp lifted its fully diluted holding to about thirty-three per cent through a block deal worth one thousand seven hundred and fifty-eight crore rupees. Hero bought existing shares from an existing holder, so no new money entered the company. Where that record could not be recovered, the likeliest explanation sits in a file nobody could open.

## 7. The movers, and the ones we never looked at

One warning about the list. Our screen only shows companies worth a thousand crore or more that moved at least six per cent, so on Thursday it produced ten gainers and no fallers on a session where four hundred and forty-nine shares fell. Do not read ten green rows as a good day.

Where we established a cause, Cyient, an engineering services company, rose twenty-four per cent, with its biggest day on its Investor Day. Where we could not, IDBI Bank rose nearly thirteen per cent with nothing dated to the day it moved, and a decision window running out is not an event. The largest faller was BLS International, down twelve per cent, on reports that Spain's National Court has widened a visa-fraud investigation to examine the company's document-processing role. The company rejected that in a filing while the market was open. The denial is confirmed from the filing. The allegation is reported only, and it is not established that the company is a named party or an accused of anything.

And six of the week's twenty-two biggest movers appear in none of our seven briefs at all. That is a different failure from the blanks. For IDBI we looked and found nothing. For these six we did not look, because Tuesday's run died mid-execution and nothing in this process sends anybody back to a dead session.

One competitive event with a date on it. Ola Electric launched the S1Z scooter at seventy-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine rupees during Friday's session, well below the bracket the market expected a rival launch to occupy. I am not going to frame that as one company declaring war on another's prices, because our sources disagree about what the rival scooter will actually cost. The launch, the price and the timing are facts. What they add up to is not yet one.

## 8. What to watch

Monday the thirty-first is four things landing in one session, and it is worth saying so in advance because it will be much harder to say afterwards. A growth number before the open. An index reshuffle transacting in the closing auction, where funds that have to hold exactly what the index holds buy the joiners and sell the leavers, which is mechanical buying by people with no view. A foreign-currency deposit window shutting. And the first chance to price a product event held over the weekend. When Monday's number appears, nobody will be able to say cleanly which of the four caused it.

The growth number is India's national accounts for April to June, against 7.8 per cent in the March quarter. Carry the forecasts as a spread, because there is no consensus to collapse them into. They run from 6.9 per cent at one house to eight per cent at another, with most of them clustered between 6.9 and 7.3. That cluster would be a four-quarter low. The eight would be an acceleration. No published median reconciles them. And we are printing no single figure for how much money the index reshuffle moves, because the estimates in circulation measure different things.

The American central bank's Jackson Hole keynote landed on Friday evening India time, after our market had closed, so it explains nothing about last week. It is Monday's input. Kevin Warsh, in his first keynote as Chair, said inflation is running above target and declined to commit to any forward guidance. We are printing no probability figure for a September rate rise, because the number in circulation could not be checked at source.

The comment window on the Reserve Bank's draft rules for non-bank lenders closed on Friday. Say the word draft. A closed comment window is the end of consultation, not the start of obligation. Nothing was enacted and nothing binds anybody.

And one event I deliberately cannot report. Ather Energy's Community Day was scheduled for Saturday the twenty-ninth, after every brief in this week was written, with a new scooter expected. No dated report of the outcome exists in anything we can reach, and the manufacturer's own page says the scooter has not been launched and no price announced. If you search for it, a summary will tell you confidently that it launched, at a price. That is pre-event copy rewritten in the past tense. Monday is the first session that can price any of it, and Friday's move in Ather was the block deal, not the scooter.

## 9. Where we got it wrong

This is the section I would most like to skip, and it is the reason to watch.

Last week's brief published ten things to watch and promised that a watch list gets graded in the next weekly. This is the first grade. Four items fired and were covered properly.

Now the miss. Last week's brief flagged three companies to watch on Monday: Lloyds Luxuries, Manipal Hospitals and Winsome Interiors. None of the three appears anywhere in any of this week's seven briefs. A full-text search returns zero hits for each name. And here is the part that convicts us. The brief that flagged them said it was flagging them precisely because they were the same shape as a failure it had just described. So we identified the failure mode, which is pointing readers at named reactions and then never reporting them. We named it. We wrote a new item in exactly that shape. We warned in writing that it was the same shape. And then we reproduced it exactly. Naming a failure was not a control against it. The check that would have caught it is three ticker lookups in a file this desk builds every evening anyway.

There is also an error still in print. Last week we said a thirty-six thousand crore figure circulating against the Jio Financial vote traces to a vote at a different company. That is wrong. It traces to a postal ballot at a subsidiary in the same group. So this is a correction of a correction, and the wrong version is the published one. Added specificity is not added evidence.

We corrected ourselves nine times this week across four briefs, which is more often than we were corrected. The one to lead with is the clock, and I have already told you that story. The reporting was right and the framing was wrong, and Friday's brief said so in its own lead paragraph rather than in a footnote. And one of the nine was itself withdrawn the next day, which should read as low confidence rather than as a fix. That was our commodity data, and it is why I asked you to treat the six per cent crude figure as approximate.

And two instruments. Our weekly sector table was wrong for nine of its eleven sectors, four with the sign reversed, for the third consecutive week. That is why every sector figure in this episode is our own recomputation and not the exchange's published table. And the record of large negotiated trades was unavailable on every session.
