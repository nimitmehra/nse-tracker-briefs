## 1. The week in plain English

The Nifty 50 finished the week at 24,570.65, up 0.77%. The whole of that gain was made in one morning. Monday rose 1.60%, and the four days after it gave back about eight tenths of a percent between them. Friday itself closed down 0.27%. So the plain summary of the week is: one very good Monday, then four days of slow drift lower that did not quite undo it.

Three things mattered, and each of them can be said in a single sentence.

**Oil got much cheaper over the weekend, and India buys most of its oil from abroad.** Brent crude, the benchmark price most of the world's oil trades against, fell 7.30% across Saturday and Sunday to $83.54 a barrel. India imports roughly 85% of the oil it burns, so a cheaper barrel means a smaller import bill, fewer rupees leaving the country, and less upward pressure on prices in the shops. That is the reason Monday happened.

**About 190 companies reported their quarterly results, and the market paid for profit margins rather than for growth.** Margin is the share of every rupee of sales that is left over as profit after costs. Companies that grew their sales fast but kept less of each rupee were sold. Companies whose costs behaved were bought. Several of the best sets of results published all week fell on the day they came out, because the share had already risen in anticipation.

**On Thursday evening the Reserve Bank of India published a draft rule that would stop finance companies that are not banks from offering revolving credit, and on Friday the market treated the draft as though it were already law.** Revolving credit is a borrowing limit you can repay and then draw on again, the way a credit card works. Bajaj Finance, the largest of those lenders, fell 5.84%. The document is a consultation paper. Comments are open until 28 August. Nothing has been decided, and nothing has changed for any borrower yet.

One more fact belongs up here, and we cannot explain it. Small companies had a far better week than large ones: the Nifty Smallcap 250 rose 2.44%, roughly three times the Nifty 50's 0.77%. Section 9 sets out what we know about that and what we do not.

**Monday 3 August is the story of the week, and its cause is well established.** Two things happened over the weekend, both of them before Indian trading opened at 09:15 on Monday, so both were fair game as causes. OPEC+, the group of oil-producing countries that coordinates how much they pump, met on Sunday and agreed to raise output from September, finishing the process of restoring the 2.2 million barrels a day it had voluntarily held back. And Donald Trump called off a planned strike on Iran. Oil fell 7.30% on the two together. Separately, on Saturday 1 August, India's carmakers published July's numbers: more than 4.7 lakh units, up 33%, the strongest month the industry has ever had. Monday was the first session anyone could trade any of it. The market gapped up at the open and held the gain all day. 534 stocks in our 680-stock universe rose against 144 that fell, which is close to four risers for every faller, and the typical stock gained 1.35%.

**One honest caveat about Monday's number.** Monday was also day one of a new method for setting closing prices (Section 6 explains it). Our own briefs disagree about it. Monday's brief rebuilt the index from its individual companies and got 1.74%, which supports the 1.60% figure. Tuesday's brief looked at the same evidence a day later and concluded that part of Monday's close was probably the new mechanism rather than genuine buying. Both readings are on our record. The direction is certain and the size is not, and the count of risers to fallers is the more reliable measure of what Monday was.

**Tuesday 4 August fell 0.64% on no news at all.** Three explanations were available and two of them were machinery rather than information: the weekly derivatives settlement fell on that day, and the new closing auction was on its second day. The third was simply caution ahead of the Reserve Bank's rate decision the following morning, an event that had not happened yet. The fall was also narrow. Across our own universe, which leans towards larger companies, 279 rose against 392 that fell, but across the whole exchange it was 2,030 up against 2,037 down, essentially even. The selling was concentrated where the large institutions own things and never reached the smaller companies. Property was the day's worst corner, and it fell on its own results rather than on anything the central bank did (Section 5).

**Wednesday 5 August was almost flat for large companies and the best day of the week for small ones.** The Reserve Bank held its policy rate at 5.25%, a fourth hold in a row, decided unanimously. Nobody had expected anything else, so the decision itself carried no new information, and across Wednesday and Thursday together the Nifty moved a total of nine hundredths of one percent. That is a market that had already worked the answer out. The genuinely new content was in the Bank's forecasts, covered in Section 6. We did not publish a brief on Wednesday because our pipeline did not run, which is dealt with honestly in Section 9.

**Thursday 6 August looked flat and was not.** The Nifty closed up 0.05% while 371 stocks fell and only 302 rose. The index finished green because Reliance Industries rose 3.52%, and at roughly 8% of the index that single company is worth about 0.28 percentage points on its own. We could not establish any Thursday-dated reason for the Reliance move. Underneath it the day was entirely about results, sorted hard: five companies published strong quarters and the market bought none of them, while two that grew sales with weakening margins were hit hard. India also traded through the session in which Asia's dominant trade broke, with Korea's KOSPI down 4.58% and the chipmaker SK Hynix down 10.37% on doubts about whether the money going into artificial-intelligence hardware will ever earn a return. India closing up 0.05% against that is non-participation, not strength.

**Friday 7 August fell 0.27% because of one document.** The Reserve Bank's draft rule on non-bank lenders, published the previous evening, took Bajaj Finance down 5.84%, Bajaj Finserv down 3.70%, Shriram Finance down 2.28% and Jio Financial down 2.39%. The Sensex fell more than the Nifty (0.58% against 0.27%) for an arithmetic reason and not a second reason: the Sensex carries more financial-sector weight, and financials were where the damage was. The other half of the day went up. Hindalco filed a record quarter and rose 3.17%. Siemens Energy India rose 12.19%. State Bank of India, which the draft does not touch because it is a bank, rose 1.12% on its own results. An index down a quarter of a percent with names like those rising underneath it is what a one-sector event looks like, not a market-wide one.

**A note on what you may have read elsewhere.** The common Friday framing in the press was that Indian shares fell on rising crude and West Asian tension, with lenders as the drag. Each of those statements is separately true and the connection between them is wrong. Crude rose on a transit arrangement in the Gulf. Lenders fell on a regulator's consultation paper published the night before. The two have nothing to do with each other.

## 3. The market underneath the index: breadth and flows

**Breadth is a count, not an average.** It asks how many separate companies went up and how many went down, rather than how far the index travelled. An index can rise because one enormous company rose, or because most companies rose, and only the count tells you which of those happened. Thursday is the perfect illustration: the Nifty closed green on a day when most of the market fell, because Reliance had a good day.

**Over the full week, 427 shares rose and 259 fell, out of 686 we track.** That is 62 out of every 100 finishing the week higher. So the week's gain was reasonably broad, and it was not the work of a handful of giants. Monday is what did it. A session with 534 risers to 144 fallers puts most stocks net higher for the week, and the three published down days that followed chipped away at that without reversing it.

**Money flows are the other half of the picture, and this week our record is patchy.** Two pools of professional money dominate Indian trading. Foreign institutional investors are money arriving from outside India. Domestic institutional investors are mostly Indian mutual funds and insurance companies, which is largely your and my monthly savings arriving in the market. They often pull in opposite directions.

What we can say with confidence: **domestic institutions were the buyers.** Through Thursday, they had put roughly ₹7,532 crore into Indian shares in August so far, and they bought on every session in the week for which a figure exists but one. **Foreign money was, in net terms, simply absent.** If you strip out a single session whose numbers we could not verify against a second source, August-to-date foreign flow works out at about minus ₹40 crore, which on the scale of this market is zero.

What we cannot say: **what either did on Friday, because nobody published it.** No foreign or domestic figures were released for the last session of the week, and one data aggregator's numbers were implausible enough that we chose not to print them. We also cannot give you a clean weekly total for either pool, because one session is unverified and another was never published at all. So please do not read "foreign selling" or "foreign buying" into this week. The honest description is that foreign flow was small and mixed, and domestic money carried the market.

**Volatility says something odd and worth noting.** India VIX, which is the market's own estimate of how much the index will swing over the next month, ended the week at 12.16, up 3.38%. It has now closed below 13 for five sessions in a row, which is a calm market by any historical measure. What makes it odd is Friday: the gauge did not move at all, minus two hundredths of a point, on a session that contained a stock up 19.99%, another down 10.82%, and fourteen names moving more than seven percent in one direction or the other. Enormous disagreement about individual companies, registering as no market-level anxiety whatsoever.

## 4. Earnings: the week the market paid for margins and ignored growth

More than 350 companies filed June-quarter results this week, somewhere between 190 and 233 of them on Friday alone. Three words are worth fixing in your head first. **Revenue** is what a company sold. **Operating profit** is what was left after the costs of running the business, before interest and tax, and **operating margin** is that profit as a percentage of revenue, so it tells you how many paise out of every hundred rupees of sales the company actually keeps. **Net profit** is what survives at the very end.

The entire week fits in the sentence our Thursday brief opened with: five Indian companies filed strong results that day and the market bought none of them. It was reading the margin line and ignoring the revenue line.

### The cleanest separator: Bajaj Finance

Bajaj Finance touched a fresh 52-week high on 31 July, on a quarter with profit up 29%. Seven days later, on Friday, it fell 5.84% to ₹1,078 and was the worst stock in both headline indices. Nothing about the earnings changed in those seven days. What changed was the list of products it might be allowed to sell.

That is the lesson underneath this whole section. A share price is a claim on a company's future. A quarterly result is only one of the things that can revise that claim, and this week it was often not the most important one.

### The best result that was sold: Garware Hi-Tech Films

Garware Hi-Tech Films filed the best quarter in its history: revenue ₹633 crore, up 28%; operating margin above 30% for the first time at 30.3%; net profit ₹133 crore, up 60%. It fell 0.96% on Thursday and a further 4.98% on Friday.

Nothing in those numbers is weak, and the explanation has nothing to do with the company. The share had already risen 41% in three months and 72% this year, and Wednesday's close was its highest price in a year. Everyone who was going to buy it on a good quarter had already bought it. When a share has moved in advance of an event, the event arriving is no longer new information.

### The most instructive: Muthoot Finance

Muthoot Finance reported profit up 43% to ₹2,825 crore and its loan book up 43% to ₹1.91 lakh crore. The share fell 7.33% on Monday, and at one point in that session it was down 14.4%.

The reason sits in one number. A lender's margin, usually called net interest margin, is the gap between the interest rate it earns on money it lends out and the rate it pays for the money it borrows. That gap is where a lender's profit is made. Muthoot's fell to 10.41%, which is 297 basis points, or 2.97 percentage points, below the previous quarter. Measured against that same previous quarter, profit was 16.9% lower. The 43% headline was measured against a weak quarter a year earlier.

So the headline described a good year and the margin described a deteriorating present. The market traded the second one.

### Base effects and one-off gains, which explain half the confusing reactions

A growth rate compares this quarter against the same quarter a year ago. If something unusual sat in that year-ago quarter, the comparison is broken and the growth number is telling you about history rather than about now. That is a base effect, and this week gave clean examples in both directions.

**Escorts Kubota** reported profit of ₹385.93 crore, which looks 72% lower than a year earlier, and the share rose 1.60%. The year-ago figure contained ₹1,027.63 crore from selling its railway equipment division and some land, which is not something any company does every quarter. On the business it still runs, revenue grew 28% to ₹3,207.55 crore and it recorded its highest ever first-quarter domestic tractor volumes, up 22.9%. The genuine negative was smaller and elsewhere: operating margin fell from 13.0% to 11.2% on higher commodity costs.

**Neuland Laboratories** is the same trick in reverse. Revenue of ₹642 crore against ₹293 crore and profit of ₹148 crore against ₹14 crore looks spectacular, and the share rose 7.95% on Thursday and 6.09% on Friday to a 52-week high. Measured against the immediately preceding March quarter, the same company shows revenue down 17% and profit down 31%. We are not going to present the year-ago figures as clean growth.

Two one-off gains are worth knowing about. **S.J.S. Enterprises** reported profit up 115% and fell 2.89%, because ₹24 crore of that rise was a one-time gain on selling an old factory; strip it out and the underlying growth was 45%. **Blue Star's** reported profit contains ₹9.2 crore of one-off property income, so its underlying decline is nearer 23% than the 15% the headline shows.

### Weak or complicated results that were bought

**Ather Energy** rose 13.96% on Tuesday to an all-time high on a quarter that still lost money. What changed was a sign, not a size: revenue up 89% to ₹1,217 crore, the loss narrowed 71%, and the first positive operating profit in the company's history, ₹9 crore against minus ₹106 crore a year earlier. To be clear, ₹9 crore on ₹1,217 crore of revenue is breaking even, not profitability.

**Urban Company** rose 12.98% on Monday, the largest riser on the board, on revenue up 44% and order volumes up 79%, with a loss of ₹92 crore that was 43% narrower than the previous quarter but worse than a small profit a year earlier.

**KEI Industries** rose 9.49% on Tuesday on profit up 40% and revenue up 23%, alongside ₹700 crore of new capacity paid for out of its own cash, with no new borrowing and no new shares issued. The honest wrinkle is that both revenue and profit were lower than the previous quarter. The market bought the annual growth and the expansion, not an acceleration.

And some reactions we cannot explain at all. CG Power rose 6.53% on Tuesday on a quarter that came in below expectations. Alivus Life Sciences rose the full 10% permitted in a day on results filed a week earlier. Great Eastern Shipping filed the most profitable quarter in its history, profit up 160% and revenue up 67% with a record interim dividend, and rose 2.61%. We went looking for reasons in each case and did not find any we would stand behind.

### The sectors where the numbers were strongest

**Power and grid equipment was the clearest tailwind on the board.** Siemens Energy India rose 12.19% on Friday on revenue up 39.3% to ₹2,485.6 crore, profit ₹440.9 crore up 67.8%, an operating margin of 21.9%, and an order backlog of ₹19,331 crore, up 16.4%. An order backlog is work already won and not yet built, so it is the closest thing to visible future revenue a company can show. Growth, margin and backlog all moving the same way is rare. KEI is the same theme from the other end: it is expanding because its stainless-steel wire lines are already running at 91% of capacity.

**Integrated metals worked and metal recycling did not.** Hindalco filed profit of ₹7,013 crore against ₹4,004 crore, up 75%, on revenue up 32.1% to ₹84,825 crore, with all four of its businesses at their highest ever quarterly operating profit, and rose 3.17%. Jain Resource Recycling grew revenue 76% and profit only 21%, and fell 7.64%, because it buys scrap and sells metal and the gap between those two prices narrowed.

**Specialty chemicals expanded margins while growing.** Navin Fluorine grew revenue 44.1% and profit 107.7% at a 34.2% operating margin, and rose 13.67% on Thursday to a lifetime high. Garware did the same. Expanding a margin while growing is precisely what the auto suppliers below could not do.

**Precision manufacturing and electronics.** Avalon Technologies lifted operating profit 93.9% with margin from 9.2% to 12.0%, and cut its working capital cycle from 142 days to 117. Shivalik Bimetal grew revenue 33.4% and profit 44.9% (Section 7 covers the other document it published the same afternoon).

**Garments.** Pearl Global filed record revenue of ₹1,528 crore, up 24.5%, at a record 10.7% operating margin, up 140 basis points, having shipped 20.8 million pieces against 17.2 million.

**Large banks held the line where mid-sized lenders did not.** State Bank of India filed standalone net profit of ₹21,121 crore, up 10.23%, with interest income up 14.88% and its domestic margin held at 3.00% against 3.01%. Holding that gap steady while the Reserve Bank's policy rate has been coming down is the difficult part. Sundaram Finance was the cleanest quarter anyone filed all week: profit up 22%, loan book up 17%, return on assets improved to 3.06% and bad loans down to 1.71% of the book. The share rose 3.50%. When growth, margin and credit quality all improve together, the market pays without arguing.

### The sectors where the numbers were weakest

**Cooling and appliances was the worst.** Blue Star fell 9.95% on the week and finished Friday at a 52-week low. Revenue grew 13.3% to ₹3,378 crore while the operating margin went from 6.7% to 5.2%, and inside the segment that is effectively the whole company, room air conditioners and commercial refrigeration, the margin halved from 5.8% to 2.9%. Management named its own problems in plain language: a short summer, old unsold stock sitting with dealers, higher metal costs, and prices too weak to pass those costs on. Two things it got no credit for: its order book rose 13.5% to ₹7,764 crore and it holds ₹900 crore of net cash. Crompton Greaves Consumer Electricals fell 7.43% on Friday after losing roughly ₹200 crore of sales to supply disruption. Two large names down that hard in one session, both citing demand timing and input costs, is a sector event rather than two coincidences.

**Auto components that buy a lot of copper and aluminium.** Motherson Sumi Wiring grew revenue 36.6% and its profit was flat, and the company itself named the reasons: higher copper prices and minimum-wage revisions. The share fell 4.17%. ASK Automotive posted record revenue up 52.4% with margin down from 13.8% to 12.0% on alloy costs. Carraro India's margin went from 11.0% to 10.4% with exports down 14%, and it fell 7.40%. Greaves Cotton was the extreme case: revenue up 31%, operating margin from 7.6% down to 5.8%, profit down 22%, share down 14.04%, the biggest move on that day's board in either direction. Against all of them, UNO Minda filed a record quarter and rose 1.40% on a day its group fell. Same industry, same month, same input costs, opposite outcomes. What separated them was the ability to pass costs on.

**Mid-sized IT was sold while the large exporters rose.** On Friday the IT index closed up 1.42% with 9.35 percentage points between its best and worst member, Tata Consultancy Services up 3.36% against Saksoft down 5.99%. The index was useless as a guide all week. Firstsource Solutions fell 13.40% on Thursday and another 4.80% on Friday: revenue grew 23.9%, but it capped its full-year guidance at 10 to 13% growth and its operating cash flow fell 72%. One correction worth carrying, because it is circulating: a syndicated wire report said Firstsource's operating profit fell 45.2% against the previous quarter. The company's own factsheet shows it rose 7.1%. The 13.40% fall is real; the reason being given for it is not.

**Consumer staples paid for imported inputs.** Emami grew revenue 15% and its net profit fell 16.4%, and it named the cause itself: raw-material costs pushed up by the West Asia conflict, which it called one of the sharpest inflationary phases the sector has faced in recent years. The share fell 3.50%.

**Hospitals in the middle of building capacity.** Narayana Health grew revenue 78% but its operating margin compressed by 511 basis points and net profit rose only 5.35%; the share fell 5.40%. Krishna Institute of Medical Sciences ended June with 7,459 beds against 5,499 a year earlier and its profit fell; the share dropped 3.01%. New hospitals carry costs long before they carry patients.

**Agrochemicals.** UPL reported a ₹73 crore quarterly loss on revenue up 10.5% and fell 6.15%. The June quarter is seasonally the weakest for the industry, so part of that is the calendar. Dhanuka Agritech reported revenue down about 13%, profit down 35% and cut its full-year guidance, and the share closed flat. We cannot explain that one.

### Timing decided who owned which reaction

Half the week's apparent puzzles dissolve once you check the clock. A price that printed at 15:30 cannot be a verdict on a document filed at 16:36. Four companies filed after Friday's close and therefore had no market reaction at all this week: **Titan**, with profit up 62.87%; **Ola Electric**, with revenue down about 45% and a wider loss; **Univastu**, with revenue up 296%; and **Hitachi Energy India**. Those are next week's story. Nykaa is the other timing casualty: it filed an excellent quarter 44 minutes after Tuesday's close, its first tradeable session was Wednesday, and Wednesday is the day we did not publish.
