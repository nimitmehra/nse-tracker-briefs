## 1. The week in plain English

The Nifty 50 finished the week at 24,570.65, up 0.77%. The whole of that gain was made in one morning. Monday rose 1.60%, and the four days after it gave back about eight tenths of a percent between them. Friday itself closed down 0.27%. So the plain summary of the week is: one very good Monday, then four days of slow drift lower that did not quite undo it.

Three things mattered, and each of them can be said in a single sentence.

**Oil got much cheaper over the weekend, and India buys most of its oil from abroad.** Brent crude, the benchmark price most of the world's oil trades against, fell 7.30% across Saturday and Sunday to $83.54 a barrel. India imports roughly 85% of the oil it burns, so a cheaper barrel means a smaller import bill, fewer rupees leaving the country, and less upward pressure on prices in the shops. That is the reason Monday happened.

**More than 350 companies reported their quarterly results, and the market paid for profit margins rather than for growth.** Margin is the share of every rupee of sales that is left over as profit after costs. Companies that grew their sales fast but kept less of each rupee were sold. Companies whose costs behaved were bought. Several of the best sets of results published all week fell on the day they came out, because the share had already risen in anticipation.

**On Thursday evening the Reserve Bank of India published a draft rule that would stop finance companies that are not banks from offering revolving credit, and on Friday the market treated the draft as though it were already law.** Revolving credit is a borrowing limit you can repay and then draw on again, the way a credit card works. Bajaj Finance, the largest of those lenders, fell 5.84%. The document is a consultation paper. Comments are open until 28 August. Nothing has been decided, and nothing has changed for any borrower yet.

One more fact belongs up here, and we cannot explain it. Small companies had a far better week than large ones: the Nifty Smallcap 250 rose 2.44%, roughly three times the Nifty 50's 0.77%. Section 9 sets out what we know about that and what we do not.

## 3. The market underneath the index: breadth and flows

**Breadth is a count, not an average.** It asks how many separate companies went up and how many went down, rather than how far the index travelled. An index can rise because one enormous company rose, or because most companies rose, and only the count tells you which of those happened. Thursday is the perfect illustration: the Nifty closed green on a day when most of the market fell, because Reliance had a good day.

**Over the full week, 427 shares rose and 259 fell, out of 686 we track.** That is 62 out of every 100 finishing the week higher. So the week's gain was reasonably broad, and it was not the work of a handful of giants. Monday is what did it. A session with 534 risers to 144 fallers puts most stocks net higher for the week, and the three published down days that followed chipped away at that without reversing it.

**Money flows are the other half of the picture, and this week our record is patchy.** Two pools of professional money dominate Indian trading. Foreign institutional investors are money arriving from outside India. Domestic institutional investors are mostly Indian mutual funds and insurance companies, which is largely your and my monthly savings arriving in the market. They often pull in opposite directions.

What we can say with confidence: **domestic institutions were the buyers.** Through Thursday, they had put roughly ₹7,532 crore into Indian shares in August so far, and they bought on every session in the week for which a figure exists but one. **Foreign money was, in net terms, simply absent.** If you strip out a single session whose numbers we could not verify against a second source, August-to-date foreign flow works out at about minus ₹40 crore, which on the scale of this market is zero.

What we cannot say: **what either did on Friday, because nobody published it.** No foreign or domestic figures were released for the last session of the week, and one data aggregator's numbers were implausible enough that we chose not to print them. We also cannot give you a clean weekly total for either pool, because one session is unverified and another was never published at all. So please do not read "foreign selling" or "foreign buying" into this week. The honest description is that foreign flow was small and mixed, and domestic money carried the market.

**One last measure.** India VIX is the market's own estimate of how much the index will swing over the next month, calculated from option prices. It ended the week at 12.16 and has closed below 13 for five sessions running, which is a calm market by any historical standard. Friday is the striking part: it did not move at all on a session containing a stock up 19.99%, another down 10.82%, and fourteen names moving more than seven percent either way. Investors disagreed violently about individual companies and not at all about the market as a whole.

## 4. Earnings: the week the market paid for margins and ignored growth

More than 350 companies filed June-quarter results this week, somewhere between 190 and 233 of them on Friday alone. Three words are worth fixing in your head first. **Revenue** is what a company sold. **Operating profit** is what was left after the costs of running the business, before interest and tax, and **operating margin** is that profit as a percentage of revenue, so it tells you how many paise out of every hundred rupees of sales the company actually keeps. **Net profit** is what survives at the very end.

The entire week fits in the sentence our Thursday brief opened with: five Indian companies filed strong results that day and the market bought none of them. It was reading the margin line and ignoring the revenue line.

### The cleanest separator: Bajaj Finance

Bajaj Finance touched a fresh 52-week high on 31 July, on a quarter with profit up 29%. Seven days later, on Friday, it fell 5.84% to ₹1,078 and was the worst stock in both headline indices. Nothing about the earnings changed in those seven days. What changed was the list of products it might be allowed to sell.

That is the lesson underneath this whole section. A share price is a claim on a company's future. A quarterly result is only one of the things that can revise that claim, and this week it was often not the most important one.

### The best result that was sold: Garware Hi-Tech Films

Garware Hi-Tech Films filed the best quarter in its history: revenue ₹633 crore, up 28%; operating margin above 30% for the first time at 30.3%; net profit ₹133 crore, up 60%. It fell 0.96% on Thursday and a further 4.98% on Friday.

Nothing in those numbers is weak, and the explanation has nothing to do with the company. The share had already risen 41% in three months and 72% this year, and Wednesday's close was its highest price in a year. Everyone who was going to buy it on a good quarter had already bought it. When a share has moved in advance of an event, the event arriving is no longer new information.

### The most instructive: Muthoot Finance

Muthoot Finance reported profit up 43% to ₹2,825 crore and its loan book up 43% to ₹1.91 lakh crore. The share fell 7.33% on Monday, and at one point in that session it was down 14.4%.

The reason sits in one number. A lender's margin, usually called net interest margin, is the gap between the interest rate it earns on money it lends out and the rate it pays for the money it borrows. That gap is where a lender's profit is made. Muthoot's fell to 10.41%, which is 297 basis points, or 2.97 percentage points, below the previous quarter. Measured against that same previous quarter, profit was 16.9% lower. The 43% headline was measured against a weak quarter a year earlier.

So the headline described a good year and the margin described a deteriorating present. The market traded the second one.

### Base effects and one-off gains, which explain half the confusing reactions

A growth rate compares this quarter against the same quarter a year ago. If something unusual sat in that year-ago quarter, the comparison is broken and the growth number is telling you about history rather than about now. That is a base effect, and this week gave clean examples in both directions.

**Escorts Kubota** reported profit of ₹385.93 crore, which looks 72% lower than a year earlier, and the share rose 1.60%. The year-ago figure contained ₹1,027.63 crore from selling its railway equipment division and some land, which is not something any company does every quarter. On the business it still runs, revenue grew 28% to ₹3,207.55 crore and it recorded its highest ever first-quarter domestic tractor volumes, up 22.9%. The genuine negative was smaller and elsewhere: operating margin fell from 13.0% to 11.2% on higher commodity costs.

**Neuland Laboratories** is the same trick in reverse. Revenue of ₹642 crore against ₹293 crore and profit of ₹148 crore against ₹14 crore looks spectacular, and the share rose 7.95% on Thursday and 6.09% on Friday to a 52-week high. Measured against the immediately preceding March quarter, the same company shows revenue down 17% and profit down 31%. We are not going to present the year-ago figures as clean growth.

Two one-off gains are worth knowing about. **S.J.S. Enterprises** reported profit up 115% and fell 2.89%, because ₹24 crore of that rise was a one-time gain on selling an old factory; strip it out and the underlying growth was 45%. **Blue Star's** reported profit contains ₹9.2 crore of one-off property income, so its underlying decline is nearer 23% than the 15% the headline shows.

### Weak or complicated results that were bought

**Ather Energy** rose 13.96% on Tuesday to an all-time high on a quarter that still lost money. What changed was a sign, not a size: revenue up 89% to ₹1,217 crore, the loss narrowed 71%, and the first positive operating profit in the company's history, ₹9 crore against minus ₹106 crore a year earlier. To be clear, ₹9 crore on ₹1,217 crore of revenue is breaking even, not profitability.

**Urban Company** rose 12.98% on Monday, the largest riser on the board, on revenue up 44% and order volumes up 79%, with a loss of ₹92 crore that was 43% narrower than the previous quarter. **KEI Industries** rose 9.49% on Tuesday on profit up 40% and revenue up 23%, alongside ₹700 crore of new capacity paid for out of its own cash, with no new borrowing and no new shares issued. The honest wrinkle there is that both revenue and profit were lower than the previous quarter: the market bought the annual growth and the expansion, not an acceleration.

And some reactions we cannot explain at all. CG Power rose 6.53% on Tuesday on a quarter that came in below expectations. Alivus Life Sciences rose the full 10% permitted in a day on results filed a week earlier. Great Eastern Shipping filed the most profitable quarter in its history, profit up 160% and revenue up 67% with a record interim dividend, and rose 2.61%. We went looking for reasons in each case and did not find any we would stand behind.

### The sectors where the numbers were strongest

**Power and grid equipment was the clearest tailwind on the board.** Siemens Energy India rose 12.19% on Friday on revenue up 39.3%, profit up 67.8%, an operating margin of 21.9%, and an order backlog of ₹19,331 crore, up 16.4%. An order backlog is work already won and not yet built, so it is the closest thing to visible future revenue a company can show, and growth, margin and backlog moving together is rare. KEI is the same theme from the other end: it is expanding because its stainless-steel wire lines already run at 91% of capacity.

Jain Resource Recycling grew revenue 76% and profit only 21%, and fell 7.64%, because it buys scrap and sells metal and the gap between those two prices narrowed.

**Specialty chemicals grew and widened their margins at the same time.** Navin Fluorine grew revenue 44.1% and profit 107.7% at a 34.2% margin, and rose 13.67% on Thursday to a lifetime high. Garware did the same. Widening a margin while growing is exactly what the auto suppliers below could not do.

**Precision manufacturing and garments.** Avalon Technologies lifted operating profit 93.9%, with margin from 9.2% to 12.0%, and cut its working capital cycle from 142 days to 117. Pearl Global filed record revenue of ₹1,528 crore, up 24.5%, at a record 10.7% operating margin, having shipped 20.8 million pieces against 17.2 million.

**Large banks held the line where mid-sized lenders did not.** State Bank of India filed standalone net profit of ₹21,121 crore, up 10.23%, with interest income up 14.88% and its domestic margin held at 3.00% against 3.01%. Holding that gap steady while the policy rate is coming down is the difficult part. Sundaram Finance filed the cleanest quarter of the week: profit up 22%, loan book up 17%, return on assets 3.06%, bad loans down to 1.71%, and the share rose 3.50%. **Auto, the week's best sector, and a lesson in where good news lands.** On Saturday 1 August the industry published July's volumes: passenger-vehicle wholesales above 4.7 lakh units, up 33%, the best month on record, with Maruti Suzuki's own domestic number its highest ever, up 42.41%. A wholesale is a dispatch from the factory to the dealer. It is not a sale to a customer. The maker books it when the vehicle leaves the plant, whether or not anyone has walked into a showroom, so a record wholesale month tells you what the industry produced and pushed into its dealer network, and the retail confirmation is a separate number published later. Both Maruti and Mahindra were also flat to lower against June, so record does not mean accelerating.

The interesting part is who got paid. On Monday the parts suppliers rose 3.83% as a group across 17 names with not a single faller, against 2.26% for the car makers, and **Maruti itself closed down 0.59% on its own record month.** The reason is simple: the makers announce their own monthly volumes, so the number was already in their share prices. How much profit a supplier earns on a 33% industry month was not. Cheaper oil helped the same sector twice over, by lowering the running cost of a car for the buyer and by lowering the cost of the oil-derived inputs, mainly resin, rubber and freight, that the parts makers buy.

**Realty, the worst sector, and the number the market actually read.** When a developer sells a flat, the money and the profit do not show up in that quarter's accounts. Revenue is recorded only as construction crosses agreed milestones, sometimes years later. So bookings, also called pre-sales, are the value of homes actually sold in the quarter and are a picture of the future, while reported profit is the delivery of sales made years ago and is a picture of the past.

DLF makes the point exactly. Its profit rose 4.1% to ₹794 crore and its bookings collapsed to ₹657 crore from ₹11,425 crore a year earlier, about 94% lower, on launches that were pushed back. Management still expects to reach its ₹20,000 crore pre-sales target for the year. The share fell 3.76%. The market traded the bookings line and ignored the profit line, and that is reading the right line. Godrej Properties ran the identical split in reverse: profit down 41.6% to ₹349 crore with the operating margin down from 58.1% to 41.7%, while bookings rose 22% to ₹8,651 crore. The rest of the group followed, with Ajmera down 5.71%, Prestige down 4.49% and Oberoi down 4.42% on Tuesday.

One discipline point, because the obvious wrong answer was available all week. It was tempting to read developers falling in a week containing a rate decision as fear about interest rates. Three of the five worst property names had filed weak results inside the same window. We are not going to credit the rate-setting committee with a fall that already has an earnings explanation.

**Energy fell while oil fell, which confuses people, so here it is in one sentence.** Cheaper oil is good for India and bad for the companies that sell oil, and the Energy index measures the second thing. Oil and Natural Gas Corporation is the clean case: it pumps crude rather than buying it, so a cheaper barrel cuts what it earns. The genuine oddity is that the refiners and fuel marketers, who buy crude and should have welcomed the fall, did not trade it either: on Friday Bharat Petroleum fell 0.83%, Indian Oil rose 0.50% and Reliance rose 0.74%. Two sessions running in which neither side of India's oil chain priced a large move in crude, and we cannot tell you why.

## 6. Three things worth understanding properly

### The closing auction: the number on your screen is now an auction result

From Monday 3 August, the way India's official closing prices are produced changed for every stock that has futures and options listed on it, which is the large and liquid end of the market. Before Monday, the closing price was the average of a stock's trades over the last thirty minutes of the day, a summary of ordinary trading. Since Monday, continuous trading in those stocks stops at 15:15 and the closing price is decided in a single auction that runs until 15:35.

**What a closing auction is.** In ordinary trading, buyers and sellers are matched continuously, one order against another, and a price exists at every second. In an auction, matching stops. For a fixed window, everyone who wants to trade at the close puts their order into a common pool and nothing executes while the window is open. At the end, the exchange's computer finds the single price at which the largest number of shares can change hands, fills every order that can be filled at that price, all at once, and that price becomes the official close. Orders can only be placed within a 3% band around a reference price.

This matters because that number is not decorative. It is the price your mutual fund is valued at each night, the level the index prints at, the basis of every percentage change you read, and the price at which derivative contracts settle.

**The part people noticed.** On Monday the Nifty rose 1.60% and the Sensex rose 0.70%, a gap of about 90 basis points, or nine tenths of a percentage point, which is roughly the widest since 2010. On Tuesday the gap narrowed to about 37 basis points and flipped sign, with the Nifty the weaker of the two.

How can two indices covering the same market disagree like that? The Nifty 50 is the National Stock Exchange's headline index and the Sensex is the Bombay Stock Exchange's, and they hold largely the same big companies. Each exchange now runs its own closing auction. So a company listed on both gets two closing prices on the same evening, one from each auction, and the Nifty is built from the NSE's while the Sensex is built from the BSE's. The two auctions are nothing like the same size: on 4 August, auction turnover was reported at about ₹1,542.65 crore on the NSE against ₹9.36 crore on the BSE, roughly 165 to one. That figure comes from a single newspaper report whose page we could not open, so we attribute it rather than assert it. A thin auction has few orders to absorb an imbalance, so each rupee of buying or selling moves the price further than the same rupee would in a deep one. The same company can therefore finish the day at two different prices, and once the heavyweights close at different levels, two indices holding the same companies report different days. **That gap is a side effect of how the closing price is now recorded. It tells you nothing about how many shares rose, or about the market's appetite for risk.**

Two honest updates. If Monday's Nifty close was pushed above fair value, Tuesday started from an inflated base, which makes Tuesday's Sensex fall the cleaner read of that day. And by Friday we had downgraded this explanation rather than closed it: a gap between the two indices can also arise simply because they weight sectors differently, and Friday's gap fits that without the auction being involved at all.

The framework comes from a SEBI circular dated 16 January 2026, effective 3 August, and the exchanges ran a joint mock session on Saturday 1 August. Since January, SEBI has published nothing further. That is neither a finding of fault nor a reassurance, and we are not going to dress it up as either.

### Oil's round trip

Brent fell about 9.4% across the week. The fall was steepest early, and later in the week the price stopped falling and partly retraced. We are not giving you a figure for Friday alone, because our own data and an independent read disagreed on the size of that day's move while agreeing on its direction. The weekly number is the one we stand behind.

**Why it fell early.** Two things. OPEC+ met on Sunday 2 August and agreed a September output increase completing the return of the 2.2 million barrels a day it had been holding back, and more supply kept arriving all week: a further 188,000 barrels a day from the OPEC+ core, Turkey and Iraq extending their pipeline agreement by a year, and Kazakhstan resuming Caspian flows. The second reason is the contested one. Trump called off a planned strike on Iran, and Washington said a Strait of Hormuz deal was done and the strait would be permanently open. Iran's foreign ministry said there are no negotiations with the United States, and that its talks are with Oman, about a route Iran itself calls temporary. So Monday's crude collapse, and therefore Monday's rally in Indian shares, was priced off the claimed half of that story rather than the confirmed half.

**Why the strait matters, in facts rather than adjectives.** It has been largely closed to commercial shipping since 28 February, and passage requires Iranian permission. On 23 July there were 10 transits against a normal of about 88 a day, and war-risk insurance was running at eight times normal. A cargo ship was struck 37 km north-east of Al Khasab in the early hours of 4 August, the third vessel hit in four days. Oil does not get cheaper because two governments are friendlier. It gets cheaper because barrels can physically move again, ships stop paying punitive insurance, and supply that exists on paper starts to arrive.

**Two separate Gulf documents, which must not be merged.** We merged them ourselves once and corrected it, so this is worth stating carefully. The first is the Iran and Oman transit arrangement, agreed in principle: agreed geographical coordinates for a safe shipping route, a proposed joint coordination centre for maritime traffic, inbound traffic routed through a northern corridor near the Iranian coast and outbound through a southern corridor along the Omani coast, and **transit fees structured around services rendered and explicitly not as a share of cargo value.** The second is a separate Iranian parliamentary committee measure, at an earlier stage, which would bar United States and Israeli vessels, and that is where tolls set as a percentage of cargo value belong. Different body, different document, different stage. Three outlets carried the same conflation, which is the lesson in one line: corroborating across news outlets confirms the reporting, not the underlying document. American acceptance is unresolved, and Trump has balked at any deal giving Iran more control over the strait.

### The Reserve Bank's draft rule, and the rate decision

**Say the first thing first: this is a draft, open for public comment until 28 August. It is not law and nothing has been notified.**

The draft, published on Thursday evening, would allow a non-bank lender to offer only term loans and no revolving credit at all. Revolving credit is a limit you can use, repay and then use again. A credit card is the everyday example: a ₹2 lakh limit, you spend ₹50,000, you repay it, and that ₹50,000 is available to you again without asking anyone. A business overdraft works the same way, and the defining feature is that repaying restores the limit. A term loan is a fixed amount borrowed once on a fixed schedule: you borrow ₹8 lakh for a car, you repay it over five years, and repaying ₹3 lakh does not give you ₹3 lakh back to spend. The loan only shrinks. The draft would write that into the rules formally: fixed principal, a pre-set repayment schedule, and a limit that is not topped back up once principal is repaid.

The only carve-out is for lenders the Reserve Bank has authorised to issue credit cards, reportedly two companies, so an exemption that reads wide is narrow in practice. The draft also says the rules would take effect immediately on notification, with no transition period written into the text. Three things are unsettled and none of them is priced into anything: whether the ban survives at all, whether the card exemption widens, and whether a transition period gets added. **That is exactly what a comment window is for, and it closes on 28 August.**

The week makes the point sharper. Before the draft landed, non-bank lenders were the week's best group, rising on the Reserve Bank's own inflation revision: as at Thursday, Shriram Finance was up 11.08% on the week, Cholamandalam up 9.37% and Bajaj Finance up 8.67%. The same regulator that helped them on Wednesday hit them on Thursday evening through a completely different document.

**Wednesday's rate decision** belongs here because we did not publish that day. The Reserve Bank held the repo rate at 5.25%, unanimously, a fourth consecutive hold. The repo rate is what the Reserve Bank charges commercial banks to borrow from it, so it sets the floor under what everybody else pays. The news was in the forecasts. It cut its inflation projection for the year to 5.0% from 5.1%, crediting cheaper crude, and cut its **core** inflation forecast by 40 basis points to 4.3%, four times the size of the headline cut. Core inflation strips out food and fuel, the two most volatile items, so cutting core that much harder is the Bank saying it reads today's price pressure as coming from supply disruption rather than spreading through the wider economy. It also raised its growth forecast to 6.7% from 6.6%. Lowering an inflation forecast and raising a growth forecast in one statement describes a better set of conditions, and the Bank declined to spend a rate cut on saying so. One tension it did not address: July's services survey landed the same morning at 53.3, a 53-month low, in the part of the economy that produces roughly 55% of India's output.
